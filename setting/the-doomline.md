# The Doomline — Purewater in five acts

**This is not the plot. It is the default**, and every beat in it is an *attempt*
that gets rolled. It exists so the living world is cheap to run: author what
happens if nobody interferes, once, and then play only has to record what
changes.

What it is *also* for, after two runs that ballooned, is **shape**. The last
telling reached the end of Act II with fourteen allies and no losses, because
every scene had the same move in it — walk in, tell the truth, gain a friend.
The acts below exist so that there is somewhere for the reversal and the crisis
to live.

---

## The rule that fixes the last run

> **Every act must take something.**

Not a setback. A *loss* — a person, a place, a standing, a route — that does not
come back. An act where the party only gains is an act that has not happened.
If the dice will not produce it, the Baron's playbook below will.

And its companion:

> **Nobody helps for free, and most people say no the first time.**

An ally is a scene, not a conversation. Every one of them wants something, is
frightened of something, or is already committed elsewhere — and the default
answer to a stranger asking for help in an occupied city is **no**.

---

# ACT I — THE COLUMN (d-3)

*The city, as it is, and then a girl dies in it.*

The di Teufel column comes up the Anminster road with a covered litter in the
middle that the outriders let nobody near. Three Dragon Knights hold the gate
bridge and take a toll they have no right to, and the watch stands twenty feet
away and looks at the water.

That evening Santo offers to buy Emmeralda's contract and Constantine refuses
him. In the night he attempts his father's working, **fails it**, and uses the
knife. Blau collects him with a folded cloth and never goes up the stair.

**Act I takes:** Emmeralda. And the party's assumption that a city has laws.

**Ends on:** the wrong man arrested for it.

---

# ACT II — THE CITY CLOSES (d-2 to d-1)

*They find out who did it, and that it does not matter.*

The look-alikes are taken. The catacombs. Marisette lets them go. The Pearl goes
dark — four hundred people out of work on one woman's word, which is the only
weapon her trade has.

They learn Santo did it. They learn there is nothing whatever to be done about
a baron's son, and that the watch has been told to go easy, and that the
magistrates will not hear it.

**The presentation of the entrants.** The helm comes off and the champion is a
thin boy with dragon-scale growing out of his throat.

**MIDPOINT.** Gardwen knows her brother is alive and in that man's hold, and can
do nothing at all — and the Baron's household becomes aware of *her*.

**Act II takes:** the belief that evidence is worth anything. And the Pearl's
livelihood, which is now a wasting asset rather than a threat.

---

# ACT III — THE PLAN, AND THE COUNTER (d0 to d1)

*They build something. He breaks part of it.*

This is where the last run went wrong: the party built and nobody pushed back.
**The Baron's playbook (below) runs from here on, and it runs on triggers, not
on a timetable.**

The shape of the act: every step of the plan requires somebody who does not want
to help, and the first real success is answered within one watch.

**Act III takes:** a route, and a person's freedom. The city stops being a place
they can move around in.

**Ends on:** the counter landing — an arrest, a raid, or a death — and the plan
in pieces.

---

# ACT IV — THE UNFORGIVING CITY (d1 night to d2 dawn)

*The worst night. Everything they built is used against them.*

The Knights come off the leash. The purge. The party is scattered, hunted, or
held, and the allies are all somewhere else for reasons already established —
Nerissa in the shallows, Temerach behind a shut door, Constantine on a picketed
island, Randall wearing a face he cannot take off.

**Act IV takes:** somebody they recruited. By name, and because they asked.

**Ends at the lowest point:** dawn on the day of the sand, with the thing they
needed most either lost or in the wrong hands.

---

# ACT V — THE SAND, THE WATER, AND THE ROOM (d2)

*Everything at once, in one place, in front of the whole city.*

The lists. Four thousand people. The champion walked out in harness for the only
time he ever leaves that ship. The Order in the shallows at the foot of the
steps. The Knights loose in the streets behind. A shut room with an elf in it.

**The payoff is not a fight.** It is a **burial**, said out loud, in public, at
the Lady's own festival — the inconvenient thing the Order has spent two years
refusing to say, spoken over a boy in the water by a woman with no house and no
standing, while a baron's house comes apart around her.

And one line, in a seventeen-year-old's own voice, left-handed.

# THE BARON'S PLAYBOOK

He is not a schedule. He is a man with more resources than the party and a
strong preference for never touching anything himself. **These are triggered,
not timed.** When the party does the thing on the left, he does the thing on the
right, within a watch.

| They do this | He does this |
|---|---|
| Ask questions in public about his money | A price on the face. Men out by dawn — to *name* first, take later. |
| Reach his son | Blau moves Santo, and the man who carried the message stops being findable. |
| Get evidence in front of an official | **He accuses back.** He is a guest of the Crown's representative; they are a foreign unlicensed sorcerer, a thief with a murderer's face, and an Assay officer acting outside his warrant. The watch will help him, *lawfully*. |
| Put a hand on the champion | The reinforcement goes deeper that night, and the boy is moved. |
| Take something off him | The Knights come off the standing order. Reprisals, publicly, on people who cannot answer. |
| Threaten the timetable | He offers a *deal* — warm, generous, entirely sincere in the moment — to the one person in the party most tired of losing. |
| Corner him | The warmth goes **up**. More attention, more hospitality, more delight. He has never once raised his voice and he is not going to start. |

**His three real assets, in order:** the law (he is lawful and they are not), the
Prince's affection, and twenty-five armed men the watch has been told to go easy
on. His money is not his and that is the thread that unravels him.

## The Dragon Knights — an escalation ladder

They get worse each act, and the watch does nothing each time, which is the
point. **Do not skip rungs.**

1. **Tolls and insults.** Taking what they want from stalls. A woman's basket tipped.
2. **A beating in daylight**, in front of people, over nothing, and the watch walks away.
3. **A death nobody answers for.** A dredger, a boy, somebody's son off a boat.
4. **Reprisal.** After a loss, they burn or break something belonging to people who
   had nothing to do with it.
5. **Open war on the streets**, in Act IV, with the city fighting back badly and
   losing.

Mechanically they should be **genuinely dangerous** — mail, shields, trained
Combat Styles, and they fight in threes. A PC who takes one on alone should lose.

# THE SET PIECES

The ones worth building a session around, and what makes each one fun to *play*
rather than to hear about.

**The gate toll.** Small, early, and the best scene of the first run. Works
because the PC is exposed (no money) and something is genuinely at risk in front
of witnesses.

**The riot at the water-stairs.** A crowd, a flashpoint, and Marda trying to hold
three hundred people while somebody in mail decides how bored he is. Mass
violence the party can shape but not control.

**The house defence.** They come for Constantine's, at night, with girls and a
thirteen-year-old inside. A fight with something to protect and nowhere to fall
back to.

**The lawful arrest.** The worst one, and the best. No violence available: the
watch has a warrant, it is correctly issued, and fighting turns them into what
the Baron has already said they are.

**The barge.** Invitation rather than stealth. Twenty people aboard, everyone
knows every face, and the only ways in are the ones somebody grants you.

**The catacombs.** Hunted underground through Crowbill's tunnels, in the dark,
by men who do not know them.

**The Washing.** Forty seconds, hands near her brother, inside a rope no armed
man may cross, with his father eleven feet away.

**The lift at the rail.** A theft under four thousand pairs of eyes, in the one
hour a man like that is reachable.

**The sand.** Watching a thousand-year-old knight fight in your brother's body
and being unable to want either of them to win.

**The room.** Three people and a door and no swords that matter.

**The bones.** Four days' ride, a border tower, and the only permanent answer —
the sequel, or the epilogue, or the thing they choose to give up.

---

# THE ENDING

Epic here does not mean large. It means **everything in one place at once**, and
the small thing at the centre of it mattering more than the big things around it.

At the climax the city should be doing all of this simultaneously: the crowd on
the lists, the Order in the shallows, the Knights fighting in the streets behind,
the dredgers on the stairs, a shut room with a prince in it — and, in the middle,
a woman standing in water saying words over a boy.

**The payoff is the rite.** Not the Baron's fall; that is the consequence. The
thing the story has been about since the first night is that somebody was buried
wrong a thousand years ago and nobody said the words, and a child was taken ten
years ago and nobody came. Both are answered by the same sentence, said out loud,
by somebody with no standing whatsoever, in front of four thousand people.

**Then Bird speaks.** One line. Left-handed.

That is the whole payoff and everything else is scaffolding for it.
