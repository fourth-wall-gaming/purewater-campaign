# How the Week Works

Purewater does not wait for the characters. Everybody in the previous part of this book wants something this week and is working at it, and the following chapters tell you what happens when they get it, day by day, if nobody interferes. The characters are the only thing in the city whose next move is not already written down. Your job is to keep everybody else's moves coming, and to let the characters' moves change them.

## The Clock

The week runs for six days. Three are before the Tourney: the day the Baron arrives, the day the city closes, and the eve with its presentation of the entrants. Then come the three days of the Tourney itself: the Archery, the Single Combat, and the Run with the closing at dusk. Each day has four **watches**: dawn, day, dusk and night. This book writes a moment as the day and the watch: *d-3/night* is the night three days before the Tourney, *d0/day* is the Archery, and *d2/dusk* is the closing.

Move the clock between scenes, a watch at a time, when the characters have spent one. A long conversation, a crossing of the city or a careful search uses a watch. A fight does not. Then look at what was due in that watch and ask, for each thing, whether a character is there.

## Onscreen and Offscreen

If a character is there, it is a scene: play it. If nobody is, it **happens anyway**, exactly as written, and the characters discover it afterwards as a rumour on a stair, a body, a shut door, or a girl who was already bleeding when they got there. The same event is a fight, an alibi or a piece of news depending only on where the players chose to go. That is the whole design, and it is why the players' choices feel heavy: going to the Lullwater tonight means not being on the Pearl.

**A time in this book is the latest something happens, not an appointment.** Events can come sooner when the characters earn it. If they work out who did it before the cellar, the cellar scene changes; if they work out the twins before Temerach tells them, Temerach's scene changes. Nothing waits forever, and nothing in this book should be withheld from players who have already worked it out. A Non-Player Character sitting on a fact the table already has is the worst thing in a scenario like this.

## The Threads

Seven threads run through the week, and every scene in the following chapters belongs to one or more of them. Keep them in your head as people, not plots, and you can run the city from memory.

**Santo's appetite.** One girl, one stolen scroll, and a boy who has never been told no. It starts the scenario on the first night and runs until he gets her, is stopped, or loses his temper in public, which makes him known to the whole city. Fear works on him; exposure works on him.

**Marisette's hunt.** A woman was wronged under the Pearl's roofs, and the Pearl wants a name, then a reckoning. It sweeps up the twins on the second morning and, by the end of the second day, has shut every door on the island to the Baron's men.

**Sinnit's grudge.** Seven years of nursing one humiliation, and one week in which the thing he hates is worth money. Each step makes Randall's life worse and the Baron's information better, until one apology turns it.

**The occupation and the docks.** Every unanswered incident in the streets teaches the city a little more about what it is now, and moves the Dredgers a little closer to stopping the water-stairs. It ends when somebody with standing answers it, or when the Baron burns a quarter of the city.

**The Baron's road to the room.** The champion must be seen for four seconds at the presentation, must win on the sand, and must walk through a door at the closing. Every part of the Baron's week serves those three moments.

**Temerach's game.** Four years of patience, a network nobody has seen whole, a secret about Conall, and an Archery entry that buys her a quarter of an hour in front of the Prince. She acts when she knows what is under the helm, or when Conall is in danger.

**Gardwen's brother.** Ten years of following a colour, four seconds of a face, a night aboard the Baron's ship, and a question at the end that nobody should answer for her.

## Keeping Track

You do not need the full machinery to run this, but a little bookkeeping helps. Keep one sheet for the week, with the six days down the side and the watches across, and mark the current watch. *The Week at a Glance* has every event in order: read down it each time the mark moves. Beside it, keep a small tally for the threads that can tip over on their own:

- **The occupation:** add a mark for each public incident the Knights get away with. The city gets worse with every mark: a man beaten in front of the watch, a woman taken off the Pearl street, a local killed in the open.
- **The Dredgers:** if nobody has helped Marda by dawn on the eve of the Tourney, the water-stairs stop.
- **Sinnit:** a mark for each step he takes toward selling Randall out. At three, he sells the faces to Blau, unless Randall has apologised.
- **The locket:** where it is at dawn on the last day decides which ending the day takes.

The same appendix lists every agenda and its clock, for Games Masters who want the full machinery.

## The Shape of It

The week falls into five movements, and each one takes something from the characters that does not come back.

**The Column** (*the first day*) brings the four strangers in and puts them all into the same night. It takes Emmeralda, cut open or carried off with something inside her, and the assumption that the law applies to everyone.

**The Cellar and the Name** (*the second day*) arrests the face twice, makes the four into a party, gives the face a name and closes the Pearl. It takes the twins' anonymity, and the belief that knowing who did it is worth anything by itself.

**The Eve** (*the third day*) stops the docks, if nobody listened, sits the characters in front of the wall of the city's law, and takes the champion's helm off for four seconds. It takes Gardwen's ignorance.

**Arrows and the Ship** (*the first day of the Tourney*) gives the characters the truth: what the champion is, what holds him, and where that thing lies when the Baron sleeps. It takes the hope that her brother can simply be taken home.

**Sand, Theft and the Last Day** (*the last two days*) is the champion's victory, the theft, and the reckoning. It takes the characters' safety, the city's peace, and on the worst road, a friend.

The chapters that follow tell each movement as a story, with what happens if nobody interferes, how to run it when the characters are in the room, and where it bends when they push.

## A Note on Pacing

At most tables a day of the scenario fills a session, and the first two days are the slowest, because four characters are meeting a city. Do not hurry them together. The cellar on the second morning will come; let the first day be four short stories that the players hear one another tell. From the eve onward, the week accelerates, and the last two days should feel like a fuse burning down. If you are short of time, the Archery morning and the eve's councillor can be folded into a single session, and the Run can be played as a montage. Never cut the cellar, Gardwen's night aboard the ship, or the theft.
