# Why Nobody Can Arrest a Baron

At some point in the first two days, a player will say, reasonably, "We have the scroll in his handwriting. We take it to the watch." This chapter is what happens next, and the middle of the scenario is built on it.

## Justice Is a Person

Nobody in this story is going to arrest Baron Hanzo di Teufel. There is no prosecutor, no impartial bench that binds a lord, and no body of officers who may lay hands on one. Justice in Thornmere flows from **King Ostmar** at Caldreth and from nowhere else. In Purewater it flows through his representative, **Prince Emeric**, who may act in the King's name today, in this city, on his own word.

Everything else is administration: the watch, the guilds, the port and the Assay. Administration can record, refuse, impound, close a workshop and turn a man away from a berth. It cannot touch a baron.

## Standing

Not just anybody may accuse a lord. A dockhand's word against a baron is no case at all. To call a lord to answer, a person must have **standing**, and these people have it:

- another lord, or the Crown's own representative;
- a knight of a recognised order, sworn and in good standing, which is what Temerach Nebulo is;
- a Crown officer under warrant, within their charter, as Conall is as an assayer of the King's Assay;
- a priest of a recognised faith, within hers: the Lady's clergy may speak to what is done to the water;
- a guild master, within his guild and no further.

The characters will have proof by the second afternoon. Their real problem is **finding somebody with standing who is willing to spend themselves**, and giving them enough that they will.

## Accusation Is a Wager

Whoever accuses a great lord and fails answers for it themselves, with ruin, exile or the rope. Abel Weir knows that, which is why a sergeant keeps a book instead of acting. Alderic Vantt knows it, which is why a bought man stays bought. Maro Quist knows it, and he would make the accusation anyway if he were shown enough, and he would be destroyed if he were wrong.

Nobody makes that wager on a rumour, and that is what the documents in this scenario are for. The referral book, the spent scroll, the Tide Master's original notes and the Assay's written finding convict nobody on their own. Each is something the characters put in front of a person with standing so that they will risk their name.

There is a sting in it. A lord who is called to answer may refuse the matter and offer **trial by arms**, by his own or by his champion's. The Baron has a champion nobody has ever seen fight. That is why Temerach has carried her right to accuse him for four years and never used it. She is too good a soldier to open with a move when she cannot predict the answer.

## Three Courts the Standing Order Cannot Reach

The standing order shields the Baron's men from the city's own law and touches nothing else. Three jurisdictions lie beyond it, and the middle of the week is about finding the one that fits.

**The King's Assay.** Seating a spirit in an unwilling person is a hanging matter under the Assay's charter, and nine lines of binding in an unregistered hand are an offence before anybody asks whose hand it is. An assayer under warrant may lay a written finding before the Crown with his own name and punch on it. The Assay's doctrine is *establish, record, refer*. It investigates and writes things down, and it does not act. Its Table sits at Caldreth, days away, so its answer arrives after everything has happened. Conall carries the warrant, and more than once he will have to decide whether he is an investigator or a man with a brother.

**Maritime law.** The Harbor Masters write down every hull on Purewater's water, including the Baron's ship, and commandeering a working boat with its boatman still in it is a matter for the port and not for the council. Harbormaster Velen Deepkeel can recite the rule being broken all the way to the end. Nobody has ever asked him for the page.

**The Order's sacred ground.** The water belongs to the goddess, and so do the Tournament Ground's rites. Temple law binds the clergy until a sacred site is threatened, and a demon-marked blade laid on the altar in front of witnesses is a threat nobody can argue with. A Sword of the Lake may act on desecration without asking anybody. The moment Temerach knows that the champion is a bound spirit in a stolen boy, she stops weighing the risk, because that is the goddess's own business.

## A Party-Shaped Hole

The Order knows the water is fouling and cannot act. The Swords can act and cannot declare anything about the water. Each could solve the other's problem in an afternoon, and neither will ask, because the forms give them no way to ask without one house seeming to claim precedence over the other.

Somebody who belongs to neither house can carry the words between them, and it costs nothing but the walk. It is one of the cheapest and largest wins in the scenario, and nobody sees it until a player looks at Nerissa and Temerach and asks why they are not already working together. Reward that question generously.
