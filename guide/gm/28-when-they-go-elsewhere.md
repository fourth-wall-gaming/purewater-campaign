# When the Players Do Something Else

They will. This chapter covers the common ways a table leaves the road, and how the city answers. The same rule runs under all of it: **the world keeps going, everybody wants what they wanted, and the characters' choices change who is standing where when it happens.** You never need to steer them back. You need to know what the people in this book do about whatever the characters have just done.

## "We go straight to the Baron."

Let them. He receives them on the after deck of his ship, or at dinner at the Vantt Estate, or in the Governor's enclosure, and he is delightful. He offers chairs, wine and a sincere interest in their work. Nobody has ever accused him of anything, and he treats an accusation as a failure of hospitality, which he corrects by becoming more generous. If they show him evidence, he is shocked and grateful, and his son will be dealt with. Santo is sent home under guard the next morning, and as far as the city is concerned that is the end of the matter. Nobody searches a man they have just had a pleasant hour with.

What the characters have really done is let him see their faces. If two of them wear the same face, he now knows he has more sons, and *A di Teufel who is not his* wakes at once. This is the most dangerous thing a party can do in the first two days. Allow it, and make it cost them.

## "We put on Santo's face."

Either twin can. They are identical to each other and to Santo, and **there is no tell**: no mark, no scar, nothing a guard could check. Every risk is in how the twin behaves.

**The face buys a great deal:**

- a place aboard the Baron's ship by day, with no invitation needed;
- the run of Santo's forward cabins, with his tracings from the locked case and the plain sign that a scroll is missing from it;
- a seat at the Vantt dinner on the eve of the Tourney;
- and, on the night of the locket, a man already aboard who can open a stern window for Nus and Randall.

**The face does not get a twin** into the after part of the ship at night, past the Dragon Circle; through the champion's door, where Orrin Sculle sleeps; or past Blau without hard work.

**Only one Santo can be aboard at a time**, so the real one must be somewhere known: drunk on the Pearl, held, or safely sent home. The most dangerous audience is the Baron himself. A twin who stands up straight under his father's contempt is wrong in a way the Baron notices, and the Baron rolls Willpower to see it.

**Blau is the hardest to fool.** Run it as Deceit against his Insight (60), opposed. It is Hard if the twin is unprepared, and Standard if the twin has Santo's clothes and rings and has learned his habits from somebody who knows him: Marisette, Emmeralda, the women of the Pearl, or Santo himself, if the characters are holding him. A failed roll makes Blau suspicious, and not yet certain. He tests the twin with a question only Santo could answer, or a hand on the collar to haul the young master home. Failing the test means exposure. Every new scene with Blau is a new roll.

**How it can end for Santo:**

- If the borrowed face exposes him to his father through the missing scroll, he is written off, a son his father has stopped forgiving.
- If he dies, Blau turns to vengeance, and the twins are the only di Teufel blood walking.
- If a twin is unmasked as a stranger in front of the Baron, *A di Teufel who is not his* wakes at once. The Baron sees a better son than Santo standing in front of him. He wants that son as a tool first, and the claim extinguished second.

## "We tell the Prince."

The Prince is the best person in the city to tell, and the hardest to reach. He is surrounded by councillors who have spent two years managing what reaches him. Getting in front of him needs standing (Conall's warrant, Temerach, Nerissa) or an occasion he cannot avoid, such as the presentation, the Archery or the closing. Once there, the characters face a young man who wants very much to do one thing properly on his own judgement.

**If they tell him the truth plainly and ask him to decide rather than sign, he may decide well.** He can withdraw the standing order. He can refuse the champion's audience, if he believes what is in the champion, and that closes the Quiet Room; the Baron then needs another way into a room with him, and he will find one. What the Prince cannot do is arrest a baron on the strength of strangers' accusations, so the characters still need somebody with standing to make the charge.

## "We kill Santo."

The Baron loses his only legitimate heir. Blau's job becomes vengeance, and he becomes the characters' most persistent pursuer. If anybody connects the twins' face to the dead heir, the Baron's hunt for them begins at once, because the twins are now the only di Teufel blood walking. The Pearl wanted a reckoning and gets one, and Marisette may be grateful in ways that matter.

The Baron's plan is not touched at all. A party that kills Santo and thinks the scenario is over discovers on the second day of the Tourney that it has barely started.

## "We go after the camp."

Good. The Vantt Estate is the soft target. It has no walls, local labour comes and goes all day, twenty-five bored men drink a councillor's beer, the champion's spare kit is there, and so is the litter. Characters who work the camp can learn the company's mood, find Tat Atarer and start him counting the cost, see the Baron's routine from outside, or steal the Knights' horses and make them a laughing stock. They cannot reach the champion or the locket, because both are on the ship.

## "We never go near the Pearl."

The Pearl comes to them. Hesper takes the twins before dawn on the second day wherever they are, because Marisette's people know every inn in the city. Magda and Gardwen are lodged at the Sylph's Embrace whether or not they are player characters. Sinnit's lie about Randall reaches every stair by noon. A party that avoids the Pearl hears about its events as news, and should feel what they missed.

## "We split up."

That usually works well in this scenario, because the city is big and the week is crowded. Cut between them in short scenes and keep each scene to one decision. A character alone can be taken off a street at night, especially once there is a price on the face. Blau is patient, and a split party is exactly what he waits for.

## "We fight the Knights."

Three or four at a time, in the open, with the city watching, the characters can beat them, and you should let them. Every unanswered public incident advances the occupation, and an *answered* one is the first thing in nine days that has made the city stand up straight. But twenty-five Knights can be in one place within a watch, and Blau escalates fast. The characters should feel that they won the scene and raised the stakes. Remember the water: almost no Knight can swim.

## "We steal the locket ourselves."

Then the characters are the thieves, and Nus is either with them or very put out. Everything in *Two Thieves and a Table* applies. The difference comes the next morning: when the Lullwater burns, the Baron is hunting the characters, and Sinnit may be selling *them*. A party that holds the locket and keeps it hidden through the burning has earned the Breakout, and it should be the hardest day they ever play.

## "We destroy the locket."

Only a powerful Dispel Magic or an Exorcism can, and nobody in the party has either. Nerissa does not; the Order has no such art. The Quiet Hand might know somebody, and Hesper might, if anybody ever sees past the costume. Destroying the locket has the same effect as keeping it from the Baron, permanently: Thuban is no longer leashed, and the Breakout follows that evening wherever the champion is. It does not free Thuban from the world. Only the bones do that.

## "We take the boy."

Without the locket, taking the body frees nobody, and Orrin will say so. The Baron can still command Thuban, and does, and a boy carried off the ship becomes a dragon-knight in a cellar under orders to walk home. If the characters hold the locket, taking the body is the Breakout under another name.

## "We do nothing."

Then the week happens without them, exactly as this book describes, and on the last evening the Prince walks out of a small stone room right-handed. Tell them so, through whoever they trust, and let them decide whether they can live with it.
