# A City Built on Water

Purewater stands across the Mouth, the narrow strait where a great sacred lake drains south into the open sea. It is rich, strategic and rotten, and its own people call it Pisswater, for the smell of the canals and the smell of its politics alike.

There is no ground. Houses come straight up out of the channels with no bank, their doors opening onto steps that go down under the surface and keep going. Everything that matters moves by boat, poled, rowed and shouted about; the rest goes over a bridge or along a ledge a body or two wide with no rail, and every journey costs a fare. Anybody raised here can handle a boat the way they can walk. Nobody ever says the other half out loud: outsiders cannot. A stranger in Purewater cannot move a sack of flour across it without hiring somebody born here, and there are no horses at all. The land stops at Caravan Square on the western shore and does not resume.

The heart of the city is High Isle, which is really four islands cut apart by water: the North Quarter of fine houses, the Governor's Palazzo and the Tournament Ground; the Merchant's Quarter of guilds and counting-houses; the Forges, all smoke and foundries; and the Seaward docks. North of them, among the scattered Northern Isles, lies the Pearl, the pleasure island. North-east, across open water and reached by no bridge at all, lies the Lullwater, where the thieves and the working poor live together in a drowned maze. The Shoals guard the seaward side with their fish-markets and smugglers' jetties. And just north of the Tournament Ground, alone, unbridged and holy, sits Temple Isle. *People and Places in Purewater* walks every one of them.

## The Water Is the Goddess

The priestesses of the Lake Lady say the water *is* the goddess, and they mean it plainly. Her holy water wells up in the Sacred Confluence on Temple Isle and runs out through the temple into the city's channels, and by ancient writ the Order of the Lake Lady owns every channel and pool. The city council owns the isles. The most sacred ground in Purewater is also its traffic system, and that gives the Order a standing no council can overrule and no baron can buy.

Two houses serve the goddess, and neither can give the other an order. **The Order of the Lake Lady** is the priesthood: temples, pools, rites, tithes, the calendar and the Tourney. It is a civic power, and it is bound by temple law, which forbids the clergy to intervene in the city's affairs unless the sacred sites themselves are threatened. **The Swords of the Lake** are a knightly order sworn to the goddess, not to her clergy: austere, propertyless, realm-wide, holding the oldest battle honours from the uprising against the Dragon Kings. They take no tithes, keep no temples, and do not answer to the Governor's council. The two houses love each other like two branches of one family that cannot spend a week under the same roof, and in four hundred years neither has developed a polite way to ask the other a favour.

**The water is going wrong.** Since Candlemas the pools have been fouling, worst in the eastern yards near the Forges and creeping north toward the lake. It comes from the Baron's ship, whose hull steams wherever it meets the water: fire magic warring with the holy channel. The Order raised its alarm, and the alarm was signed off six weeks ago as a *false reading* by the Watercrafters' Tide Master, Ivo Calder, at one polite request from a man he respected. An instruction over the Prince's signature then silenced it. High Priestess Nerissa knows the reading was right. She can feel the fouling in her own body, and she drinks the water anyway. But a corrupted channel is not yet, technically, a threatened site, and so temple law ties her hands.

There is a worse secret in the Lady's house, and only one person knows it. **Lilura Deepcurrent**, who has been the Lady's voice for thirty years, has heard nothing from the goddess for fourteen months. She has told nobody, because a faith whose goddess has gone silent has no civic power at all. Whether the silence is the fouling, the bound dragon-knight in the harbour, or something older, nobody in this scenario will find out. But a Games Master who wants the Lady to stir at the end of the week, when the Lullwater burns or the Baron is named on her steps, has a reason ready.

## The Rule of the Pearl

Every child on the Pearl can tell the story of the Widow. Two hundred years ago a merchant prince bought a fifteen-year-old girl outright, with a bill of sale witnessed by two councillors, and kept her behind a wall on a little islet. After eleven years of lighting a lamp in her window every dusk and talking to the water, she asked the basin for her husband, and one calm night it took him. She bought the biggest house on the Pearl's quay with his money, opened its doors to women who had been sold as she had been, and laid down the rule every house on the island still keeps: **a night is for sale; a person is not.** No bill of sale for a woman is honoured on the Pearl, and no man who brings one is let in anywhere on the island again.

At dusk every evening the doorman of her old house, the Mother of Pearl, lights a taper from a lamp that has supposedly not gone out in two hundred years. Every house on the quay lights its own lamps from that taper, and so do the posts of the Lantern Bridge. The Pearl's women believe that no one under that flame can be owned: a bill of sale signed under the Pearl's lamps fades off the page by morning, and a binding of any kind fails.

None of that is confirmed, and nothing in the scenario depends on it. But Santo di Teufel is about to try to *bind* something into a woman, under the Pearl's lamps, in a house that keeps the Widow's rule. If the dice say it goes wrong, every woman on the quay will have decided why by breakfast. Let them.

## The Lake Lady's Tourney

The Tourney is the year's holy business: three days of contests in the goddess's honour, older than the Governor's office, drawing competitors from across the realm. For the week of it the city fills, everybody makes a year's money in six days, and the law goes quiet.

On the **eve of the Tourney**, every entrant in every event is presented to the Crown's representative by long form: names read, colours shown, and each competitor bare-headed for the space of a bow. It is ceremony, it is tedious, and it cannot be avoided without forfeiting the entry. It is also the only time before the sand that the Baron's champion must show his face in daylight.

The **first day** is the **Archery**, shot on the Long Butts beside the Lists: two hundred paces of grass, a crosswind off the lake that everybody complains about, and the Governor's box at the near end. It is the one open event. Anybody may shoot, with no colours and no fee, which makes it the only place all week where a person with no standing can stand in front of the Governor and be owed a hearing for as long as the crowd is watching.

The **second day** is the **Single Combat**, the only martial event: one-on-one bouts on the raked sand of the Lists, in front of four thousand people.

The **third day** is the **Run**, the only event that leaves the Tournament Ground. Competitors go out through the water-gate and race the whole city, on foot and by boat, by any route and with any help, and come back over open water to the temple steps. For two hours, Purewater has no crowd control at all. At dusk the Prince takes his place, and the **closing** begins: the prizes, the speeches and, behind a closed door, the champion's presentation.

## Who Runs Purewater

Nobody runs all of it, and that is the trouble.

The **Prince** governs in the King's name, and he is two-and-twenty and has learned to sign things rather than to decide them. A **council of merchant princes** runs the city's daily business, and three of them got his signature onto the order that silenced the watch. **Marisette** rules every house on the Pearl, on nothing but the settled expectation that she will still be there afterwards. **Crowbill** owns the canals nobody maps, and keeps a code: no killing locals, no harming children, no touching the Lady's sites. **Marda Blackwater** speaks for three hundred dock labourers, and through them, for every boat on the water-stairs. The **Order** owns the water and may not act. The **Swords** may act and may not speak for the water. The **watch** has been ordered, in writing, to do nothing.

Each of them could stop the Baron, alone, if they were willing to spend themselves doing it. None of them will go first. That is the city the characters walk into.
