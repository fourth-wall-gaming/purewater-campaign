# Palazzi and Paper

The men who govern Purewater do it with signatures, and the Baron has spent two years collecting theirs. None of them is a villain. Every one of them is a door the characters can open, and most are doors the Baron has already walked through.

## Prince Emeric

*Two-and-twenty, fair, thin, extremely good clothes, in a chair slightly too large for him.*

The King's nephew was sent to Purewater as Governor to learn to govern, and he has correctly worked out that he is being handled, though he cannot yet see the hands. He is earnest to a degree his council finds embarrassing. He takes people at face value, and he actually listens, which is so unusual in that chair that everybody reads it as weakness. He is delighted by competence in other people and says so, which costs him. He has signed a great many things and decided nothing, and he has started to notice the difference.

> "My uncle sent me here to learn to govern. I have learned to sign things. They are not the same skill."

He wants to do one thing properly, on his own judgement, without being managed into it. **That is what makes him the Baron's perfect target and the characters' best hope.** Under pressure, all the court language falls away and he asks the direct question nobody has prepared an answer for. A character who gets in front of him with something true, and asks him to decide rather than sign, may find that he decides well. He is also the man who, on the last day, will summon the Baron to answer to him in private, because it is the brave, decent and stupid thing to do.

## Alderic Vantt

*Fifty-two, warm-faced, well fed, beautifully turned out without being showy.*

The best host in Purewater: generous, funny, remembers your name, remembers your wife's name. He moves a conversation the way a man closes a door, so smoothly you only notice afterwards that you are in the corridor. He is a man of real charm and no spine, who has spent his life making rooms comfortable, and has now been comfortable in the wrong room for two years. Two winters ago the Baron quietly covered four thousand of his debts and asked for nothing. He calls it friendship. The Dragon Knights are camped on his water-meadow, the Baron's money runs through his accounts, and he drafted the standing order. He has not said out loud, not to his council and not to his wife, how frightened he is.

> "I have a very good cellar and no idea any more which of it I paid for."

**The tell is two self-deprecating jokes in a row.** Self-deprecation is his whole defensive repertoire, and he uses it in bursts; two in a row means you have found the thing. He wants to reach the end of the week with the Vantt name unattached to any of it, and he loves his daughter Ilse, who has been sent to her aunt's in the city for the duration and is furious about it. Push at the seam and he will talk about his debts before he talks about anything else.

## Hesketh Pyle

*Fifty-eight, well dressed and slightly unkempt with it. There is always a glass, and it is always further on than anybody else's.*

He was formidable once, by all accounts. Two years ago the Baron's people made his son's killing of a man go away, and kept the deposition, and Pyle has agreed with absolutely everything in the council chamber ever since, because agreement is the only posture from which nobody asks you a question. What is left is amiable, absent and enormously easy to have on a committee. He is not stupid; he is a clever man who has decided not to be present. He drinks in a wine-shop where councillors do not go.

> "I second that. I second most things. It has been a very restful two years."

**He is the crack in the council.** He wants all of this to end without his son's name appearing anywhere in it. If somebody could take that deposition out of the Baron's hands, or make it worthless, he would stop agreeing. And silence from the man who has agreed with everything for two years is the loudest thing that chamber has ever heard.

## Maro Quist

*Seventy-one, upright, his councillor's chain always worn, his hands entirely still.*

The oldest councillor in the city has spent fifty years in public life and has never yet found an argument he would rather win than understand. He carried the chamber for the standing order on the merits, having been given true facts in exactly the right order. He is incorruptible, formidable and completely wrong. He reads everything put in front of him, properly, in front of you, at his own pace, and does not apologise for the wait. Then he repeats your position back to you more accurately than you put it, and tells you exactly why it is not his to act on.

> "You have brought me a conclusion. Bring me the thing you concluded it from."

**His danger is that he has no secret, and therefore no lever,** and every other person in the chamber finds that unbearable. He is a member of a dining society, the Ember Society, that is a recruiting front for the Awake the Dragon Movement, and he thinks its members are antiquarians; he went to three dinners and thought the wine was poor. **A party that finds the Society's list will find his name near the top, next to the councillor who signed both of the Baron's instructions, and will be certain.** Accusing him in public is the most expensive mistake available in the political thread. What moves him is not the crime but the evidence: a scroll in the Baron's own hand, an Assay finding with a punch on it, Ivo Calder's original notes. **If he reads a document twice, he has changed his mind,** and the room should know it. Quist turned is the best ally in the city government. He cannot touch the Baron, but he can withdraw the standing order, and that changes every street in Purewater.

## Cassian Bree and the Ember Society

*Forty-four, courteous, immaculate cuffs, a faintly damp handshake. Utterly unmemorable, and working at it.*

The Ember Society meets eleven times a year in a hired upper room off the Merchant's Quarter: dinner at eight, an address at ten, port and argument until midnight, sixty names on the list. Its stated subject is the age before the lake, and its addresses are printed and bound and extremely boring. It was real once, thirty-one years ago. Eight years ago it acquired a new secretary and became a funnel for the Movement, without anybody noticing. No address ever mentions dragons or magic. Every one of them argues, in careful and rather beautiful prose, that the age is smaller than the one it replaced, and that the men who broke the Dragon Kings broke something else at the same time. By the time anybody is asked for anything, they have spent two years agreeing over port that the world has got smaller.

The Society has three rings. The **Table** is the sixty, who are never asked for anything. The **Readings** are a dozen who are invited, flatteringly, to private evenings with genuine old texts. The **Four** are each asked, once, for one specific thing only they can give, and are never told why.

**Cassian Bree** is the secretary, and the only real member of the Movement in Purewater. Everything he does is legal, and he will help you with all of it. He reports *on* the Baron to the Movement, not *to* him, and the Baron does not know. He has four letters in a locked file and no idea which of the two men he is caught between will kill him first for having them.

> "Take the whole ledger. I have nothing in it I would not read aloud at dinner."

Under pressure he becomes more generous: more access, more names, more paper. The flood is the defence, and the one sheet you actually want is somewhere in the middle of it.

## Ivo Calder

*Fifty, distinguished, guild indigo, instruments kept beautifully. He has not slept properly in six weeks, and it has started to show in his hands.*

The Watercrafters' Tide Master and the city's appointed assessor, and one of the Society's Four. Six weeks ago the Order's fouling alarm registered the corruption bleeding off the Baron's hull, the reading went to his guild, and he signed it off as a false reading, because a man he respects asked him to, at a private reading, without telling him why. On the strength of his signature, the alarm was silenced. He is a craftsman of real integrity who signed one reading he did not believe, for reasons that were almost good, and he has discovered that a man cannot hold one lie of that size without it taking up all the room. He has not destroyed his original notes. He has moved them twice, and he cannot explain to himself why he has not simply burned them.

> "The instrument was at the edge of its range. I have said that sentence eleven times now."

**There is no middle in him.** He mounts a vigorous, well-argued defence right up until an independent reading appears, from Nerissa's people, from Gardwen's own senses at the water's edge or from the Assay, and then the whole thing comes out at once, in one breath, with something close to relief. What his confession buys is a name, not a conviction: who is actually paying the Baron, a man who is not in this city and will not die on the sand. And his notes reopen the alarm, which puts the Order officially onto the Baron.

## Guildmaster Torval Bluehand

*Middle-aged, indigo sash, hands stained permanently blue to the wrist.*

The master of the Watercrafters is an engineer who loves the city's waterworks the way other men love their children, and whose curiosity is so strong it can be used on him. It already has been, once, through his own Tide Master, and he has not let himself finish thinking about that. He is delighted by mechanisms: he leans in, picks things up while discussing them and forgets to put them down. He is impatient with politics and lit up by detail.

> "Nobody has built anything new on this water in ninety years, and then a man moors THAT at my door."

He wants to get aboard that steaming hull and find out what it is doing to his water. He offers the craft (a demonstration, a drawing, the run of his workshop) because generosity is how this man asks for help. A party that wants an honest reading of the water, or an excuse to look at the Baron's ship from up close, could do much worse.

## Harbormaster Velen Deepkeel

*Weathered, dark blue coat badged by rank, forty years of it. Formal even sitting down.*

He believes, sincerely and after four decades, that a port which is written down correctly cannot be stolen, and he has arranged his working life so that he never has to find out what his Port Captains actually take. He squares the edges of paper while he talks. He will tell you which form your complaint should have arrived on, and he is not being obstructive; he means it helpfully. He knows. He has known for years. He has built an entire administrative life around never being told.

> "Every hull on this water is written down. Including that one. Nobody has ever asked me for the page."

**He is the door to maritime law,** the second court the standing order cannot reach. A boat commandeered with its boatman still aboard is the port's business, not the council's, and so is a foreign lord's berth. Under pressure he recites the rule being broken, flat, from memory, all the way to the end, and the fact that he has it by heart is the whole of the feeling in the man.

## Sergeant Abel Weir

*Forty-one, heavy, tired, in a blue-grey watch coat, with a four-inch book under one arm that never leaves him.*

A career watchman who was given an order he considers monstrous, has obeyed it every day since, and has kept a perfect record of his own obedience against a day that may never come. The book is the referral book: every complaint against the Baron's men, numbered, dated, with two witnesses, and not one of them answered. He is slow, thorough and unexcitable, and much gentler than his size suggests. He takes real pride in the craft of it, and he has no taste for violence or theatre. He is ashamed, every day, in a way he has no words for, and procedure is the only place he has found to put it.

> "Thirty-one entries. I can tell you all of them from memory, and not one of them has been answered."

**He wants to hand the book to somebody who can use it without being the man who disobeyed.** It is four inches of ammunition with no gun; the characters' problem is finding the gun. Put the book in front of somebody who outranks the council, such as the Prince, Quist turned, the Harbormaster or a Sword, and it becomes the most dangerous document in the city. Under pressure, his procedure gets more elaborate, not less, and the more correct he becomes, the closer he is to the thing he cannot say. If the council orders the watch to clear the water-stairs by force, Abel Weir will not do it.
