# Palazzi and Paper

The men who govern Purewater do it with signatures, and the Baron has spent two years collecting theirs. None of them is a villain. Each of them is a door the characters can open, and the Baron has already walked through most of them.

## Prince Emeric

*Two-and-twenty, fair, thin, extremely good clothes, in a chair slightly too large for him.*

The King's nephew was sent to Purewater as Governor to learn to govern, and he has correctly worked out that he is being handled, though he cannot yet see the hands. He is so earnest that his council finds it embarrassing. He takes people at face value, and he actually listens, which is so unusual in that chair that everybody reads it as weakness. He is delighted by competence in other people and says so, which costs him. He has signed a great many things and decided nothing, and he has started to notice the difference.

> "My uncle sent me here to learn to govern. I have learned to sign things. They are not the same skill."

He wants to do one thing properly, on his own judgement, without being managed into it. **That makes him the Baron's perfect target and the characters' best hope.** Under pressure the court language falls away and he asks the direct question nobody has prepared an answer for. A character who gets in front of him with something true, and asks him to decide rather than sign, may find that he decides well. He is also the man who, on the last day, summons the Baron to answer to him in private, because it is the brave, decent and stupid thing to do.

## Alderic Vantt

*Fifty-two, warm-faced, well fed, beautifully turned out without being showy.*

Vantt is the best host in Purewater: generous and funny, and he remembers your name and your wife's name. He steers a conversation the way a man closes a door, so smoothly that you only notice afterwards that you are standing in the corridor. He has real charm and no spine. He has spent his life making rooms comfortable, and for two years now he has been comfortable in the wrong room.

Two winters ago the Baron covered four thousand of his debts, without a word to anyone, and asked for nothing. Vantt calls it friendship. The Dragon Knights are camped on his water-meadow, the Baron's money runs through his accounts, and he drafted the standing order. He has not told anybody how frightened he is, not his council and not his wife.

> "I have a very good cellar and no idea any more which of it I paid for."

**His tell is two self-deprecating jokes in a row.** Self-deprecation is his only defence, and he uses it in bursts. Two in a row means the character has found the sore place. He wants to reach the end of the week with the Vantt name attached to none of it, and he loves his daughter Ilse, who has been sent to her aunt's in the city for the duration and is furious about it. Press him there and he will talk about his debts before he talks about anything else.

## Hesketh Pyle

*Fifty-eight, well dressed and slightly unkempt with it. There is always a glass, and it is always further on than anybody else's.*

He was formidable once, by all accounts. Two years ago the Baron's people made his son's killing of a man go away, and kept the deposition. Since then Pyle has agreed with everything said in the council chamber, because nobody asks a question of a man who always agrees. What is left is amiable, absent and very easy to have on a committee. He is a clever man who has decided not to be present. He drinks in a wine-shop where councillors do not go.

> "I second that. I second most things. It has been a very restful two years."

**He is the weak point in the council.** He wants all of this to end without his son's name appearing anywhere in it. If somebody took that deposition out of the Baron's hands, or made it worthless, he would stop agreeing, and the whole chamber would hear the silence of the man who has agreed with everything for two years.

## Maro Quist

*Seventy-one, upright, his councillor's chain always worn, his hands entirely still.*

The oldest councillor in the city has spent fifty years in public life and has never yet found an argument he would rather win than understand. Somebody gave him true facts in exactly the right order, and he carried the chamber for the standing order on the merits. He is incorruptible, formidable and completely wrong. He reads everything put in front of him, properly, in front of you, at his own pace, and does not apologise for the wait. Then he repeats your position back to you more accurately than you put it, and tells you exactly why it is not his to act on.

> "You have brought me a conclusion. Bring me the thing you concluded it from."

**He has no secret, so nobody has a lever on him,** and every other person in the chamber finds that unbearable. He belongs to a dining society, the Ember Society, which is a recruiting front for the Awake the Dragon Movement. He thinks its members are antiquarians. He went to three dinners and thought the wine was poor.

**A party that finds the Society's list will find his name near the top, next to the councillor who signed both of the Baron's instructions, and will be certain he is guilty.** Accusing him in public is the most expensive mistake the characters can make in the political thread. Evidence moves him, where accusations do not: a scroll in the Baron's own hand, an Assay finding with a punch on it, Ivo Calder's original notes. **If he reads a document twice, he has changed his mind,** and you should make sure the room sees it. Quist turned is the best ally in the city government. He cannot touch the Baron, but he can withdraw the standing order, and that changes every street in Purewater.

## Cassian Bree and the Ember Society

*Forty-four, courteous, immaculate cuffs, a faintly damp handshake. Utterly unmemorable, and working at it.*

The Ember Society meets eleven times a year in a hired upper room off the Merchant's Quarter: dinner at eight, an address at ten, port and argument until midnight, sixty names on the list. Its stated subject is the age before the lake, and its addresses are printed and bound and extremely boring.

It was a real society once, thirty-one years ago. Eight years ago it acquired a new secretary and became a funnel for the Movement, and nobody noticed. No address ever mentions dragons or magic. Every one of them argues, in careful and rather beautiful prose, that this age is smaller than the one it replaced, and that the men who broke the Dragon Kings broke something else at the same time. By the time anybody is asked for anything, they have spent two years agreeing over port that the world has got smaller.

The Society has three rings:

- **The Table** is the sixty, who are never asked for anything.
- **The Readings** are a dozen who are invited, flatteringly, to private evenings with genuine old texts.
- **The Four** are each asked, once, for one specific thing only they can give, and are never told why.

This year's Four are:

- **Ivo Calder**, who gave a *signature*: the false reading on the fouling alarm.
- **Tide Lord Aurelie Stane** of the Harbor Masters, who gave a *berth*: the private pier below the Forge Tower where the Baron's ship lies.
- **Sister Anwen Coll** of the Order, who gave a *survey*: the old map of the three springs the Order sealed under the Forges. She was passed over for the archivist's post, and she is beginning to suspect what her map was used for.
- **Master Tobias Wrenn**, a clerk of the Prince's household, who gave a *name*, by putting Emeric's on the order that silenced the watch. Wrenn wants to be indispensable to the Prince, and he answers to Lady Marr.

**Breaking the Society does not stop the Baron this week.** Nothing in that upper room is needed for the Tourney. The Society matters after it: if the Baron wins, it is the root of his court, and if he loses, it is where he rebuilds.

**Cassian Bree** is the secretary, and the only real member of the Movement in Purewater. He picks who goes further in, and he reports to **Lady Ysolde Marr** at Caldreth, the Steward of the Ember House, who does the same work for the court. Everything Bree does is legal, and he will help the characters with all of it. He has four letters in a locked file, and a memory.

> "Take the whole ledger. I have nothing in it I would not read aloud at dinner."

Under pressure he becomes more generous: more access, more names, more paper. He defends himself with the flood, and the one sheet the characters want is somewhere in the middle of it.

### Lady Ysolde Marr

Lady Marr is not in Purewater, and the characters meet her only through paper: Bree's reports, the courier's packet, and the ember mark that countersigns the money. She is about sixty, the widow of a Caldreth judge, and she runs the Ember House as a salon where the court's younger sons learn to agree that the age has got smaller. She does not want the throne. She wants to stand behind it and be thanked. She flatters the Baron well and believes he will be grateful, and a man like Hanzo never is. If the characters can show her that, she becomes the weak point in his court.

## Ivo Calder

*Fifty, distinguished, guild indigo, instruments kept beautifully. He has not slept properly in six weeks, and it has started to show in his hands.*

Calder is the Watercrafters' Tide Master, the city's appointed assessor, and one of the Society's Four. Six weeks ago the Order's fouling alarm registered the corruption bleeding off the Baron's hull, and the reading went to his guild. He signed it off as a false reading because a man he respects asked him to, at a private reading, without telling him why, and on the strength of his signature the alarm was silenced. He is a craftsman of real integrity who signed one reading he did not believe, for reasons that were almost good, and he has found that a lie that size takes up all the room in a man. He has not destroyed his original notes. He has moved them twice, and he cannot explain to himself why he has not simply burned them.

> "The instrument was at the edge of its range. I have said that sentence eleven times now."

**He holds out completely, and then all at once he gives way.** He mounts a vigorous, well-argued defence right up until an independent reading appears, from Nerissa's people, from Gardwen's own senses at the water's edge or from the Assay. Then the whole thing comes out in one breath, with something close to relief. His confession buys a name, and no conviction: the name of who is really paying the Baron, a man who is not in this city and will not die on the sand. His notes reopen the alarm, which puts the Order officially onto the Baron.

## Guildmaster Torval Bluehand

*Middle-aged, indigo sash, hands stained permanently blue to the wrist.*

The master of the Watercrafters is an engineer who loves the city's waterworks the way other men love their children, and his curiosity is so strong it can be used against him. Somebody has already used it, once, through his own Tide Master, and he has not let himself finish thinking about that. He is delighted by mechanisms. He leans in, picks things up while discussing them and forgets to put them down. He is impatient with politics and lit up by detail.

> "Nobody has built anything new on this water in ninety years, and then a man moors THAT at my door."

He wants to get aboard that steaming hull and find out what it is doing to his water. He offers the craft (a demonstration, a drawing, the run of his workshop) because generosity is how this man asks for help. A party that wants an honest reading of the water, or an excuse to look at the Baron's ship up close, could do much worse.

## Harbormaster Velen Deepkeel

*Weathered, dark blue coat badged by rank, forty years of it. Formal even sitting down.*

After four decades Deepkeel believes, sincerely, that a port which is written down correctly cannot be stolen, and he has arranged his working life so that he never has to find out what his Port Captains actually take. He squares the edges of paper while he talks. He will tell you which form your complaint should have arrived on, and he means it helpfully. He knows what goes on. He has known for years, and he has built an entire administrative life around never being told.

> "Every hull on this water is written down. Including that one. Nobody has ever asked me for the page."

**He is the door to maritime law,** the second court the standing order cannot reach. A boat commandeered with its boatman still aboard is the port's business, not the council's, and so is a foreign lord's berth. Under pressure he recites the rule being broken, flat, from memory, all the way to the end. That he has it by heart is the only feeling he shows.

## Sergeant Abel Weir

*Forty-one, heavy, tired, in a blue-grey watch coat, with a four-inch book under one arm that never leaves him.*

Weir is a career watchman who was given an order he considers monstrous, has obeyed it every day since, and has kept a perfect record of his own obedience against a day that may never come. The book is the referral book: every complaint against the Baron's men, numbered, dated, with two witnesses, and not one of them answered. He is slow, thorough and unexcitable, and much gentler than his size suggests. He takes real pride in the craft of it, and he has no taste for violence or theatre. He is ashamed, every day, in a way he has no words for, and procedure is the only place he has found to put it.

> "Thirty-one entries. I can tell you all of them from memory, and not one of them has been answered."

**He wants to hand the book to somebody who can use it without making him the man who disobeyed.** By itself, the book does nothing; the characters have to find somebody who can act on it. Put it in front of somebody who outranks the council, such as the Prince, Quist once he has turned, the Harbormaster or a Sword, and it becomes the most dangerous document in the city. Under pressure his procedure gets more elaborate, and the more correct he becomes, the closer he is to saying the thing he cannot say. If the council orders the watch to clear the water-stairs by force, Abel Weir will not do it.
