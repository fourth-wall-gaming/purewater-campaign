# The Lullwater and the Docks

The people who actually make Purewater work live across the water from the people who run it: the thieves, the dredgers, the boatmen and the men who know which canals are not on any map. They are the city's working underside, and by the end of the week, its army.

## Crowbill

*Wiry, entirely forgettable, middle years. A small crow inked on the back of the right hand, and no other mark anywhere on him.*

The crime lord of Purewater keeps no house and no office. For thirty years he has sat on the flat lead roof over the cookshop ovens beside the Gutter, eating out of a paper and not looking up, or late at night in the back of an ice-house on the Shoals. He owns the canals nobody maps: smuggling, gambling, protection, and the street boys who see everything. He has learned that information is the only thing in a city that cannot be confiscated, and he can no longer stop collecting it, even about things he does not care about. He is quiet, dry, oddly restful company, never in a hurry, never filling a silence, curious about people the way a naturalist is curious.

> "You've not asked me for anything yet. Most people manage it in under a minute."

**He does not help people. He trades,** and his price is always the same: something he can use. That might be intelligence on the Baron, a route into a fortified camp, or control of a story the street is already telling. When he decides you are not worth it, he goes on being pleasant and simply stops trading. The conversation continues, nothing further comes out of it, and you are not told when that happened.

He keeps a code: no killing locals, no harming children, no touching the Lady's sites. The Knights are breaking the spirit of all three, and for the first time in thirty years he has no lever to answer them with. What he wants is a table that is level, a city that is predictable, and a profitable Tourney week. **Demon-craft worked on a helpless girl beside the Lady's water crosses his line.** That is not crime; it is a monster kicking the whole table over. Bring him proof rather than a grievance, and he opens canals, safe houses, men and the street's rumour machine. **And he knows about the locket**: his lamp-man on the Forge wharf watches the Baron's ship for a living. See *Arrows and the Ship*.

## Kerrin

*Dockside, unremarkable, brown coat, easy in a crowd.*

One of Crowbill's watchers: eleven years of reading faces across a room. He follows; he does not close. He worked out long ago that a watcher who lies is a watcher who gets remembered, so he tells the truth all day long and gives nothing away. Being spotted does not embarrass him at all. A character who makes him finds him pleasant, patient and genuinely good company for the length of a drink, which is considerably more unsettling than a denial would be.

> "You've made me. That's fine — I'd rather buy you a drink than follow you about in the rain."

## Kag

*Half-orc, thirties, a head and a half taller than anybody in the room and built to match. Leather, worn. A club on the belt that is plainly the thing she reaches for.*

Crowbill's muscle, and the only person in his outfit who can read a ledger. Kag runs his errands and his business on the streets: the collections round on the Shoals, cartage around the Knights' bridges, small disputes settled before they become his problem, and a hustle or two of her own, such as buying up lamp oil before the stairs stop, or laying odds on the Archery against the city's prejudice about elves. She is dry, flat, unhurried and much funnier than she appears. She sits on floors, eats standing up and sleeps anywhere, and she listens all the way to the end of a sentence, which almost nobody does.

> "That's not short because he's skimming. That's short because they took sixty feet of his line and there's nobody to bill."

**She has no part in the plot,** and that is deliberate: she is the city's working underside seen up close, a door to Crowbill, and a number when the characters need one. **How the characters talk to her decides what she is.** Address the muscle, give her orders, talk past her to the man beside her or be surprised that she reads, and she is exactly as much use as they paid for. She does not sulk or sabotage; she simply never mentions the thing that would have saved them two days. Ask her what something *costs*, and she is the best analyst in Purewater, for nothing, permanently.

## Nus

*Young, quick-fingered, dressed rather better than he can account for.*

A pickpocket with real talent and no judgement at all, and Randall's friend: lookout, fence and second pair of hands. He is grinning, restless, funny, and generous with money the moment he has any; something is always moving in his hands. He listens badly and brilliantly, missing half of what you say and catching the one detail that can be turned into a job. He has worked out that being thought the man for it is worth more to him than being alive, and he has not yet met the situation that tests that.

> "I could get in there. I'm not saying I should. I'm saying nobody else could."

**This week,** he wants one big score and a name: a theft big enough that Crowbill's people have to respect him. He takes the job nobody sensible takes. Warning him off only makes him keener. Cornered, he does not retreat; he raises the stakes out loud, and the size of the offer is the size of the fear. When Crowbill names the Baron's locket in his hearing, he is going to go after it, and if he gets it, the Baron will turn a quarter of the city inside out to find him. **Nus is the scenario's most expensive friend.** Whether he lives to the end of the week is the clearest single measure of how well the characters did.

## Sinnit

*Big, canal-rat, permanently aggrieved. Fills a doorway and knows it.*

Seven years ago on Pearl Quay, in front of the Promise of Heaven, Randall lifted Sinnit's purse and handed it back to him with a bow while the house's girls laughed. Everybody who saw it has forgotten. Sinnit has built his whole sense of himself on that humiliation, and he needs it re-staged in front of witnesses before he can put it down. He is loud, aggressive and physically encroaching, and much less frightening than he needs to be. He is not clever and not actually cruel. He has a grievance, it is the only tool in his box, and so everything gets treated as a grievance. He announces exactly what he is going to do before he does it. That is the only mercy in him, and it is a real one.

> "You'll remember it in a minute. Everyone does, once I've reminded them properly."

**This week he is the street's antagonist, and a real one,** not because he is clever but because this is the one week Randall's face is worth money. He sees things, and for the first time in his life the things he sees are worth selling to people who can hurt Randall far worse than he ever could. On the first evening he corners the wrong man. On the second day he tells the whole Pearl that Randall carved the girl. On the eve of the Tourney he sells the secret of the two faces to Blau. On the last day, with the Lullwater burning, he sells Nus's hiding place. Threats do not stop him. Beating him in public is a second humiliation and only makes him worse. Money buys a day, never more.

**One thing stops him,** and it is the cheapest thing in the scenario: an apology from Randall, out loud, in front of people who matter to Sinnit. When it comes, he stands there for a long moment, and then he is a loud, entirely unreliable and genuinely brave ally. The next time somebody in dragon colours comes for one of the Lullwater's own, Sinnit is in the doorway: *"Nobody does that to our people."*

## Marda Blackwater

*Forties, broad, dredge-boat hands, a black armband with a white wave on it.*

The leader of the Dredgers, the labour guild and mutual-aid society of Purewater's working poor: canal-clearers, dockers and fishers, three hundred strong. She was pushed into speaking for them, she is better at it than she expected, and she is discovering that the distance between leading them and being carried by them is about four days wide. She is loud, plain, physically busy and without ceremony. She trusts numbers and distrusts sympathy, and she will hand you one end of whatever she is carrying in the middle of a sentence without thinking of it as rude. Somebody's boy went into that water and came out wrong, she was the one who had to tell his mother, and she has not slept properly since.

> "Give me one man's wages and one name and I can hold them another day. Give me sympathy and I can't."

**She is the most reasonable person in this story, and the easiest to help,** and she is dangerous for exactly that reason. She does not want a riot, and she will get one anyway. She wants Emmit Rill's wages paid, the two missing boatmen accounted for, and one Knight answering for something in front of a magistrate, done properly, in daylight, by somebody official, so that it counts. She petitions the Harbor Masters and gets a referral. She petitions the council in writing and is not answered. If nothing comes of it by the eve of the Tourney, she uses the only lever she has: she stops the water-stairs. **Watch her demands.** The smaller and more reasonable they get, the closer the thing is to going off, because what she is really doing is finding the least she can accept and still hold three hundred people. Get her any one of the three, and she holds them another day, every time. She is also the fastest way to three hundred bodies if the characters ever need a crowd.

## Thorne

*Old, wrapped up warm in every weather, a cane across his knees. Blue eyes much too vivid for the rest of him.*

The storyteller of the Moist Oyster holds court by the hearth most evenings, collecting tales from new arrivals and paying for them with his own. He is sociable, performing, mildly vain about being good company, and funnier than he needs to be, and the funniness is deliberate. His cane hides a blade. Secretly, he was once an initiate of the Order of the Lake, and he survived a Dragon Knight raid forty years ago by doing something he has never described to anybody. The story he tells instead has been polished for four decades into something people enjoy hearing. He knows more about the Dragon Kings, and about the weak points of a possession ritual, than anybody else in the city.

> "I have told that one four hundred times and nobody has ever asked me whether it was true."

He wants to be listened to once as a witness rather than as entertainment. Ask him whether the story is true, and the performance falls off him entirely; what is underneath is plainer, much older, and considerably harder to sit with. Temerach sends Conall to him on the first evening with a sealed note. Conall never arrives, because a man called Sinnit gets to him first.
