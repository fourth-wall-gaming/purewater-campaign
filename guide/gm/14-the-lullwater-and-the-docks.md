# The Lullwater and the Docks

The people who make Purewater work live across the water from the people who run it: the thieves, the dredgers, the boatmen and the men who know which canals are not on any map. They are the city's working underside, and by the end of the week they are its army.

## Crowbill

*Wiry, entirely forgettable, middle years. A small crow inked on the back of the right hand, and no other mark anywhere on him.*

The crime lord of Purewater keeps no house and no office. For thirty years he has sat on the flat lead roof over the cookshop ovens beside the Gutter, eating out of a paper and not looking up, or late at night in the back of an ice-house on the Shoals. He owns the canals nobody maps: smuggling, gambling, protection, and the street boys who see everything. He learned long ago that information is the only thing in a city that cannot be confiscated, and now he cannot stop collecting it, even about things he does not care about. He is quiet, dry and oddly restful company. He is never in a hurry, never fills a silence, and is curious about people the way a naturalist is curious about birds.

> "You've not asked me for anything yet. Most people manage it in under a minute."

**Crowbill trades, and he never just helps.** His price is always something he can use: intelligence on the Baron, a route into a fortified camp, or control of a story the street is already telling. When he decides somebody is not worth it, he goes on being pleasant and stops trading. The conversation continues, nothing more comes out of it, and nobody tells the character when that happened.

He keeps a code:

1. No killing canal folk, meaning the people of the Lullwater and the Shoals.
2. No harming their children.
3. No touching the Lady's water where the low water would feel it.

High-born holy places are fair game if the poor would not feel it as a wrong, and everyone else is on the menu. He is merciless, and he keeps his rules. The Knights are breaking the spirit of all three, and for the first time in thirty years he has no lever to answer them with. He wants a level table, a predictable city and a profitable Tourney week.

**Demon-craft worked on a helpless girl beside the Lady's water crosses his line.** To him that is a monster kicking the whole table over. If the characters bring him proof rather than a grievance, he opens his canals, safe houses, men and the street's rumour machine to them. **He also knows about the locket**, because his lamp-man on the Forge wharf watches the Baron's ship for a living. See *Arrows and the Ship*.

## Kerrin

*Dockside, unremarkable, brown coat, easy in a crowd.*

Kerrin is one of Crowbill's watchers, with eleven years of reading faces across a room behind him. He follows people and leaves the closing to others. He worked out long ago that a watcher who lies is a watcher who gets remembered, so he tells the truth all day long and gives nothing away. Being spotted does not embarrass him at all. A character who makes him finds him pleasant, patient and good company for the length of a drink, which is far more unsettling than a denial would be.

> "You've made me. That's fine — I'd rather buy you a drink than follow you about in the rain."

## Kag

*Half-orc, thirties, a head and a half taller than anybody in the room and built to match. Leather, worn. A club on the belt that is plainly the thing she reaches for.*

Kag is Crowbill's muscle, and the only person in his outfit who can read a ledger. She runs his errands and his business on the streets: the collections round on the Shoals, cartage around the Knights' bridges, small disputes settled before they become his problem. She runs a hustle or two of her own as well, such as buying up lamp oil before the stairs stop, or laying odds on the Archery against the city's prejudice about elves. She is dry, flat, unhurried and much funnier than she looks. She sits on floors, eats standing up and sleeps anywhere, and she listens all the way to the end of a sentence, which almost nobody does.

> "That's not short because he's skimming. That's short because they took sixty feet of his line and there's nobody to bill."

**Kag has no part in the plot.** She shows the city's working underside up close, she is a way to reach Crowbill, and she has a number when the characters need one. **How the characters talk to her decides what she is to them.** If they treat her as the muscle, give her orders, talk past her to the man beside her or are surprised that she reads, she is exactly as much use as they paid for. She does not sulk or sabotage. She just never mentions the thing that would have saved them two days. If they ask her what something *costs*, she becomes the best analyst in Purewater, for nothing, for good.

## Nus

*Young, quick-fingered, dressed rather better than he can account for.*

Nus is a pickpocket with real talent and no judgement at all, and he is Randall's friend: lookout, fence and second pair of hands. He grins a lot, he is restless and funny, and he is generous with money the moment he has any. Something is always moving in his hands. He listens badly and brilliantly, missing half of what you say and catching the one detail that can be turned into a job. He has decided that being thought the man for the job is worth more to him than being alive, and nothing has yet tested that.

> "I could get in there. I'm not saying I should. I'm saying nobody else could."

**This week** he wants one big score and a name: a theft big enough that Crowbill's people have to respect him. He takes the job nobody sensible takes, and warning him off only makes him keener. Cornered, he does not back down. He raises the stakes out loud, and the size of the offer shows the size of the fear. When Crowbill names the Baron's locket in his hearing, Nus is going to go after it, and if he gets it, the Baron will turn a quarter of the city inside out to find him.

**Nus is the most expensive friend in the scenario.** Whether he lives to the end of the week is the clearest single measure of how well the characters did.

## Sinnit

*Big, canal-rat, permanently aggrieved. Fills a doorway and knows it.*

Seven years ago on Pearl Quay, in front of the Promise of Heaven, Randall lifted Sinnit's purse and handed it back to him with a bow while the house's girls laughed. Everybody who saw it has forgotten. Sinnit has built his whole idea of himself on that humiliation, and he needs it played out again in front of witnesses before he can put it down. He is loud, aggressive and physically crowding, and much less frightening than he needs to be. He is not clever and he is not cruel. A grievance is the only tool he has, so he treats everything as one. He announces exactly what he is going to do before he does it, and that is the one mercy in him.

> "You'll remember it in a minute. Everyone does, once I've reminded them properly."

**This week he is the street's antagonist, and a dangerous one,** because this is the one week Randall's face is worth money. Sinnit sees things, and for the first time in his life what he sees is worth selling to people who can hurt Randall far worse than he ever could.

- **First evening:** he corners the wrong man.
- **Second day:** he tells the whole Pearl that Randall carved the girl.
- **Eve of the Tourney:** he sells the secret of the two faces to Blau.
- **Last day,** with the Lullwater burning: he sells Nus's hiding place.

Threats do not stop him. Beating him in public is a second humiliation and only makes him worse. Money buys a day, never more.

**One thing stops him, and it is the cheapest thing in the scenario:** an apology from Randall, out loud, in front of people who matter to Sinnit. When it comes, he stands there for a long moment, and then he becomes a loud, wholly unreliable and very brave ally. The next time somebody in dragon colours comes for one of the Lullwater's own, Sinnit is in the doorway: *"Nobody does that to our people."*

## Marda Blackwater

*Forties, broad, dredge-boat hands, a black armband with a white wave on it.*

Marda leads the Dredgers, the labour guild and mutual-aid society of Purewater's working poor: canal-clearers, dockers and fishers, three hundred strong. She was pushed into speaking for them, she is better at it than she expected, and she is finding out that the distance between leading them and being carried by them is about four days wide. She is loud, plain, physically busy and without ceremony. She trusts numbers and distrusts sympathy, and she will hand you one end of whatever she is carrying in the middle of a sentence without thinking it rude. Somebody's boy went into that water and came out wrong. She was the one who had to tell his mother, and she has not slept properly since.

> "Give me one man's wages and one name and I can hold them another day. Give me sympathy and I can't."

**She is the most reasonable person in this story and the easiest to help, and that makes her dangerous.** She does not want a riot, and she will get one anyway. She wants three things, done properly, in daylight, by somebody official, so that they count:

1. Emmit Rill's wages paid.
2. The two missing boatmen accounted for.
3. One Knight answering for something in front of a magistrate.

She petitions the Harbor Masters and gets a referral. She petitions the council in writing and gets no answer. If nothing comes of it by the eve of the Tourney, she uses the only lever she has, and stops the water-stairs.

**Watch her demands.** The smaller and more reasonable they get, the closer the stairs are to stopping, because she is looking for the least she can accept and still hold three hundred people. Get her any one of the three, and she holds them another day, every time. She is also the fastest way to three hundred bodies if the characters ever need a crowd.

## Thorne

*Old, wrapped up warm in every weather, a cane across his knees. Blue eyes much too vivid for the rest of him.*

The storyteller of the Moist Oyster holds court by the hearth most evenings, collecting tales from new arrivals and paying for them with his own. He is sociable and a performer, a little vain about being good company, and funnier than he needs to be, on purpose. His cane hides a blade. In secret, he was once an initiate of the Order of the Lake, and he survived a Dragon Knight raid forty years ago by doing something he has never described to anybody. The story he tells instead has been polished for four decades into something people enjoy hearing. He knows more about the Dragon Kings, and about the weak points of a possession ritual, than anybody else in the city.

> "I have told that one four hundred times and nobody has ever asked me whether it was true."

He wants to be listened to, once, as a witness instead of as entertainment. If a character asks him whether the story is true, the performance falls away, and the man underneath is plainer, much older and much harder to listen to. Temerach sends Conall to him on the first evening with a sealed note. Conall never arrives, because a man called Sinnit gets to him first.
