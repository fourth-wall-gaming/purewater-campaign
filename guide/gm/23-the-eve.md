# The Eve

*The day before the Tourney.* The docks may stop. The characters will probably carry their proof to somebody who reads every word of it and then explains, accurately, why it is not his to act on. And at the presentation of the entrants, the champion's helm comes off for four seconds, and Gardwen sees her brother. By midnight, unless somebody has made peace with a canal-rat, the Baron knows he has more sons than he thought.

## The Empty Stairs

<!-- covers: the-stairs-stop -->

If nobody has helped Marda Blackwater by now (if Emmit Rill has not been paid, the two boatmen have not been accounted for, and the referral book has not reached anybody who outranks the council), then at dawn on the eve of the Tourney **the water-stairs are empty.**

No cartage, no provisioning, no boats for hire, no ice, no fish, no lamp oil. And no competitors' gear moved to the Tournament Ground, which is the part that turns a labour dispute into a sacred crisis. Marda stands at the head of the Fishmarket Stair and is entirely reasonable about it, which is what makes her formidable. Her terms are small: wages for Emmit Rill, an accounting of the two boatmen, and one Knight answering for something in front of a magistrate. Any one of the three, and the stairs move again.

Everybody feels it by mid-morning. **The Baron** cannot have this. A stopped port on the eve of the Tourney draws the Prince's personal attention onto his men two days before he needs a quiet room, and Tat Atarer tells him so bluntly. This is the pressure point where Hanzo can be made to give something up, such as a Knight handed to a magistrate or a purse for Rill, and if the characters are clever, they can be the ones who broker it. **The council** leans on the watch to clear the stairs, and the watch has to decide whether to club three hundred of its own neighbours on the orders of the men who wrote the standing order. Abel Weir will not. **The Order** cares, because the Tourney is sacred and the festival is being strangled. And **the characters** can broker it, break it or arm it. A crowd of three hundred angry dockers at the Tournament Ground is a thing the Baron's plan does not survive, and neither, possibly, does anybody standing in front of it.

Put the empty stair in front of the players and stop. If nobody brokers it, the stairs open again at dusk on the first day of the Tourney, on the Prince's personal promise to hear Marda after the closing, and the anger goes back into the Lullwater to wait. If somebody did help Marda earlier in the week, none of this happens. She holds her people, and remembers who helped her.

## The Man Who Reads Everything

<!-- covers: quist-reads-it-to-the-end -->

At some point on the eve, or whenever the characters think they finally have enough, somebody will take the proof to the city's government. The obvious door is the oldest councillor in Purewater, at Palazzo Quist in the Merchant's Quarter. It is the wall of the scenario, in the person of a decent man.

Maro Quist is seventy-one, upright, with his chain on. He does not interrupt and cannot be interrupted. He reads the whole thing, actually reads it, in front of them, at his own pace, while they stand there. Then he repeats their position back to them more accurately than they put it, and tells them exactly why it is not his. The watch is under a written standing order, which he carried through the chamber himself, on the merits. He is not corrupt, he is not frightened, and he is not persuadable, because he is right: a baron's son with a knife is weather, and you do not sue the weather.

Play the wait. Let the players stand in that room while an old man reads, and do not fill the silence for them. **If he reads it twice, he has changed his mind, and the room should know it.** What moves him is not the crime but the hand: a scroll in the Baron's own writing, an Assay finding with Conall's punch on it, or Ivo Calder's original notes. A turned Quist cannot touch the Baron, but he can withdraw the standing order, and that changes every street in Purewater by nightfall. And if the characters have found the Ember Society's list and come in certain that he is the Baron's creature, this is the most expensive mistake in the political thread. Let them make it, and let it cost them the one honest man in the chamber.

## The Helm Comes Off

<!-- covers: the-presentation-of-the-entrants -->

On the eve of the Tourney, by long form, every entrant in every event is presented to Prince Emeric on the Tournament Ground: names read, colours shown, and each competitor bare-headed for the space of a bow. It is ceremony, it is tedious, and the whole city half-watches it from the stands. The Baron cannot avoid it without forfeiting the entry. So the champion is walked out in harness with the rest of them, and for perhaps four seconds the helm is off, and a seventeen-year-old who has been opened every night for a decade stands in daylight in front of several hundred people.

**If Gardwen is there, she sees her brother.** Ten years, and he is thirty yards away in somebody else's colours. Play the recognition, and do not resolve what she does about it. Hand it back to the player and wait. If Gardwen is a Non-Player Character, she still sees him, and the party learns it from a woman who has stopped speaking.

**And Blau sees her see him.** He stands behind the champion where he always stands, watching the crowd rather than the Prince, because watching the crowd is his whole job this week, and one woman in it has stopped breathing. He does not know who she is. He knows what that face means on anybody looking at this boy. By dusk he has a description of her, and from then on the watch on the Baron's ship is doubled and changes at no fixed hour. Her night aboard the ship tomorrow is harder for it, and should feel it.

If Gardwen is not there, somebody else may recognise the boy anyway: Sana, who notices things; or anybody who looks at a boy too thin for the armour he is in and wonders. This is the last moment before the bout when anything can be arranged calmly. After this there are hours, and after the final there are minutes. What Gardwen will do, if nobody stops her, is go aboard that ship to talk to him.

## Dinner at the Vantt Estate

<!-- covers: alderic-vantt-jokes-about-his-cellar -->

That evening, the Baron dines with his host at the Vantt Estate, two miles east of the walls. Alderic Vantt is fifty-two, warm, well fed and genuinely good company: there is drink, chairs, somebody's coat taken, and your name remembered aloud. He moves a conversation the way a man closes a door, so smoothly you only notice you are in the corridor. He is the channel the Baron's money runs through, and he has not said out loud, even at home, how frightened he is. **The tell is two self-deprecating jokes in a row.** A character who notices it (Insight, Hard) can push there, and he will talk about his debts before he talks about anything else.

Watching the house is worth as much as anything said inside it: who arrives, in what order, and who leaves early. The Baron arrives last and leaves first. Tat Atarer does not come at all.

## The Canal-Rat at the Camp

<!-- covers: sinnit-sells-the-face -->

If Sinnit has taken his three steps (cornering the wrong man, seeing the real one walk free, and spreading his lie), and Randall has not apologised to him, then tonight he does the worst thing in his life without understanding that it is.

He has worked it out slowly, the way he works everything out. A man in the Lullwater with Randall's face who did not know Randall's story. Randall himself walking the Pearl free the next day. And the young lord on the Pearl street with a bandaged hand, shouting fire, wearing the same face as both of them. That makes three, and a man who knows there are three of something has something to sell. He goes east to the Vantt camp in a borrowed boat and asks for the albino. He is kept waiting an hour in the wet. Then Blau comes out and listens without a word while Sinnit tells it, badly, twice, with the grievance in it: a thief called Randall, off the Pearl, and another one, a gentleman with a ring, both with his lordship's face. Blau pays him, more than he expected, and asks one question: where the thief sleeps.

**The Baron knows.** Blau goes to the ship that night, and from that moment the hunt for the sons the Baron did not know he had never stops. By dusk tomorrow there is a price on the face, in the same two days that one of the twins is meant to be on the Baron's ship in the dark. What Sinnit wanted was Randall hurt, and he will get that. What he did not think about is what the Baron does with sons. When he finds out, it is the first thing that has ever frightened him more than being laughed at.

**If Randall apologised to him** (out loud, in front of people who matter to Sinnit), none of this happens. It is the clearest thing the apology buys.

## A Boat in the Dark

<!-- covers: a-courier-from-the-movement -->

Late that night, a quiet boat comes alongside the Baron's ship with a sealed packet, and the Baron is reminded that he is not the top of this pyramid. The courier carries instructions about the champion, and a line of credit that explains where the tournament money came from.

This is offscreen unless the characters are watching the ship or intercepting messages, and catching it is their only chance this week to learn the shape of what stands behind the Baron. The packet names nobody who is in Purewater, and it makes one thing plain: killing the Baron does not end this. The Movement is patient. A failure here costs it a season, not a life's work.
