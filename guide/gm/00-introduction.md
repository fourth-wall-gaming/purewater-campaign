# And Then the Dragons Came

Three days before the Lake Lady's Tourney, a baron rides into Purewater with twenty-five mercenaries, a great deal of borrowed money and a covered litter that nobody is allowed to walk beside. In the litter is his champion, whom nobody outside his household has ever seen. The city has already decided not to notice what his men do on its bridges. Four strangers arrive the same day without knowing about one another: a wild elf following a ten-year-old trail, an old woman following a dream about her own death, a thief who has just been called *my lord* by a terrified guard, and an investigator who has just been called *Randall* by a man who wanted an apology.

By the first night, one of them has watched a young man with a burnt hand run down the back stair of a pleasure house. By the second morning, two of them are tied to chairs in a cellar under the city, looking at each other's faces. By the fifth evening, the Crown's own nephew has walked into a small stone room behind the Governor's box, and whether something else walks out wearing him depends entirely on what the characters did in between.

*And Then the Dragons Came* is a scenario for *Classic Fantasy Imperative*, built for four characters and around five in-game days. It is an investigation that turns into a heist that turns into a siege, set in a city where everyone wants something, nobody can arrest a lord, and the most dangerous thing a character can do is be seen.

## What Kind of Story This Is

Purewater is a living city. Every person in this book wants something this week and is working at it, whether or not the characters are watching. The Baron's plan runs on a timetable. So does his son's appetite, a madame's anger, a dockers' leader's patience and a canal-rat's grudge. If the characters spend a day drinking in the Lullwater, those things still happen, and the characters hear about them afterwards as rumours, bodies and shut doors. The scenario is not a corridor of encounters. It is a week, and the characters are the only thing in it that is not already decided.

That makes it a game about **leverage**, not combat. The characters cannot fight twenty-five armoured men, and nobody in Purewater has the authority to arrest a baron. What they can do is find the one person with the standing to act and give them a reason to, steal the one object the Baron cannot do without, and be standing in the right place when the city decides it has had enough. A party that thinks about water, paper and pride will beat a party that thinks about swords.

It is also, underneath, a story about family. Two brothers who have never met. A sister who has followed the men who took her brother for ten years. A father who does not know he has three sons, and would not love any of them if he did. Lean into that. The scenes players remember are the cellar where the twins first see each other, and the night Gardwen goes aboard the Baron's ship and hears her brother's voice say something that is not her brother.

## How This Book Is Arranged

The book has three parts and two appendices.

**Background** tells you the secret first. It explains what the Baron is really doing, then the city, the occupation, the three sons and the law. Read it before anything else. Once you know what is in the litter, everything else in the book will make sense as you read it.

**People and Places in Purewater** is the city and everybody in it: each island and the buildings that matter, with maps, and then the people, grouped the way the city groups them, with how to play them and what each of them is doing this week.

**Events and Story Structure** is the week itself, day by day: what happens if nobody interferes, how to run it when the characters are in the room, where the story bends when they push on it, and the three ways it can end.

**The Week at a Glance** is the one-page timetable and the clocks, for the table. **Statistics** holds a full *Mythras* stat block for every person in the book.

A separate **Players' Guide** holds everything the players should see: the city as its people know it, the four characters with their sheets, and a primer for players new to *Mythras*. Point your players at it before the first session. Nothing in it spoils anything.

## What You Need

The *Classic Fantasy Imperative* rules, which are free; this book; and four players, or fewer. The scenario is written for the four pregenerated characters described in *The Four Strangers*, because each of them is wired into the plot by somebody else's mistake. Players who roll their own characters can still play it. Advice on bringing them in is in the same chapter. Any of the four the players do not choose stays in the world as a Non-Player Character, and the party meets them in the cellar on the second morning.

## A Word on Tone

Purewater is a beautiful city that has quietly agreed to look the other way, and the horror in this scenario comes from how ordinary that is. Nobody is dead when the characters arrive. Everybody expects somebody to be. Play the Dragon Knights as weather, not monsters: three men eating in the middle of a bridge while a whole morning's traffic edges round them against the parapet, and a watchman at the far end doing nothing. Play the Baron as the nicest man in any room. Play the city's powerful people as decent, frightened, bought or wrong, and rarely as wicked. Then let the characters be the first people in nine days to do something about it, and let that feel enormous, because it is.

There is a seventeen-year-old boy at the heart of this, who has been somewhere nobody should be for ten years. Let him matter.
