# The City on the Water

Purewater is built across the Mouth, the narrow strait where a great sacred lake to the north drains into the open sea to the south. It is rich, strategic and rotten, and its own people call it Pisswater, for the smell of the canals and for the smell of its politics.

## The City Has No Ground

Purewater is built on water and does not apologise for it. Houses come straight up out of the channels with no bank and no shore, and their doors open onto steps that go down under the surface and keep going. Between the buildings there are ledges a body or two wide with no rail, and bridges, and water.

**Everything that matters goes by boat**, poled, rowed and shouted about. The rest goes over a bridge or along a ledge, and every single journey costs a fare. Two things follow, and both will come up constantly:

- **Anybody raised here can handle a boat.** It is like walking; nobody is praised for it. The half of that nobody ever says out loud is that **outsiders cannot**, which means a stranger in Purewater cannot move a sack of flour across the city without hiring somebody born here.
- **There are no horses.** The land stops at Caravan Square on the western shore and does not resume. A column coming down the road reaches the gate and has to be taken to pieces and put into boats, in public, slowly, one crate at a time.

And one thing everybody knows: every local can swim. Not every visitor can.

## Getting About

Three facts shape every journey in the city.

**No bridge crosses the Grand Canal.** The city's two halves, the North Quarter and the Merchant's Quarter, meet only by ferry: the Palazzo Ferry and the Guild Ferry.

**No bridge reaches the Lullwater or Temple Isle.** The Lullwater is reached by boat from Shilling Stairs, at a shilling a crossing, and somebody always sees who paid. Temple Isle is reached only by the temple's own ferries, on purpose, so that the profane city cannot simply walk onto holy ground.

**The Triumph Bridge is the only way in by land,** from Caravan Square through the Land Gate.

The maps below show each part of the city with a numbered key. Crosses mark places, discs mark taverns, shops and amenities, and squares mark posts of the city watch.

## High Isle

The heart of the city is High Isle, which is not one island but four, cut apart by the Grand Canal and its branches.

**The North Quarter** faces the open lake. Its western half is houses, rich toward the Pearl and middling eastward, and its eastern half is the official quarter: the Governor's Palazzo, where Prince Emeric governs in the King's name, the Hall of Justice and the Barracks around Palace Square. A wide avenue, the Processional, sweeps down through it. At its eastern end stands the walled Tournament Ground.

<!-- map: districts/grandcanal -->

**The Merchant's Quarter** lies south across the Grand Canal, where the Triumph Bridge brings the caravan trade ashore and the city's money is made. Its walled Market Square is ringed by the guildhalls of the Merchants, Coopers and Dyers, and the palazzi of the merchant princes who sit on the city council line the canal.

<!-- map: districts/merchants -->

**The Forges** are the industrial island: foundries, boatyards and tanneries, many of them dwarf-run, with the Forge Tower over the south shore and smoke over the water day and night. **The Seaward Quarter** runs along the south shore behind them, a comb of piers and slips with the round Harbour Master's Tower at the tip of the longest mole.

## The Pearl

North of High Isle, among the scattered Northern Isles, lies the Pearl, the pleasure island: lamplit and lovely over dark water, its houses stringing their lamps like pearls along Pearl Quay. The Siren's Call, the Sylph's Embrace, the Mother of Pearl, the Lily Garden and the Promise of Heaven are all here, and all of them answer to one woman, the madame Marisette.

Every child on the Pearl can tell you the story of **the Widow**. Two hundred years ago a merchant prince bought a fifteen-year-old girl outright, with a bill of sale witnessed by two councillors, and kept her behind a wall on a little islet. Every dusk for eleven years she lit a lamp in her window and talked to the water, and one calm night the water took him. With his money she bought the biggest house on the quay, opened its doors to women who had been sold as she had been, and made the rule every house on the island still keeps: **a night is for sale; a person is not.** No bill of sale for a woman is honoured on the Pearl, and no man who brings one is let in anywhere on the island again.

Every dusk, a doorman carries a flame from the Widow's lamp down the quay, and every house lights its lamps from it, and then the posts of the Lantern Bridge. The Pearl's women will tell you that nobody standing under that flame can be owned. Whether that is true is a matter of opinion.

## The Lullwater

Across open water to the north-east, reached by no bridge, lies the city's poor-and-criminal quarter: the narrowest, most crooked canals in Purewater, and tenements leaning over them until they almost touch. Its name sounds gentle, a lull, a still water, and that is the joke of it. Thieves, dredgers, smugglers and dockhands all live crammed into the same drowned maze. One straight canal, the Gutter, runs down its spine, and at its heart lies the Still, a round pool of dead water with the Moist Oyster tavern on its edge. The watch post here has its door nailed shut.

It is easy to get lost in the Lullwater, and dangerous to look lost.

## The Shoals

The seaward jaw of the Mouth: a low, ragged island of fish-markets, wharves, net-lofts, ice-houses and smugglers' jetties at the city's south-east corner. The Fishmarket Stair at the foot of the Fishmarket Bridge is the busiest water-stair in the city, and the Low Canals beyond it, four thousand people on plank walkways and stilt-houses, are the poorest. The dock labourers' guild, the Dredgers, are strongest here.

## The Tournament Ground and Temple Isle

At the east end of the North Quarter stands the walled Tournament Ground: a six-sided list of raked sand ringed by timber stands for four thousand people, with the Governor's box built into the western stand. The Long Butts, where the archery is shot, run along its north side.

Just north, across a narrow water and in plain sight of the stands, lies **Temple Isle**: the temple of the Lake Lady and the great round pool of the Sacred Confluence, where the purest lake-water wells up. Its broad water-steps face the Tournament Ground.

## Caravan Square

The city's only land approach, on the western shore: staging-yards, warehouses, a beast yard and a wagon park, ending at the Land Gate and the Triumph Bridge. Everybody who comes to Purewater by road comes through here, and most of them stand in it for an hour.
