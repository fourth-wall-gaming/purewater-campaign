# Chapter 6 — Two Powers

Marisette did accounts by lamplight at four in the morning and had been up since
the third bell.

Her counting room was small and warm and lined with ledgers and smelled of
coffee. She wore a plain grey wrap and no jewellery and looked like a woman who
had been doing sums for two hours and would rather still be doing them.

She poured three cups without asking whether anyone wanted them.

"Before you start. At about the third bell there was noise at your house,
Constantine. A woman screaming, and then not screaming, which is worse. My man
on the bridge saw your water door open and your skiff out twice — once with
three people in it and once with four, and one of the four was Ila, and she went
in the water, and she came out of it crying." Her hands were folded. "He came
and told me because he knows I like Ila.

"You've got twenty minutes before I have to be someone else. Tell me what's in
my island."

Magda told her. It took about six sentences and she left nothing out, including
the pigs.

Marisette did not move at all while she talked. At the end she turned one of the
coffee cups a quarter-turn on its saucer, and that was all that happened for
five seconds.

"Say the last part again."

"Hanzo di Teufel's son."

"Santo." Flat, like checking a number. "He came to my house three days ago
asking after Asphodel and I turned him away because I didn't like his hands." She
was completely still. "I sent him to *Constantine's*."

Constantine said her name.

"Don't."

---

She went through the cloak's contents like a woman counting stock. The ring she
barely glanced at. The letter she read twice. Then she came to the oiled silk,
and opened the first twist with her thumbnail, and the copper wire and the long
dark red hairs came out into the lamplight.

She stopped.

"Constantine. Who at your house has red hair?"

"Nobody."

"No. Nobody at mine either." She laid the hairs flat on the desk and separated
them with one fingernail, precisely, the way you lay out evidence. "There are
two women on this island with hair that colour, and I know them both, and
neither of them works. One of them is a Watercrafter's daughter. The other is
the High Priestess of the Lake Lady's own niece."

---

Then Magda nodded at Gardwen, and Gardwen told hers.

She told it the way you give a report, with no shape to it, which is the worst
way to hear it. Nine of them at the steading. She was eleven. They fired the
house and killed everyone in it and she lived because she was small and the
woods took her. In the morning she went back and counted them, and counted them
all, and her brother was not there. He was seven. They had taken him alive and
taken only him out of nine people and burned the rest so nobody would count.

"That boy in the canal said his father would *have to see*. That means his
father makes things too. And his father has had my brother for ten years."

Nobody said anything.

Marisette sat back.

"A litter," she said slowly. "Curtains sewn shut. Guards facing inward."

"Aye."

"He entered a champion for the Single Combat. Nobody has seen the man. It's been
the joke of the week." Her fingers had gone still. "How old would your brother
be now?"

"Seventeen."

"Then it isn't him. Santo was twenty-two." She let it sit exactly long enough.
"But a champion nobody has seen is not a champion. It's a *thing being kept*.
Men who are proud of a swordsman show him off. You don't face inward to keep
people out. You face inward to keep something in."

She stood.

"Which makes it my island's business, and I don't have twenty minutes any more."

---

Temple Isle after dark was a different city. The canals ran quiet and the water
in them was clear — you could see the bottom by lamplight, which you could not
do anywhere else in Purewater.

The temple sat low and wide with its foundations in the water, all open
colonnades and no doors, and the whole building was full of the sound of water
moving through it on purpose.

The High Priestess was sitting on the step of the great tank with her feet in
the channel and her sleeves pushed up, doing nothing at all.

"Leave the wolf," she said. "It can drink. Nothing in this water will mind."

Then, with no ceremony left in it: "Marisette does not send people to me. Ever.
So say the worst of it first and we'll work backwards."

"That'd be when I killed Hanzo's evil little shit of a son after he bound a
demon into one of the girls at the Sylph's Embrace. And then fed his body to
pigs."

Nerissa looked at her for a while.

"You smiled," she said, "when you said *pigs*."

"Aye."

"Mm." She took her feet out of the tank and dried them on the hem of her robe
like a farmer's wife. "Good. I'd not trust a person who could say that flatly."

---

She traded.

"Yesterday, four minutes after noon, this channel went *wrong*. Not fouled —
I've had fouled, fouled is a dead ox and a week of prayers. This went thin. The
Lady's presence in her own water went thin, the way a voice goes thin at a
distance, in the space of about ten heartbeats. I have served here since I was
nineteen and it has never once done that.

"So I rang the tower. And by the third peal there was a boy from the Governor's
household at my gate with a written instruction to cease, on the grounds that
the city was welcoming a distinguished guest."

"And you stopped."

"I stopped." Something flat and furious went across her face. "Because I had
nothing. A thin feeling in the water and no cause I could name in front of a
magistrate. If I had rung on I would have been removed by Thursday and replaced
by somebody who wouldn't ring at all."

She came and sat closer.

"Say the part about my niece again. Slowly."

---

Magda laid it out on the low table by the lamp the way you show someone a bad
joint of meat. The cloak. The tailor's tower. The ring. The letter dried against
a lamp glass. Eleven border coins.

Then the oiled silk. Five. Four still full.

"So he came into this city carrying five women in his coat," Nerissa said. "And
he spent one of them on a girl at Constantine's, and he had four left."

She picked up the first twist and did not open it. Her thumb moved once across
the silk.

Then: "Beneath the wire. Is there a knot in it, or is it cut clean at both
ends?"

Magda turned it. The wire was at one end. The other was blunt and square, every
strand the same length.

"Cut clean."

"Then it was taken with shears, off a head, by somebody standing behind her."
Nerissa laid it down very carefully. "It was not gathered off a comb. Somebody
got close enough to my sister's daughter to put shears in her hair, and she did
not scream, and she has not told me."

She looked up.

"Her name is Sana. She's nineteen. She has been at the school on the Northern
Isles for six weeks and I have not seen her in three."
