# Chapter 10 — The Latch

The first rite failed.

They laid the two drowned men on the stone at the head of the swept stair and
pulled the sheet back off their faces, because you do not say words over a
covered face. Ellick Sarn, and the sandy one whose name nobody had asked.

Magda knelt on wet stone and said it.

*Mother of the darkness. Take these drowned back to their rest. It's time they
went home.*

Nothing happened. The water stayed cold. The lamps went on burning. She waited,
because she had stood at enough gravesides to know the words sometimes take a
minute.

They did not.

Ravella crouched on the other side of the bodies and looked at her across them,
and was not angry, which was worse.

"It's not the words. The words were right. You said *take them*. You're asking
her to come and fetch. She won't. She never does — that's the entire nature of
your goddess. She takes the ones nobody sings about, and she takes them when
somebody finally *brings* them."

She laid two fingers on Ellick's forehead.

"Nobody has come back for these two either. Ellick's wife thinks he ran off. So
do the part that costs you. Not ask her to take them. **Give** them to her. Say
whose they are and say what was done to them and say that somebody came back —
and mean the last one, because you're the somebody."

---

Magda muttered that she was not a priest, and then did it anyway.

*Oi. Listen up then.*

She named them as what they were. Brothers, workers, toilers, men. She said the
true thing about a life, which is that it is made of imperfect plans and a
little hope and a little selfishness and probably a great deal of love, and that
they had died here, in the water, the water that takes all things.

Then the part that cost.

*But then these bastards brought you back. To haul, and work, and toil. Because
it helped them.*

*So fuck 'em.*

She was shouting at a hole in the ground.

*Come with me, brothers. Rage with me at the loss and the death — and then go
back to the dark deep sea. Go to your rest. Go now, my brothers. Go.*

---

The water did not part or glow.

It went warm. That was all. It came up the steps past her knees like a draught
from an open door in a cold house, and every lamp on that island bent toward it
at once.

And the two men on the stone let go. There was no light. There was a slackening
— she had seen it four hundred times in byres and beds and would know it
anywhere. Whatever had still been holding the shape of Ellick Sarn's face
stopped holding it, and what was left was a boatman who had died, and looked it,
and looked finished.

Ravella had both hands flat on the stone and her head down and stayed like that
a long time. When she got up her face was wet and it was not canal water.

"They're through. The Court has them."

She looked at Magda.

"Nobody has said whose anybody was down here in four hundred years."

---

The quiet one came in an hour later with one set of oars and no lantern, and
landed exactly where Ellick had landed, and took three tries to get the thing
out of the boat.

Oiled cloth, roped, five feet long. He carried it up the drowned street on his
shoulder and set it down on the stone at the foot of the stair — gently, the way
you set down someone sleeping — and stood there breathing.

Then he said, to nobody, and it carried beautifully across still water:

"**Ellick. Get up here and help me with him.**"

Twelve Swords of the Lake came over the wall and he was face-down on the stone
before the echo finished. He did not struggle and he did not shout. He turned
his head sideways against the stone and looked at the lamps opening one by one,
and at the priestess, and then at the oiled bundle eighteen inches from his
face, which he had set down so carefully and was not going to be allowed to
touch again.

He answered three questions and they were all the wrong ones. Yes, that was
Thuban Eta in the cloth. Yes, the removal would work at this stair. No, he would
not tell them how.

Then he settled his shoulders against the wall and stopped.

To Hesper, unprompted, almost kindly: "You'll open him at the wrong point and
put a thousand-year-old knight into a priestess. I'd rather like to see that."

---

They cut the wire off the wrists.

Hesper undid the reliquary knots one at a time, naming each, and the oiled cloth
came back on long bones brown with age, laid the way you lay a man — and around
the wrists, corroded almost to nothing but still there after a thousand years,
two turns of iron wire.

They had put him in the water tied and nobody had ever taken it off.

Nerissa cut it and dropped it in the water, and the temperature of the air on
that stair changed.

---

Before that, Magda had gone to the boy and told him what she meant to do: strip
the mail off and go at him bare-handed and beat the general out of him.

Thuban Eta said no.

"But I understand why you thought of it, and I would have taken that offer from
you gladly, if it worked." He shifted, and the boy's ruined chest moved, and he
winced on the boy's behalf. "You would beat me out of a child. I do not feel
your hands, woman. *He* does. They have been cutting him open every morning for
ten years to do exactly what you described. If pain could shift me I would have
been shifted four hundred times."

He let that sit.

"They did not fasten me in with iron. They fastened me in with the night I died.
Every hour I have spent in here I have spent back on that boat with my hands
tied, going over the side, looking up at Corran and Vey and the rest of them not
doing anything. That is the room I have been kept in.

"And I cannot leave it, because to leave it I have to accept that it happened
and that nobody came, and I have not been able to accept that, because it is
unbearable, and I have had a thousand years of finding it unbearable."

He looked at her steadily.

"So you do not need to fight me out. You need to come and get me out of that
water. Say it was wrong. Say whose I am. Say somebody came.

"And you must mean it, and I will know if you don't, because I have had a very
long time to learn what people mean."

Then, quieter:

"Open my bones and open your door. And then stand where they stood, and do not
look away when I go under, because they did.

"That is all it ever needed. It has only ever needed one person to turn up."

---

So she said the name and told the tale and stood where his company had stood,
and named the murder out loud in front of twelve Swords and a priestess and a
mage and a druid and a wolf and a whole Court listening from underneath.

She put Vell's axe in the bones' hands, because his own had gone into the water
with him and hers was better anyway.

It was the first telling he had ever had.

And it was not enough.

She felt it not land. It was like putting a shoulder to a door that turns out to
be a wall. The water stayed cold. The lamps did not lean.

The boy started to shake and the old voice came out ragged for the first time.

"—I can't reach it, woman, there's something across—"

And against the wall the roped man began to laugh. Quiet, delighted, entirely
genuine.

"Oh, that's *good*. You've done all of it properly and you've done it
beautifully and you have forgotten one thing. He isn't only held by the bones."

He tipped his head back, thoroughly enjoying himself.

"**The Baron wears the rest of him around his neck.** And the Baron is at the
Governor's table three miles from here, being toasted."

---

Hesper had it a moment later.

"The bones are the primary anchor. They're where the weight of him sits. But the
locket is the working one — the piece they carry, the one the daily ritual runs
through. It's not a spare. It's the *latch*. They cut a piece off him a century
ago and put it in that locket, and every morning they use it to hold the seat
shut."

He looked up.

"The door is open. The bones are open. He is willing — you did that, and it
worked, and I want you to hear that it worked. But there is a lock on this and
it is around Hanzo di Teufel's neck."

"The Tourney is at noon," Nerissa said.

---

Magda did not join the planning.

She stood at the edge of it with the axe hanging from one hand and watched the
roped man, and he knew she was watching, and after a while he opened his eyes
and looked back, and the two of them held that while the others worked out how
to get into a Governor's box.

Then she walked over and did it.

It took one.

She found a grain sack by the stair and tipped out what was in it and put the
head in and knotted the top.

"Might come in handy," she said, to nobody in particular.

Corvin walked twenty feet away and was sick against a wall. Hesper closed his
eyes for a moment and then went back to the bones, and did not look at her again
for some time. Ravella said quietly to one of hers: *not that one. He doesn't go
down there.*

Gardwen did not ask why. She asked: "Was he still worth anything?"

---

Nerissa came and stood in front of her, and her voice did not rise, and every
Sword on that island could hear her.

"He was bound. On my island, under my cordon, taken by my people who had orders
to hold him. And he was the only living soul who knew where Bettan is. Fourteen
years old. Cheerful. Cutting girls' hair at a school on the Northern Isles for
people who fill them up."

Her hands were shaking slightly and she did nothing to hide it.

"I gave you a reason to stop, once, and you took it. So I will not pretend I did
not know what you were. But you have just taken that child away from me and I do
not know how to get her back."

Magda said the rot was in her city and it would not leave because she asked it
nicely.

Nerissa did not soften.

"You've mistaken me. I did not object because it was unclean. I have buried men
off the tide-flats with their throats opened by people I then had to bless at
Solstice. I objected because you cut up the only map to a fourteen-year-old girl
and then said *might come in handy*.

"That isn't purity, Magda. That's arithmetic."

She went to the boats. At the last she turned.

"I will work with you until this is finished, and I will do it wholeheartedly,
and I will not pretend at you afterwards. When the boy is out and the Baron is
down, you and I are done, and you will not be welcome on Temple Isle."

A beat.

"Sword-Captain Vell's axe stays with you. She'd have wanted that and I'm not
petty."
