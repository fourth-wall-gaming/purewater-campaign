# Chapter 9 — The Mouth

Nobody paid for any of it with money.

Crowbill wanted the cargo. He was wiry and forgettable but for a small crow
inked on the back of his right hand, and he ate bread and cheese off a paper
while Magda talked and did not stop.

"Every day since it tied up. That's not a boast, that's my trade." He wiped his
fingers. "And here's why it's still sat there with everything on it. Ten men in
scale and one way up. It's a ship, so if it goes badly they don't have to win,
they just have to cast off. And a Baron. A man with a border tower and three
hundred men and a long memory."

He sat back.

"So tell me the two things I need. When is she sailing, and what do you want off
her that isn't cargo?"

"Tonight. And the knight who's supposed to be competing in the tourney."

He put the bread down and was quiet a long time, and then went and stood at the
window that looked at a wall.

"I've a rule," he said to the wall. "I've a few. Most of them are about money
and one of them isn't, and the one that isn't is why I've still got a city to
work in. Children aren't stock. You don't sell them, you don't use them, and you
don't let anyone do it on my water and call it business."

He came back but did not sit.

"Everything on that ship that isn't the boy is mine. My name is nowhere. And
when it's done, the High Priestess of the Lake Lady stands up and says publicly
that the canal folk kept her water clean. Not me. Not names. *The canal folk.*
Because I've four hundred people who get moved on and blamed and dredged out of
the channel, and one sentence from that woman is worth more to them than a
Baron's whole hold."

Then, briskly: "And how do you propose we get past ten men in scale on a deck?"

"You're the river men. I'm just a pig farmer." Magda shrugged. "But she has to
leave the pier to sail. Once she's off it she's not got four men at the bollards
and four at a ramp. She's a hull in the dark with a channel round her, and the
channel's yours."

Crowbill's face became interested.

---

Ravella's price was two dead men.

She was half of one and half of the city's, and she poured with faintly webbed
fingers and did not hide them, and her eyes held the canal light a half-second
too long.

"There are people living in the deep channels of this city who were here before
the causeway. And for five days there has been a hot wound in the eastern
channel that pushes ahead of the tide, and every one of them has had to move —
old, young, sick. Two haven't managed it."

She turned her cup.

"And they came down our stairs. Four days ago. Three men with a lantern, digging
out a door that has been shut and unmarked since before this city had a name.
And they took things off it — the small confused ones that sit near the
threshold and don't go through, which are ours to look after and nobody else's."

"How many of the taken have you seen?"

"Two."

"Then give them back. That's the whole price." She looked at Magda very
directly. "You should understand what you're agreeing to. There isn't a version
of this where a boatman walks home to his supper. He said yes to a warm coat in
a cold winter and he has been paying for it ever since, and the kindest end
available to him is the right one. You'd be taking him to that stair and
standing there while it's done."

She turned the cup again.

"And I understand that's your trade."

---

The Mouth was a mile of slow black water where the harbour let out to sea.

Six punts, no lights, oars muffled in somebody's shawl. Twelve canal men in dark
wool, barefoot, each with a short blade and a coil of line and a grapnel wrapped
in sacking so it could not ring. None of them asked what was on the ship.

Ravella sat in the bow of Magda's punt with four of hers in the water alongside,
invisible except that now and then the surface did something a surface should
not.

A mile back, a lantern went up at the pier end and came down. Then another.

She came down the channel under a scrap of canvas and the sweeps, unlit but for
one shaded lamp at the stern, and she was enormous close-to, and the hiss of her
waterline carried flat across the surface.

"When I go in, that water will kill anyone who isn't mine for about a minute
while it turns," Ravella said. "After that it's cold and it's climbable and I
can hold it as long as I'm conscious. So say when, and be sure. I only get to do
this once tonight."

At eighty yards Magda said *now*.

---

The white line along the hull stopped. Forty feet of hissing steam ceased,
mid-length, like a candle pinched out, and the slick behind it went dark and
ordinary and cold.

Six punts moved at once. Grapnels landed with a sound like a fist in a pillow.
Canal men went up the side of a moving ship barefoot with knives in their teeth.

But a ship that has hissed for five days and suddenly does not has changed its
noise, and there was a man aft who had lived beside that sound long enough to
notice its absence.

A voice on the quarterdeck. Then a shout. Then a bell.

---

Magda did not go up. She was seventy and in mail and could not swim, and what
she was instead was a woman with a sling and a clear line at forty feet of rail
with the sky behind it.

The first man leaned out with a knife for the grapnel lines and she put a stone
past his head close enough to make him flinch, and that bought three seconds.

The second one took him under the raised arm where the scale gapes, side-on, and
went in. He folded over the rail with his arms hanging and his legs still on the
deck, which was worse for the men behind him than if he had gone into the water,
because now the rail was blocked at exactly the place they were massing.

Then the bell stopped, and the shouting stopped, and it went very quiet up there
very suddenly, which was the most frightening thing that had happened all night.

A lantern shutter opened on the quarterdeck and swung out over the side, and the
cone of light came along the hull, punt by punt.

Looking for the boats.

He was a man who did not wear armour and had apparently never expected to need
any. She put the stone through the middle of him before the light reached her,
and she heard it hit at forty yards — the flat sound of a stone going into a
body with nothing on it but wool. The lantern dropped out of a hand that had
stopped working and hissed out in the water.

Somewhere up there a man said something short with no breath behind it.

---

She went up the rope on the second try and with the rage coming on, and came
over the rail making a noise that stopped the fighting at the bow for a full
second. It was not a war cry. It was the sound you make over a byre full of dead
stock in a bad winter, and there were words in the middle of it about violators
and stealers of souls and rapists of the dead, and every canal man on that deck
decided at once that whatever it was, it was on their side.

Blau was on the quarterdeck steps on one knee with a hand pressed flat into his
own middle, directing men who were peeling back from the bow to form on him.

"That's her," he said. "The northern woman." A pause, and something that might
have been a laugh with no air behind it. "Nobody told me she was *seventy*."

He drew a working knife left-handed and could not make it work, because there
was a hole in his abdomen and every time he bent at the waist something inside
him refused. He came in short and slow and wide and had to put his other hand on
the deck.

Neither of them touched the other. For a second they were a foot apart on a wet
deck, both bleeding, both too old or too broken to finish it.

"Ah," he said.

And he looked past her at the barred door under the quarterdeck.

"*Cut the boy's throat.*"

The axe took him across the thigh and then, on the turn, into the hole she had
already made from a rowing boat forty yards out. He went flat. There was a
moment where he was looking at the mast, and then there was not anybody looking
at anything.

Behind her, Gardwen's entangle came down the length of the deck and took the
whole waist of the ship — cordage and weed and wet rope coming alive off the
pins — and caught the knight at the door with the beam half out of its brackets.

---

The cabin was small and low and hot, and the air in it was scorched hair and
something sweetish under it. Chalk on the boards, redrawn daily. A cot bolted
down, stripped to the canvas because the sheets had gone ashore that morning in
a laundress's bag.

The boy was seventeen and thin the way people are thin when someone else decides
what they eat, and the whole upper left of his chest was a long-healed,
re-opened, re-healed mess.

He looked straight at her, and there were two people looking out of his face.

"*Don't*—" said a boy's voice.

"—**she's not here for you**," finished a voice a thousand years older.

Then, to Magda:

"Woman of the death-goddess. I have been listening to you come up this ship for
a quarter of an hour, and I know who sent you, and I have been waiting for
somebody to come with that particular smell on them for a very long time."

He got an elbow under himself.

"Get us off this ship before the other one comes back with my bones."
