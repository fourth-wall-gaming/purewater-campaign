# Chapter 7 — Lost Isle

They had sent a boat at dusk and it had never arrived.

The mistress of the school opened the door at one in the morning and had been
crying. Sana had been gone since the previous morning. They had every girl out
looking and they had dragged the channel.

And across four hundred yards of black water, on the drowned ruin the boatmen
gave a wide berth, there was a light moving. Dull red, low, close to the
waterline. It went in a straight line for a bit and stopped and after a while
moved again.

Gardwen went down on one knee at the channel edge and put both hands flat in the
water.

Nothing came. She sat back on her heels breathing like she had run there.

"Too far. It's not the distance. The water's all hers here and I'm not hers, and
I was asking a favour in someone else's house."

Nerissa was already unpinning her sleeves.

"You reached. From the bank. Across running water that isn't yours." She stepped
down into the channel to her knees and held out her hand. "So come inside and
ask properly."

The second time was not like the first.

Gardwen's fingers locked on hers hard enough to hurt and her breath went out of
her all at once, and Nerissa stood in the water and took it the way you hold a
rope.

"The whole island's humming. Underneath. Not evil, just old." Her eyes were
shut. "Above that, on the stone, there's a working. Same hand as the chalk on
her floor. It's not finished. It's laid out, like a table before dinner. And
it's cold — whoever set it hasn't come back to it in a day."

"Living things."

"Two moving. Both of them wrong. Not demons — men, but wrong, the way milk's
wrong." Her head turned, tracking. "They're low down and they're together and
they've gone still since the light went out."

She stopped.

"There's a third. Not moving. Not wrong." Her voice cracked. "She's small in it.
Gone right down small the way you do when you're hiding. She's above the other
two. Up high, in the stone."

"Is she alive?"

"She's *frightened*," Gardwen said. "You can't be frightened if you're not
alive."

---

Lost Isle was a street.

That was the thing. You could see the line of it — doorways and window-holes at
chest height, a gutter, the stub of a wellhead — and all of it standing in four
feet of black water with weed growing out of the windows. They waded up somebody's
road with the water at their thighs. Nerissa touched a doorframe as she passed,
briefly, the way you touch a coffin.

The tower-stub came up out of the weed eighty feet on. Three storeys, one corner
gone, the bottom ten feet drowned.

Gardwen put a hand flat on the stone and called up it, pitched to carry no
further.

"Sana. Your aunt sent me. She's stood in the water at the corner and she's got
her mace and she is extremely angry."

Nothing. Then, three floors up, small and hoarse:

"*Say what she calls my mother.*"

"I don't know," Magda said. "But I know you sent her an eleven page letter about
a heron. The three line one raised an alarm, and that's why we're here."

A long silence. Stone grit came down and landed in the water.

"I don't— I don't remember writing eleven pages."

"You did. She's told it twice tonight and she's not a woman who repeats
herself."

She came down slowly, stopping twice to breathe, and Gardwen went up the
half-stair and brought her the last of it. Nineteen years old, barefoot in a
school shift, her hair chopped ragged on the left side of her head.

She gripped Gardwen's forearm and would not let go.

"There were three of them yesterday. Only two since this morning — the other
went off in the boat before dawn and hasn't come back." She was shaking. "They
didn't come for me. They came for the *place*. I only ran because I saw. They
had a girl from the school with them and she was walking with them like she knew
them."

She looked between them.

"Bettan. She's fourteen. She's the one they sent to the temple last night with
the message."

---

They were sitting side by side on the edge of a slab in the dark, in a room with
half a roof and no wall on the water side, and they turned their heads at the
same moment. Not together like men who heard the same thing. At once, the same
speed, like two doors on one hinge.

"You're not her," one said.

"—she was meant to bring the girl," the other finished.

Neither stood up.

The sling took the sandy one high on the right arm and she heard the bone go
from twenty feet. The arm dropped. He did not make a sound and did not look at
it.

They stood up together, the same speed, as if the slab had tipped them.

"That's going to make the poling difficult," he said, with his arm hanging off
him.

"We'll manage."

---

It went on longer than it should have.

Magda broke the sandy one's arm at the elbow with the staff and he straightened
up and inspected the ruin of it with interest, the way you'd look at a torn
coat. Neither of them bled. Ruhi took the other under the water and he did not
struggle for air because he did not need any, and got a hand into her ruff and
worked it deeper while she thrashed.

Gardwen's Hold Person took the sandy one mid-step and he froze with his weight
forward and his eyes still moving.

"It shouldn't hold," she said, white. "Magda — that's *Hold Person*. There
shouldn't be enough person in him to grip."

The other one caught Magda's forearm in the shallows and closed on it like a
piece of machinery, no squeeze and no threat, just a closing to a fixed position
and then no further movement at all. He called to nobody in particular that he
had this one, and the frozen man said *good*, and Ruhi took his legs from under
him and all three went under together.

He did not try to hold her down. He lay on the bottom of a drowned street
holding on, waiting, because he did not need to breathe and had all night.

Gardwen put three words into the water and his hand opened, all four fingers at
once, like a catch releasing.

He sat up in the water and looked at his own palms.

"That wasn't me," he said, with something like interest.

---

Afterwards Magda killed him with the staff while he was standing with his hands
open, and Nerissa was four feet away and did not move for a moment.

"He had stopped," she said. Not accusing. Reading a figure back to check she had
it. "He had stopped, and he was showing me the stone."

And what he had been showing her was not a slab.

It was the top of a stair. Four steps of cut stone going down at an angle into
black water that did not hold the lamplight, with the weed hacked off the edges
and the treads swept clean where there should have been a foot of silt.

"It's a door," Nerissa said, on her knees with both palms on the carving. "Not
for people. For the drowned."

Her fingers went under the surface and kept going.

"There are stories about the Northern Isles going under. A slow flood, over
generations, and everything in them that died went down with the streets. And
whoever cut this cut it so that what stayed in the water had somewhere to go. A
way out." She looked up. "You lay them to rest through a thing like this."

Behind them, roped and sitting in the shallows, the sandy one said pleasantly:

"That's what he went to fetch the boat for. You'd best hurry — he's been gone
since before dawn and he'll not be coming back alone."
