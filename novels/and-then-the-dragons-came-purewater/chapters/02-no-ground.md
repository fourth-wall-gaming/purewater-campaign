# Chapter 2 — No Ground

They came into the city at midday and did not see the column arrive.

The column came through the water-gate into Caravan Square at the same hour:
twenty-odd Dragon Knights, banners cased, and a covered litter in the middle
with the curtains laced shut and the escort facing inward as much as out. The
Baron rode uncovered and let the city look at him. A knight at his stirrup got
a clear look at a thief's face in the crowd and went white to the lips, and
said nothing, and by evening the whole quarter was talking about a litter that
had to be watched.

Magda and Gardwen were two miles out on the causeway and heard none of it.

---

The water-gate had a chain-boom and a customs man in Harbor Masters' green with
ink on his fingers and a tally-board under his arm. He had turned two people
back that morning.

Magda went up the line past the fishmonger and the family with the goat and
stood at his elbow with the wolf at her knee.

He turned with his mouth already open.

She looked at him.

She had buried people. Not in wars. In beds. She had put her hand over the eyes
of men who had been shouting an hour before and then gone out and fed the pigs,
because the pigs do not care. She let him have all of it.

"We're going in."

He looked at the staff, which had never once pretended to be a walking stick.
He looked at the wet grey animal. He looked back at her face and did not find
anything there that was negotiating.

"There's a fee," he said. There was no starch in it.

"There is."

She did not fill the silence. He filled it himself by looking down at his
tally-board, which was a good board and had a lot of him in it.

"Two on foot," he said to it. "And a *dog*." He wrote *dog*. He underlined it.
"Muzzled inside the Merchant's Quarter, that's not me, that's the Guild, and if
it goes for anyone it's your neck not mine—"

"Understood."

"—and I never saw it."

"You never did."

He stepped aside, small, so it would not look like stepping aside. As she
passed under the boom he said, to nobody:

"Everyone's mad this week. It's the Tourney." Then, because he could not help
it: "Third lot today gone through with something they won't show me."

---

Gardwen stopped six feet inside the gate with her back to a wall.

The noise came up off the water and off the stone at once and hit from
underneath. The smell was salt and tar and hot oil and under it the sweetish
note of a city sitting in its own water.

"There's no *ground*," she said.

"Yeah. It's all boats and barges and slipways here, lass." Magda took her arm.
"Let's get to the Pearl."

She learned it the way woodland people learn things, badly for ten minutes and
then all at once. By the third bridge she had stopped looking at her feet. By
the fifth she was reading the water — which boat had right of way, which shout
meant *move*. The wolf hated all of it and did it anyway, low and close and
wet.

Away to the left, over the roofs, a bell started. Then another, ragged, out of
time with the first.

They stopped before anyone could ask what they were for.

---

The Pearl was an island and the loudest quiet place in the city: swept steps,
lamps lit at three in the afternoon, music from an upper window, two women on a
balcony watching the street the way farmers watch weather.

The green door had a sylph carved on the lintel, worn smooth by two hundred
years of hands.

Magda knocked the way she had knocked twenty years before.

The woman who opened it was older than the knock remembered. Grey coming in at
the temple, an apron over good clothes, flour to the wrists.

"*Magda.*"

"Constantine."

"You're not dead." She said it with real pleasure, one professional to another.

She took in the rest of it in one pass — the young woman standing too straight,
the staff, the wolf — and only one eyebrow moved.

"Beds. It's Tourney week, I've got the city and half the border in my lap and
every room's spoken for—" She held the door wide. "So you'll have the top back
room over the water, and it's cold, and the shutter sticks. Bring the dog. If
it eats a client I'll take it out of your purse."

A girl of thirteen went past the foot of the stairs with an armful of linen
twice her size, saw the wolf, and dropped all of it.

"*Ila*," Constantine said, without heat.

She was not listening.

On the landing above, leaning on the rail with her hair down, an elven woman
with a cup in one hand looked down the stairwell and laughed.

"Constantine. You've brought home a wolf."

"I've brought home a friend, Emmeralda. The wolf is incidental."
