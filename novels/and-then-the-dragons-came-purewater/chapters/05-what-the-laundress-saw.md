# Chapter 5 — What the Laundress Saw

The ship steamed where it sat.

It lay at a private pier on the seaward side of High Isle, black-hulled and
dragon-prowed and moored bow-in so it could leave at any hour without turning.
Where the hull met the water the water boiled, in a continuous white sheet a
hundred feet long, and there was no fire under it. Gulls would not go near. There
was a dead-water slick round the stern with things in it floating belly-up.

Magda stood in the lee of a cooperage for the better part of an hour and watched
what a ship actually needs.

Almost nothing went aboard. That was the first thing. No chandler's boy, no
water butts. Everything came up under a covered gangway with the canvas laced
down, in the crews' own hands, from their own boat.

Things came off. Slops, twice, carried down in a covered pail by a knight and
tipped into the dead water where nobody was collecting it. Barrows of ash and
clinker, far more than a galley fire makes. And one bag of laundry, handed down
at the bollards to a woman with a barrow who was not allowed past.

Nobody who was not a Dragon Knight went past the bollards all afternoon.

She counted the men. Eight. Two at the pier head, two at the ramp, three working
on deck, one going up and down with the clinker. No banner at the masthead. No
household, no clerk, no valet, no Baron.

Amidships, well above the waterline, one shuttered port. Closed on a hot bright
day, and barred from the outside.

---

The hog-man's lad was called Pell and could not back a nag.

Magda went to the horse's head, and the horse laid its ears flat and swung its
quarters and put the offside wheel into the spoil heap, and a barrel went over.
They spent two minutes getting the wheel out and by the end they were both
filthy and he had stopped being embarrassed.

"She does that. Every time. Right at the end."

"She's testing whether you've noticed her. Go to her head *before* she starts,
not after."

He considered this with the deep suspicion of a fifteen-year-old being told
something obviously true.

He did the Forge Quarter yards on alternate days for a man called Renko out on
the Shoals, which Magda kept her face still about. He complained at length about
the rope-walk, who had put nails in their waste. Then he jerked his chin down
the pier at the steaming hull.

"Not that lot, though. *They* don't give us nothing."

"Tight bunch, are they?"

"They're *unnatural*, is what they are." He hauled the rope down with his whole
weight. "Every yard on this side gives us their waste. That's how it works. That
lot tip it in the water. In the *water*. Good scraps, meat and all. Renko went
down there himself first day and offered to take it for nothing, and they walked
him back up the pier by his elbows.

"Only one that gets anything off them is the laundry woman. Yannick's wife. Bag
down at the bollards, coin in her hand, off you go. She's not allowed past
neither." He shrugged. "She won't talk about it, though. Went right off, she
has. Which is a shame, 'cause she'll talk about anything."

Magda asked how big the bag was.

He stopped and thought about it, which he clearly had not done before.

"Not big. One bag. She could carry it on her hip." He frowned. "Every day,
though. That's the thing. Rope-walk's forty men and they send out twice a week.
That ship sends out every day and it's one bag. And it's all the same, she said
once. Not shirts and hose like a normal house. All the one thing."

---

The wash-house was behind the rope-walk. Three coppers on the boil, lines of
other people's linen the length of it, and the heat enormous.

Ann Yannick was alone in there with a wooden paddle.

Magda came in out of the sun, took the bench by the door where the draught was,
and sat down the way a woman of seventy sits down. Then she did not say
anything. She looked at her boots. She rubbed her knee. She said, once, to
nobody, that the steam was pleasant on the joints.

The paddle went on. Copper, lift, turn.

It took a long while.

"Do you know what boiled blood smells like?"

Magda said nothing.

"'Course you do. You've a face like you've buried people." She went on
paddling. "Four days I've had that. Every morning. One bag, and it's all sheets,
and every one of them's the same."

Copper. Lift. Turn.

"It's not a wound. I've done a house with a consumptive in it. I've done a
birth-bed. I've done a man who lost a hand at the rope-walk. You get a shape to
it — where it came from, how it lay, whether he thrashed." She looked up. "This
is the same shape every day. Same place on the sheet. Same size. Like it's been
let out of him careful, in the same spot, and mopped, and put back."

She jerked her chin at the end copper, on its own with the lid half over it.

"And there's something else in it that doesn't come out. Not blood. Blood boils
out with lye and time. That goes *grey*, and it smells like the forge, and my
hands were numb to the elbow the first day and I've worn gloves since."

She set the paddle down.

"They pay double. My man says take it. And I lie there at night thinking —
whoever's in that ship, he's still bleeding tomorrow. Four days. Somebody's
keeping him alive to do it again."

---

Magda tried sympathy and it did not work. Her face did not know how to be that,
and the joke she made about blood was the sort that is funny in a byre at four
in the morning and is not funny in a wash-house.

Ann Yannick's face closed like a door.

"You're not from the yards."

So Magda told her the truth instead.

"No. Not from here. I'm from the north, where the winds are cold and the ice is
sharp. I joke about blood because I've cleaned up my fair share. And I'm
interested because the men on that boat are up to some evil shit that might need
stopping."

The woman looked at her a long moment through the steam. Whatever she was
checking for, she did not find it.

"North," she said. "Aye. I thought there was something in the vowels."

She worked for a bit.

"Three things, then. And I'll deny every one of them if you ever say I said it.

"One. It's not a sick lad. Sick men sweat, and they piss the bed, and they stink
of the sickness. There's none of that. There's blood in the same place every day
and nothing else. Whoever's in that bed isn't ill and isn't dying and isn't
getting better. He's being kept.

"Two. Second morning the bag came heavier and there was a shirt in it. Not
linen — a fighting man's quilted under-shirt, for wearing under scale. The
arming-points were cut. Not untied. *Cut*, front and back, the way you do when
you're getting something off a man who isn't helping you.

"Three." She stopped paddling. "The knight who hands the bag down. Big fellow,
white eyebrows, no colour in him at all. Yesterday he asks me, all friendly,
whether I ever wash for the houses on the Pearl."

She looked straight at Magda.

"I said no. But he wasn't asking about laundry, was he."

---

Magda asked one more thing: which part of the boy they were bleeding.

Ann Yannick took a clean sheet off the stack and shook it out flat across the
bench and put her hand down on it.

"Sheet goes on the bed this way — hem to the head, you can tell from the wear
where a man's heels go. So. It's *here*." High, a little left of the middle.
"Every morning, four days. About the size of my two hands together. Upper body.
Left side. Chest, or just under the arm."

She folded it back up.

"Somebody opens the same place, the same way, every morning. And then somebody
else changes the bed."

She squared the stack off and stood there with both hands on it.

"You said choose sides."

She went to the end copper and lifted the lid and started fishing the load out
into a basket.

"They're due at the sixth bell for that. They'll get it. I'll smile at the
white-haired bastard and take his coin. But you tell Marisette that Ann Yannick
washes for that ship every morning, and she'll go on doing it, and she'll go on
*looking*."

Her jaw set.

"And if that boy's still bleeding on the day of the Tourney, I want somebody to
have done something about it."
