# ElevenLabs pack — *The Short Ledger*

Voice Design prompts, casting notes, and sound-effect prompts for an audiobook
of this manuscript. Paste each **Voice** block straight into Voice Design; each
one is self-contained and includes accent, age, timbre, pace and register.

Everything here is English of no particular county — this is a canal city, not
Venice and not London. Keep the whole cast on the same island.

---

## 1. The narrator — the most important casting decision in the book

**Voice.** A warm, unhurried British woman in her fifties, mid-to-low register,
slightly husky, with the relaxed authority of somebody telling you a long story
in a kitchen rather than reading to you. Educated but entirely unposh. Dry, fond,
faintly amused most of the time, and completely unembarrassed by profanity when
it arrives — she swears the way people actually swear, in passing, without
flagging it. Natural conversational pace with real variation: she slows right
down for the one-line paragraphs and rattles through the long accumulating
sentences without losing the thread. No narrator "performance" voice. No
breathiness. No hushed-thriller delivery.

**Why.** This narrator is not neutral. She likes these people and tells you what
they feel and why, and the book falls apart if she is read coolly. Every chapter
opens inside somebody's head — she is warmest in the first paragraph and driest
in the last line.

**Direction for the small-caps openers.** Every chapter begins with its first
clause in capitals running straight into lowercase. Do **not** shout it. Read it
as a slightly slower, slightly lower lead-in that settles into normal pace by
the end of the sentence.

**Direction for "A beat."** It is a stage instruction to the reader. Read the
two words flat and leave a full second of silence after them.

## 2. Principal cast

### Kag
**Voice.** A very large half-orc woman in her thirties. Deep contralto with real
weight behind it, unhurried, slightly gravelled, working-class and completely
unbothered about it. Speaks more slowly than everyone around her because she has
never once needed to hurry to hold a room. Dry to the point of deadpan; the
jokes are delivered exactly like the arithmetic. **Never** growls, never snarls,
never does anything monstrous — she is a woman who does sums, and the whole book
depends on her sounding like one.
**Under pressure:** the voice drops and the sentences get shorter and she starts
putting numbers on things. Colder, not louder.

### Crowbill
**Voice.** A wiry, forgettable man in his late fifties. Quiet, dry, pleasant,
unhurried to a degree that other people find uncomfortable. Mid register, no
edge on it at all, never raised — the menace is entirely in how reasonable he
sounds. Slight amusement always available. He never fills a silence.

### Marisette
**Voice.** A woman of indeterminate age with money in her vowels and none of the
strain of somebody performing it. Cool, gracious, beautifully paced, privately
entertained by nearly everything. Warm without being kind. She is the only
person in this book who sits while others stand and you should be able to hear
that. **Under pressure she becomes more hospitable, not less** — read her most
dangerous lines as her most charming.

### Blau (Commander Vask)
**Voice.** A thin, pale, burn-scarred man in his forties. Absolutely level —
no emphasis anywhere, no stress on the important word, every sentence delivered
at the same volume and the same speed whether he is confirming the tide or
telling you what will happen to a district. Slightly quiet, so listeners lean
in. Not sinister. Not cold. **Reasonable**, which is worse. He never hurries and
he never repeats himself.

### Emmeralda
**Voice.** An elven woman in her early thirties, light, quick, musical, with the
professional warmth of somebody who has been charming for a living for fifteen
years. **Then take all of it away.** For the whole of this book she is under it:
flat, plain, considerably younger than she performs, and with no music in her at
all. Read the carved-scene lines as a tired nineteen-year-old, not a courtesan.
The one laugh in chapter six is cracked and unused and should be slightly hard
to listen to.

### Gardwen
**Voice.** A young elven woman raised outdoors. Quiet, direct, no social armour
and no small talk, a voice that has spent more time with weather than with
people. In chapter ten she is hypothermic for most of her dialogue: read those
lines slowly, with the mouth not working properly, short clauses, long gaps —
and do not make it pathetic, because she is not.

### Magda
**Voice.** A barbarian woman of about sixty, built like a gatepost. Blunt,
gravelled, unsentimental and much funnier than she lets on. Says affectionate
things in the tone of an insult and insults in the tone of an affection. Her
level voice while she is rubbing Gardwen's hands is the most frightened anybody
sounds in the book, and none of that fear may appear in the delivery.

### Constantine
**Voice.** A woman in her forties, dockside-raised and not remotely ashamed of
it, sleeves-past-the-elbow. Exact, dry, warm the moment you are inside her door.
Swears like the quay she grew up on and does not moderate it for anybody.
When she dictates her ledger entry, read it as a woman giving evidence: pace
slow, every clause separated, entirely without drama.

### Hild (Orsk & Daughter)
**Voice.** A dwarf woman of about fifty with a cutter's hands. Low, flat,
unhurried, absolute. Short declaratives. Trade pride in every line and no
warmth on the surface at all, with one or two places where enormous kindness
comes out sideways. Her refusal — *I'll not swear a Baron bought it* — is the
moral centre of the book: read it as a statement of fact, never as defiance.

### Temerach Nebulo
**Voice.** An elven knight of about a hundred and fifty who reads as a composed
woman of fifty. Economical to the point of severity, no small talk, no flattery,
no reflex to make a conversation comfortable. Precise consonants. **Never
raises her voice; she narrows it** — under pressure the range of what she is
willing to say shrinks, and the delivery should shrink with it.

### Ila
**Voice.** A thirteen-year-old elven girl. Clear, flat, unblinking, completely
without the up-tilt children use to check whether an adult is pleased. She does
not soften anything, does not perform and does not hedge. Read every one of her
lines as a plain statement of fact, including the ones that break your heart.

### Pell
**Voice.** An eleven-year-old boy of the quays. Fast, cheerful, relentless,
breathless, talking while doing something else. Enormous appetite in the voice.
He recites messages **word-perfect and completely flat**, which should be an
audible gear change from his ordinary chatter.

### Hanzo di Teufel
**Voice.** A man of fifty-one, educated, border-noble. On the pier he has just
run a mile and his voice is wrecked — hoarse, short of breath, not loud.
**The critical direction:** when he speaks to the thing in the chest he is
*kind*. Low, steady, gentle, the voice you use to settle a frightened horse.
That is the most disturbing sound in the audiobook and it must not be sinister.

### Thuban Eta
**Voice.** A grown man's voice — forties, a soldier, worn out — coming out of a
seventeen-year-old's chest, so it must sit slightly wrong: a shade too resonant
for the body, with the resonance in the wrong place, like a note out of a
cracked bell. Very tired. Not demonic. Not processed into a monster. His final
line is the gentlest thing anybody says in the book.

### Nus
**Voice.** A nineteen-year-old thief. Grinning, restless, funny, pitched up and
fast, a voice with something always moving in its hands. Listens badly and
brilliantly. Under pressure he **raises the stakes out loud** and the size of
the offer is the size of the fear — read those lines faster and higher, not
braver.

### Santo di Teufel
**Voice.** Nineteen, and his voice has not finished breaking — it should crack
at least once. Entitled, unexamined, and in the linen room genuinely, horribly
*hopeful*. Play him as a boy who has never been told no and does not recognise
the room he is in. No villain colour anywhere.

### Ossa Kyne
**Voice.** A dredger of fifty-odd. Slow, dry, thoroughly unimpressed, talking
round a pipe that does not come out for conversation. Very long pauses. Finds
most people childish and does not hold it against them.

### Hessa
**Voice.** A woman of about fifty who has kept the tallies for the whole water
since she was nineteen. Flat, fast, economical, and reads figures aloud the way
other people read the weather. Never dramatic and never warm on the surface.
She does not stop working while she talks, and the one time she stops — sits
down, puts the pen down — the delivery should change so audibly that a listener
sits up.

### Tarrent
**Voice.** A soldier of about thirty, unshaven, a long way from home and no
longer impressed by anything. Loud, cheerful, genuinely likeable, and entirely
unaware that he is the villain of the scene he is in. Delivers *"Sweetheart, if
we had a quartermaster I'd have had a hot dinner in Anminster"* as a friendly
joke at his own expense — because that is exactly what he thinks it is.

### Walk-ons
**Sim** — a ferryman with a twenty-minute story and an audience; drops his voice
for the good bit and knows he is doing it.
**Rew** — sixty, a bad hip, tells you the exact size of what he will do and not
one inch more.
**Deller** — asks his question without anger, which is what makes it land.
**Sabbe** — eighty, cheerful about the money, and *"you put it down right, girl"*
is the warmest line in the first act.
**Dess** — anxious, apologetic, rope-seller, talks too much when caught.
**Sten** — a big careful man with his legs not working, grateful and awkward.
**Mariarta** — exact, quiet, repeats a question back before answering it.
**Bett the cook** — Lullwater, crying into onions and blaming the onions.
**Tam** — says almost nothing; one quarter-inch of a nod is his whole part.

## 3. Sound-effect prompts

For ElevenLabs Sound Effects, as chapter stings or bed. Keep them sparse — the
prose is doing the work and the book is not a radio play. They should sit under
the chapter opener and fade, the way the plates sit in the margin.

- `Wet flat-bottomed wooden boat hull knocking once against stone steps, water slapping, distant gulls, outdoor night ambience`
- `A crowded stone market bridge falling completely silent, forty people stopping at once, then a single sack of fish being dragged along wood`
- `Heavy ice blocks sliding and settling inside a cold stone ice-house, low creaks and cracks, dripping, hollow room tone`
- `A single heavy iron chain ringing once against a ship's wet hull at night, then black seawater moving under a stern counter`
- `Blacksmith's shed interior: bellows roaring, a hammer at an anvil in the next building, quench water hissing, no voices`
- `A small hardened steel cutting wheel turning three times against a cold iron ring, close and dry, then a sharp metallic spring as the ring parts`
- `Four crossbows released almost simultaneously at close range outdoors at night, bolts striking, then complete silence over moving water`

## 4. Production notes

**One head per chapter.** Seventeen chapters, six heads. Kag (1, 2, 4, 5, 6, 8,
9, 12, 13, 16, 17), Hessa (3), Marisette (7), Emmeralda (10), Ila (11),
Gardwen (14), Blau (15). The narrator should carry a touch of each POV's
register: driest in Kag's, flattest and most precise in Hessa's, cooler and
more amused in Marisette's — and in chapter fifteen she should be **fond of the
villain**, which is the point of that chapter.

**Chapter buttons.** Every chapter ends on a short flat line that usually
reverses what came before. Full stop, one beat of silence, and out. Never lift
the last line.

**Profanity is native to the narration**, not just the dialogue. Do not
emphasise it and do not soften it.

**Never read a number as a dramatic reveal.** Twenty-six shillings, twenty-five
tents, four hundred and eleven people, the fourth of June, six hundred, three
turns — these are the spine of the book and every one of them must be read as a
plain fact. The prose knows they are devastating. The reader must not.
