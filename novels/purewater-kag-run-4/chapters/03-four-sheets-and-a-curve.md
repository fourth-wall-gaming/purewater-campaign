# Chapter 3 — Four Sheets and a Curve

HESSA HAD KEPT THE TALLIES FOR THE WHOLE OF THAT WATER OUT OF A BACK ROOM BEHIND A CHANDLERY since she was nineteen years old, which was long enough to have formed one settled opinion about the trade, and the opinion was this: almost nobody who works for a man like Crowbill ever brings him anything. They bring him *problems*, which they have decorated, in the hope of being thanked for the decoration.

She had never once lost anything and had never once read anything she was not meant to, and between those two facts she had built a life nobody in that city could see the edges of.

The half-orc came in at six with four loose sheets.

Not the book. Nobody ever got the book, and Hessa respected that more than she would have respected being handed it.

She went through them standing up, fast, the way she did.

Eleven bridges. Priced by the day, by the bridge, with the traffic that was not crossing costed separately from the cut that was not coming, which was a distinction Hessa had been trying to get two grown men to understand since Candlemas. Then the second-order line. Then a curve, drawn by hand, with three shortfalls at one end of it and thirty at the other.

And the Pearl, on its own, at the bottom.

"That's a curve," Hessa said.

"It is."

"He'll like that." She squared the sheets on the table. "He won't like where it goes."

---

The rest of the evening was the part that went wrong, and it went wrong in the most interesting possible way, which is that it produced nothing.

Kag had spent the afternoon asking about the men on the bridges — who they were, where they drank, what their names were — and had come back with a face like a closed account.

"Nobody knows anything."

"Nobody knows anything," Hessa agreed, because she had been getting the same all week and had not until that moment realised it was a fact rather than an absence.

Nine weeks, and not one name. You got *dragonshits*. You got "the tall one" and "the one with the beard" from four separate people who each plainly meant a different man. The publican at the Oyster said they had been in twice; the potboy said every night; both of them were entirely certain. One of Marn's lads swore blind there had been four on the Fishmarket bridge on Tuesday, and there had been two, because Kag had counted them herself that morning.

It was not that people would not tell you. It was that they had nothing to tell.

Everybody on that water had spent nine weeks *not looking at them* — going round, eyes down, getting past — and you cannot remember a face you have spent two months deliberately not seeing.

---

"I think we have to do a little more than that," Kag said.

Hessa did not say no.

What she did was sit down, finally, which she had not done all evening, and which was very much worse — because Hessa standing up is Hessa on an errand and Hessa sitting down is Hessa having a conversation.

"I think we have to put one or two of these boats under the water for a while." Kag had both hands flat on the table. "It's a protection racket. If we're not protecting anyone—"

A beat.

"One of them called me *sweetheart*."

"Boats." Hessa thought about it properly, and you could see her doing the sums, because she did them the same way Kag did. "Two of theirs on the water at the Guild landing, and whatever came off the barge today. It'd cost them a week and there's no body in it."

A pause.

"It's not against the code. It's not even clever enough to be against the code."

She turned the cup a quarter-turn on the table.

"And he'd want to have said yes first. You know that."

Then she looked up, and she had a face like a closed ledger and had had it since she was nineteen, and it did not change at all when she said:

"One of them called you sweetheart."

She let that be the whole thing for a moment.

"Kag. You brought me four sheets and a curve this afternoon and it's the best bit of work anybody's done for him in a year, and Hessa saying that means something." No emphasis anywhere. "And you've just given me eleven bridges, a protection racket that doesn't protect, and *one of them called me sweetheart*, all in the same breath, and I can't tell which one you're actually here about."

She picked up the cup and drank what was left of it herself.

"So I'll ask it the way he'd ask it. If the sweetheart one had said nothing to you all morning — would the boats still be a good idea?"

---

"We both know it's a good idea, even without the insult," Kag said. "You know me. I'm all about business and what works. The only blowback I can see is if these accidents get attributed to us in particular, and if we do it right there shouldn't be a problem."

And then, leaning back:

"And with these fuckers it doesn't matter if we're loud on the streets. Because they're not paying attention to any of it anyway."

Hessa took that. She ran it through and it held and she did not pretend otherwise, which is the entire reason people told her things.

"Yeah. All right. It's a good idea."

She set the cup down.

"And you're right about the last bit, and I'll go further. They don't know who anybody is. Nine weeks and they've not learned a name either — it runs both ways. You could sink both of those boats on Thursday and they'd spend a fortnight looking at the Dredgers, because the Dredgers are who you look at when something goes under and they've been shouting for a month."

That was not comfort. She said it flatly, as a fact about the world, and they both heard the other half of it at the same moment and neither of them said it.

"Here's the only thing I'll stand on."

She held up one finger, which from Hessa was practically shouting.

"He said *cost it*. He didn't say *do* anything. You go under the water without him, and if it goes right he'll say nothing — and if it goes wrong you'll have done it on your own name and not his." A beat. "And he'll still say nothing. That's the bit people miss about him."

She pushed her chair back.

"So ask him. He's on the Lullwater tonight and I can put you in front of him before midnight. Take the boats to him the way you took me the curve. *Cost* it, don't sell it, and he'll either say yes or he'll tell you what you've got wrong, and either way you'll be better off than guessing."

She stood.

"And Kag. The four sheets were good. He's not going to say so."

---

The Oyster was filling up by then, and over in the corner a boatman called Sim who ran the Guild landing ferry had been talking for twenty minutes and had no intention of stopping.

"They can't come in, see." He was enjoying himself enormously. "No road. So the whole lot of them stop dead at the Square and everything's got to go on the water, and *we* all went down to watch, because what else are you doing at noon."

Then he got to the part he had been building to and dropped his voice, which made the eight people round him lean in, which he knew.

"The Baron comes over first. Stands up in the bow, no hand on anything, bare head — lets the whole city have a look at him. Bold as you like."

A beat.

"And then they bring the *box*."

Somebody said something about gold and Sim waved it away, irritably, like a man being interrupted in church.

"Four to carry it and two to keep us off, and it's a bad set of steps and it went over about *that* far—" he showed with his hands, a good fifteen degrees "—and one of them called the other one something you'd not say in front of your mother."

"And?"

"And *nothing*." Sim was delighted. "Not a sound out of it. Not a knock, not a word, nothing shifting inside. And them curtains are laced. Not tied — *laced*. Bottom and sides, like a boot."

The potboy said his cousin was on the caravan road and said it had been like that the whole way. Nine weeks. Never opened.

An old man said it would be relics, for the Prince. A woman said it would be a wife.

And the enormous half-orc at the back of the crowd said, cheerfully, to the room:

"Could be a monster! Something green with big teeth!"

---

It got a laugh. A proper one — the whole corner of the room, and the potboy had to put a jug down, and Sim laughed hardest of anybody because his story had been running twenty minutes and he had been losing them.

"*Big teeth!*" he said, and hit the table.

The woman who had thought it was a wife said, "Green, though, Kag — that's the detail," and somebody else said "that's why they're facing in, they're keeping it *fed*," and for about thirty seconds the Moist Oyster was a very good place to be.

Somebody bought her a drink. Hessa, watching from the end of the bar, made a private note that it was the first time she had seen that happen in nine years.

And then Sim — warmed right up, and talking to Kag now rather than the room, because she was the one who had made his story land:

"You'd not get near it anyway. They've put the lot on the far side of High Isle, the seaward pier. The Baron's own ship — the one that *steams*." He pulled a face. "Have you seen that? Where the hull meets the water. Steams like a kettle, all day, cold or not. I don't go past it any more. The ferrymen won't take a fare that way."

He drank.

"Only twenty aboard and no more. Everything else is east at the Vantt place — horses, farriers, the whole circus. So there's nothing on this water of theirs but the two lighters they're running stores out on, and those tie up at the Guild landing overnight, because there's no room at the pier."

He said it exactly the way a man says a thing that could not possibly be of interest to anybody.

"Two lads on them. Not even proper watchmen. Sleep aboard because it's warmer than a tent."

---

Hessa did not say anything until they were forty yards along the fondamenta with the Oyster's noise shut behind them and nothing but black water on the left.

"Two lads sleep aboard."

Just that. The way she would read out a figure.

She walked another ten yards.

"You said boats. You've been saying boats all evening and I've been agreeing with you about boats — and boats has got a body in it now, and it got one about four minutes ago while a room full of people were laughing at a joke about teeth."

Her breath went up in the cold. It had turned properly cold since dark.

"I'm not telling you no. I've said my one thing and I'm not going to be the woman who says it twice." A pause. "But you've written a lot down today and you've got a head for it. So take four minutes and write this one down as well, before you're standing in front of him."

And here is the question Hessa asked, on a dark fondamenta, forty yards from a room still laughing about a monster with big teeth — the question that is the entire reason four thousand people in the low canals went on living their ordinary lives that week without ever knowing how close it came.

"If a lighter goes under at the Guild landing at three in the morning with two boys asleep in it — what's the bill, and who gets handed it?"

She already knew the answer, because she had read the four sheets.

So did Kag. She had written it on a left-hand page at one o'clock that afternoon and underlined it once.
