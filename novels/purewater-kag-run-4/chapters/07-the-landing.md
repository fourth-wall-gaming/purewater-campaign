# Chapter 7 — The Landing

ILA HAD BEEN TOLD TO BE SOMEWHERE ELSE FOR FOUR DAYS, by adults who were managing their own grief so badly that they had entirely failed to notice she was managing hers alone, on a step, in the cold, where she could see the front door — and she had decided, at some point on the Wednesday, without telling anybody, that she was going to do something about it.

She was thirteen. She had lived at the Sylph's Embrace since she was nine. Emmeralda had taught her to read out of a book of shipping notices because it was the only book in the house that wasn't filthy, and had fed her every single night for four years without once making a performance of it, and had cried in front of her exactly twice, and had never, not once, told her to go and be somewhere else.

So on Thursday morning, when the enormous grey woman came up the stairs and the room went quiet in that particular way that means adults are about to arrange something without you, Ila stood in the doorway with a jug she hadn't been sent for and said the true thing, which was that he had never once looked at her.

Nobody argued with it, which surprised her.

She got her shoes.

---

The Long Butts had four thousand people on it by noon and every single one of them was complaining about the wind, which was coming off the open water across the range instead of along it and had been doing it since before light.

Ila went up the slope with a basket of somebody else's washing on her hip — three different houses' linen in one basket, which was the sort of detail that never mattered and this once did — and she went slowly, and she stopped twice, and she watched.

That was the whole of what Kag had told her. *Don't hurry, don't be clever, and look at the ground before you walk on it.*

The ground was this: a stone quay at the west end where the slope ran out, nine steps down to the water, a bollard line, and four thousand people who were all, in about two hours, going to want it at the same moment.

The household box was black with six chairs and five of them were full.

He was in the one on the right, forward in the seat with his elbows on the rail, watching the shooting like a boy at a fair, and he was nineteen years old and quite beautiful and he had opened Emmeralda up the length of her body with a barbed knife, and Ila looked at him for a long time from forty feet away in a crowd of four thousand and felt nothing whatsoever except a great and useful clarity.

The wide grey man stood on the stone below the box and read the crowd in slow sweeps, left to right, the way you read a page.

---

The elf in plain harness won the archery, which Ila did not really watch, and afterwards there were ninety seconds in front of the Governor's box where four thousand people leaned forward and waited for something.

The elf took the wreath and said eleven words and walked off the grass.

The slope didn't know what to do with that and cheered anyway, and Ila thought, with the ruthlessness available only to the very young: *you had them and you didn't use it.*

Then the horn went for the close, and four thousand people stood up at once and started thinking about the water, and it happened exactly the way the half-orc had said it would at dawn.

They came off the slope. The landing took forty at a time. The boats were not ready and were never going to be ready, because Purewater boatmen have never in the history of that city been ready for anybody in a hurry. And the household came down onto the stone with everybody else and stood in it — the Baron with two knights at his shoulder, and the son three feet from a fishmonger's wife with a live crab in a bag.

Ila went in from the bollard side.

She came up on his blind side, which is the side away from the water, because a man on a quay watches the water. The bundle went from her hand to his without either of them looking down at it. He closed his fingers on it out of pure reflex, the way a man takes a note at a party.

She was four people away before he'd understood he was holding anything.

It was perfect. It was absolutely perfect, and she knew it was perfect, and she was eleven feet clear and already composing how she would tell Bett about it.

And then there was a hand around the top of her arm.

---

He did not grab her. That was the thing she would think about afterwards, on and off, for years. He put his hand round her arm the way you take hold of a gate — no squeeze in it, no shake — and turned her to face him, and crouched slightly so that his eyes were level with hers, which no adult had done to her since she was seven.

Up close he was thin and white-skinned and red-eyed with old burn down one side of his face into the collar, and he smelled of nothing at all.

"What's your name?"

And Ila, who had no social reflexes, who did not move aside for people, who did not soften things and had never learned to perform, heard herself say: "Ila."

"Where are you taking that washing, Ila?"

"The Promise of Heaven."

Which was a lie, and it was a good lie, and it came out of her flat and unblinking — and she watched him receive it, and file it, and not believe it, all without moving his face at all.

"That's not true," he said, without any unkindness whatsoever. "But I'm not going to do anything about it, because I have hold of a child in a crowd and that is not a thing I do."

Eight seconds. That was the whole of it. Eight seconds and a name.

---

What saved her was the water, and the water was not an accident.

Somewhere behind her in the press the whole landing lurched — forty people going forward at once because a boat had finally come alongside — and a woman two along from the grey man lost the stone entirely and went in between the hull and the steps with a shriek and a great green gout of canal.

Three men grabbed. The grey man let go of Ila's arm and took the woman's wrist and hauled her out one-handed onto the stone like a man landing a net, and set her on her feet, and looked around.

Ila was already gone, over the Fishmarket bridge and home by the back of the ice-houses, running the way she had been taught by nobody at all.

She was sick twice on the way, behind the ice-houses, quietly, and then went in the water door and up the back stairs and sat down on the second-floor landing with her arms round the wolf, who put up with it.

---

Out on the water, the household's boat pulled for High Isle. A nineteen-year-old sat in the stern with one hand flat inside his coat, looking at the wake and not at anybody, already composing the rest of his life.

And on the stones the wide grey man stood a while in the thinning crowd with his hat in his hands, turning over a name he had been given for nothing by a child who had lied to him about everything else.

He did not use it that day. He did not use it the next day either.

He was extremely good at his job, and the worst thing about him — the thing that made him worse than any of the men with swords — was that he never once had to hurry.
