# Chapter 12 — The Ice Run

KAG HAD EXPECTED IT TO BE HARDER, and the thing she was not prepared for — the thing nobody warns you about, because the people who could warn you have all made a private arrangement not to discuss it — was how ordinary it was, and how completely the world declined to mark it.

He went down into the boat still in a girl's blue cloak with his feet bare, and he did not fight, because it had not yet occurred to him that there was anything to fight. He had been told nothing. He had spent four hours in a chair on a wet floor being asked questions by a mage in plain clothes who never once raised their voice, and he had answered all of them, at length, eagerly, the way a clever boy answers a tutor, because being asked about his father's work was the closest thing to affection anybody had offered him in a year.

Out past the last of the Pearl the light came up grey off the water. The Lost Isle was a low green nothing with a fringe of poles in the shallows where the pots were set. Nobody lives on it. The men who lift the pots come at nine.

He asked one question on the way over, about halfway.

"Is my father paying?"

She did not answer him, and after a while he stopped expecting one, and then he sat and watched the water go past exactly the way anybody does.

It was quick, and it was done from behind, and he did not see it coming.

---

The rest of it was work, and it is a strange mercy of this world that work is always available to a person who needs the next ten minutes to have a shape.

There is a gutting board on the stilt-shed that the pot-men use, and a knife on a lanyard, and a bucket. The pots went down full and heavy on the far string where the water runs deep and cold. The blue cloak went down with a stone in it somewhere else entirely, because you never put two things in one place. The board got three buckets of seawater and a scrub with sand and another three buckets.

The tide turned while she was washing her arms.

And that was all. There was no thunder. No gull did anything significant. The sun came up over the Mouth the way it comes up over the Mouth about three hundred and sixty times a year, and somewhere eleven miles east a hundred and forty men were waking up in a cold field to find there was still no bread.

She sat on the stilt-shed steps for four minutes with her wet hands on her knees and let herself have those, and then she got in the boat.

Afterwards, when people asked her about that week — and eventually people did ask her, because that is what happens to you once a story gets loose — she would talk about the bridge, or the bill, or the pier, and she would be generous and funny about all of it.

She never once mentioned the Lost Isle.

---

By the time she was back on the Shoals the city had stopped working, and it had stopped working *politely*, which was the part that made it beautiful.

There was a Dragon Knight on the Fishmarket steps trying to hire a boatman to take him to High Isle, and not one of the six men sitting there was looking up. There was no ice. There was no bread off the Fishmarket. There was no boat at the Vantt landing and no boy to run a message and no rope to be had when a man wanted rope, and everybody was very, very sorry about all of it.

Nobody was rude. That was the instruction and that was the genius of it. You cannot put a city to the sword for being apologetic.

Four Knights rowed themselves to High Isle that morning in a hired boat with nobody to hire, and went round and round in the Lullwater for twenty minutes while the fish market watched in reverent silence, and one of them lost his pole and had to be given it back by a nine-year-old.

Nobody laughed out loud.

The entire city laughed for the rest of the year.

---

Temple Isle has no bridge to it, so half of Purewater was afloat by first light, gunwale to gunwale in a great slow ring off the steps with families standing up in the boats and children on shoulders and a man working the outside of the ring selling something hot out of a tub, which is the most Purewater fact available about any religious occasion.

The Sacred Confluence comes down off the temple in shallow terraces to a pool at the waterline and a broad stair of wet green stone, and by the Order's long form every combatant and the head of their house goes down to be blessed before the sand. Nerissa's novices worked the running order with white rods and not one atom of deference to anybody, which Kag enjoyed enormously.

The di Teufels were called near the end.

The Baron came down the green stone with his shirt off and there it was: a flat black chain, close to the throat, and something small on it under the hollow of his collarbone. Nine weeks in this city and nobody had ever seen him without it, and here he was in front of four thousand people stripped to the waist on somebody else's ground, because the Lady's water does not care whose army you brought.

And behind him, bare-headed in daylight for the second time in his life, came a boy of seventeen who was too thin for the body he was in and who walked like somebody being walked.

The stair packed solid behind them with forty people trying to see.

A body-length off the Baron's left shoulder, going down the steps with his head turned the other way, was a nineteen-year-old thief called Nus who had the best hands on that water and had been circling Kag for two days looking for somebody to tell him he was the man for it.

---

The priestess put the Baron under with a hand flat on the back of his neck and held him there a good deal longer than that man had been held anywhere in nine weeks, and Kag, sitting in a hired skiff twenty-five metres out, revised her opinion of the Order considerably upwards.

He came up streaming. Somebody put a cloth in his hands. The whole stair closed in behind to get the boy in next, and for about four seconds there was nothing on those steps but wet backs and elbows.

Nus went past him on the step above, close as a man passing in a corridor, and his hand went across the Baron's throat and off again, and he kept walking.

Nothing came away in his fist.

And nothing happened, either — the Baron did not turn round, because he had a cloth over his face and his son's killer was twenty-five metres away in a hired boat and a thief had just had his fingers on the thing at his throat, and he was drying his eyes.

Kag was the only person on that water who saw it, and she was the only person who saw *why*.

There was no clasp on it.

It was a closed ring, worn short and tight at the collarbone, and it had been on that man since before any of this started, and it did not lift and it did not come over a head. It came off with a cut, off the living man, or it did not come off at all.

Nus got to the top of the stair and turned and found her across the water, and stood there, waiting to be told.

And Kag shook her head. Once. That was the entire conversation.

Something went out of his shoulders, and he turned and went up between the temple wall and the crowd and did not look back.

---

She would find out weeks later what that had cost — or rather what it had *not* cost, which is a harder thing to be told about and impossible to feel properly.

Below the Fishmarket there are four thousand people living on plank walkways and boat-houses on stilts and six rope bridges, in the one part of Purewater that armed men have never gone into, because horses are useless there and the locals know every roof. If that chain had come off the Baron's neck on the Temple steps in front of the whole city, they would have gone in at dawn with hooks, and they would have cut the bridges first, because cutting six rope bridges turns a neighbourhood into forty islands of frightened people who cannot reach each other.

None of that happened.

It did not happen because a debt collector in a hired skiff shook her head at a boy across twenty-five metres of water, and not one of those four thousand people will ever know it, and that is simply how it works.

The boy came up out of the Lady's water and stood there dripping and did not wipe his face and did not blink.

The priestess who had put him under took her hand off him early — a count or two early, where every other man on that stair had got the full measure and a bit over for being important — and stepped back off the bottom step with her skirts dragging and looked up the terraces at the High Priestess, and waited to be told something.

Nerissa did not tell her anything. Nerissa said the words over the boy from where she stood, in the same voice she had used all morning, and the white rods moved the next house down.

The Baron already had his shirt on.

Kag watched the pair of them go up the green stone and thought about a chain with no clasp, and how you would get at a thing like that, and where a man like that ever stands still.

She had no idea at all.

She had, however, just watched four thousand people on a religious holiday, and every last one of them had been looking at somebody else.
