# Chapter 15 — An Itemised Bill

KAG FOUND THE MAN WHO WAS FOLLOWING HER IN ABOUT A MINUTE, which was not a triumph of tradecraft on her part but an act of mercy on his, because he was two boats back and he had plainly never done this before in his life and he was trying extremely hard.

He changed ferries when she changed ferries. He got out at the Fishmarket steps and then could not think of a single reason to be at the Fishmarket steps, so he bought a fish he did not want and stood on the stones holding it.

It was Dess. Rope and cordage, third pitch along. Eleven shillings short on Monday because four of them had walked off with sixty feet of his best line.

She came up behind him and cleared her throat, and Dess went about four inches straight up and the fish went in the water.

"That was my dinner," he said, and then shut his eyes.

It took ten seconds after that. A shilling a day and sixpence for anything written down, and he was to say where she went and who she spoke to and whether she went on the water at night, and he gave the whole thing up so fast and so completely that it was obvious he had been sick about it since noon.

"They gave me a slate," Dess said, and produced a slate. There was nothing on it. "I didn't know how to spell half orc."

So she took the slate off him and wrote it out in a hand better than his and worse than a clerk's.

*Went Pearl about 3. Sylph's Embrace. Big argument with the woman Constantine out in the street, heard by all. Left angry. Fishmarket. Ate. Did not go on the water.*

Not one word of it was a lie. That was the trick of it, and it was a trick she had off nine years of debt collection: the very best lie you will ever tell is a true statement with the interesting bit taken out.

"There's eight of us," Dess said, when he had got his coat closed over it, and he said it without being asked, because he was talking now and could not stop. "He hired eight off this bridge out of his own purse and told every one of us we were the only one."

"Where do the slates go?"

"Six o'clock. Under the third arch of the Gate of Triumph bridge, on the water. He doesn't come himself." Dess rubbed his thumb on the frame. "There's a woman in a boat."

---

The woman in the boat was fifty-odd and built like the winch she was sitting next to, with cloth wound round her head and her boots off in October and a pipe that did not come out of her mouth for conversation, and she saw Kag coming a very long time before Kag arrived, because she had spent thirty-five years looking at water for a living and knowing what was in it that should not be.

She hauled the bucket up off its line and set it inside the gunwale, which meant no slates tonight, and then let Kag come the rest of the way in her own time.

"Say it out loud or clear off."

Kag laughed at herself, which turned out to be the correct opening, and asked whether the woman knew her.

"Everybody on this water knows you. You're Crowbill's counting — big grey one, does the bridges on the Shoals, reads a book." The pipe went round to the other side. "And you're the one that stood on the Fishmarket bridge on Monday and told four of the Baron's men to their faces they were thieves, in front of the whole market, and then walked up the steps."

A beat.

"My sister's boy was on that bridge. He's told it nine hundred times and it gets better every time."

Her name was Ossa Kyne and she did not do favours — she said so, plainly, twice, because favours make you part of a thing and she had spent thirty-five years not being part of things. Then she answered the question anyway, for her sister's boy, which is how almost everything in that city actually gets done.

"He's not looking for a man. Not once did he say a man, nor a body, nor a size, nor a night." She came up with a folded square of paper out of an oilcloth. "He's looking for a knife."

It was a drawing. A single blade near enough to life size, done in ink with a fine nib, barbed along the back edge, and a mark near the hilt rendered with a great deal more care than the rest of it — every line of it separate, by somebody who knew that mark extremely well.

Underneath, in a small tidy hand: *any canal, any depth. Ten sovereigns. No questions asked or answered.*

"He gave one of those to every dredger on the water," Ossa said.

Ten sovereigns was more than that woman made in a season, and there were eleven dredgers on that water, and the man had paid it out of his own purse in a city that would not sell him bread.

*He is not frightened of a body*, Kag thought, sitting in a skiff under a bridge in the dark with a drawing in her hand. *He is frightened of a knife.*

---

And the knife had never left the room.

That was the whole of it, and she had not done it out of cleverness. She had done it on Tuesday night because a debt collector knows that a thing in your hand is yours and a thing on a floor with a witness in the doorway belongs to the truth. She had turned it a quarter-turn with the back of her own knife and taken a rubbing and left it lying.

So on Friday evening, four nights on, it was still against the skirting on the third floor at the Sylph's Embrace — in a house the grey man had walked through that same afternoon, asking politely after a thirteen-year-old girl — with the blood gone from black and tacky to black and dry.

She took it out in a doubled linen off Constantine's press, tied at both ends with kitchen twine, laid in with the flat of her own blade so that no finger of hers went near the hooks.

And in the other hand she had the thing that was worth more, which was a house ledger written on the night, standing up, in a clear round hand, by the woman who owned the room.

---

Temerach Nebulo was at the back corner table of the Moist Oyster with her back to the wall and a mail coat across her knees, going along the rings one at a time with a small tool, while down at the fire end Thorne told forty people about an elf who had put four arrows in the middle at two hundred paces that morning. It had been three arrows that morning. By Thursday it would be six and a gale.

The elf was ignoring all of it, which Kag liked her for immediately.

She put the tool down flat on the table, which from a woman who had not stopped for the whole of Thorne's performance was a considerable quantity of attention.

"Say the girl's name or don't, I don't need it yet. The scroll. How much of it is there, is it burnt, and how do you know the hand is his?"

Kag told her. Three pieces. Out of a grate. A grown man's fast practised hand, and the same figure drawn twice that was cut into the pommel.

"Who saw it come out of the grate besides you?"

Constantine. And it was in her house ledger, written that night, standing up, and she had offered to countersign it in daylight.

"And the mark on the knife?"

Orsk & Daughter, in the Forge Quarter. Hild's book, chained to the bench, four generations of it. Cut to a stranger's drawing on the fourth of June — four months before the column ever came down the Anminster road.

Something happened behind the elf's face and was gone before it could be named.

"Where is the knife now?"

"We've got it. You can have the lot of it if you ask."

And Temerach Nebulo, who had spent four years trying to get Hanzo di Teufel called to answer and had never once dared open her mouth about it, did not say yes.

"A Purewater thief walks into a tavern," she said, "and offers a King's officer the one thing she has wanted for four years, for nothing at all." Her eyes came up. "What do you want?"

"I want the fucker gone. He's a scourge on this city and I resent his men strutting about like they own the place."

The elf nodded once, because it matched what three separate people had told her about this woman before supper.

"Good. That I can work with. A man who wants paying I have to think about."

---

At seven the next morning, in a shed on the water at the end of the founders' lane, two women who kept books established inside about ninety seconds that they liked each other, and then went at it like a stocktake.

Constantine described a clerk's hand. Hild described a callus. Constantine said *ink on the middle finger, not the first*, and Hild said *that's a man who's held a pen fifty years*, and Temerach said almost nothing and wrote almost everything down in a small tight hand on paper she had brought with her.

Kag stood at the back by the quench tub with her arms folded and let the honest people work, and she was, on the whole, more uncomfortable there than she had been on the Lost Isle, and she did not examine that.

Then Hild put both hands flat on the bench and said no.

"I'll not swear a Baron bought it. A Baron didn't buy it. A Purewater man of about fifty-five bought it, with ink on his middle finger and the buttons cut off his coat so there'd be nothing on him to know him by, and he stood where you're stood and he was frightened the whole time he was in this shed."

She looked up.

"My great-grandfather started that book. Nobody has ever put a thing in it that wasn't so."

And Temerach Nebulo, who had not moved from the door in half an hour, let out a breath that was almost certainly relief.

"Good," she said. "That's the only kind of witness worth having."

A beat.

"Would you know him again?"

---

Hild went to the back of the shed and was gone a while, and there was the sound of a chest, and she came back with a second book — smaller, unchained, the instrument book — and turned the pages with her thumb.

"I've made for him. Twice. A pair of calipers and a brass rule with a Guild stamp on the tang."

She turned it round.

"I never knew him in the shed in June because he wasn't in his coat. That's why he cut the buttons off." She tapped the page. "It's not the buttons. It's the *indigo*."

There was a name on the line, and a Guild, and the date was two years old, and the hand was Hild's own.

Ivo Calder. Tide Master, Watercrafters Guild. Assessor by appointment to the city of Purewater.

"That," said Constantine, to nobody in particular, "is the man who signed off the Lady's fouling as a false reading in the spring."

The bellows went next door and the whole shed breathed in, and four women stood round a bench in the Forge Quarter and understood, all at the same moment and none of them saying it, that whatever had come down the Anminster road nine weeks ago had been *invited*.

Kag went out into the founders' lane at eight in the morning with sixty workshops around her and a question she had no idea how to answer, which was how you get a closed ring of black iron off a living man's throat in a room full of people in about two seconds.

She was still standing there when Pell came round the corner at a dead run.
