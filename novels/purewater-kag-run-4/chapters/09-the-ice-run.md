# Chapter 9 — The Ice Run

He went down into the boat still in a girl's blue cloak with his feet bare, and he did not fight it, because it had not occurred to him yet that there was anything to fight.

Past the last of the Pearl the light came up grey off the water. The Lost Isle was a low green nothing with a fringe of poles in the shallows where the pots were set. Nobody lived on it. The men who lifted the pots came at nine.

He asked one question on the way over, about halfway.

"Is my father paying?"

She did not answer him, and after a while he stopped expecting one, and then he sat and watched the water go past the way anybody does.

It was quick and it was done from behind and he did not see it coming.

The rest of it was work. There was a gutting board on the stilt-shed that the pot-men used, and a knife on a lanyard, and a bucket. The pots went down full and heavy on the far string where the water ran deep and cold. The blue cloak went down with a stone in it somewhere else. The board got three buckets of seawater and a scrub with sand.

The tide turned while she was washing her arms.

---

By the time she was back on the Shoals the first thing she saw was a Dragon Knight on the Fishmarket steps trying to get a boatman to take him to High Isle, and not one of the six men there was looking up.

The streets had stopped working at first light and they had stopped working politely.

Nobody was rude to any of them. That was the thing about it. There was no ice. There was no bread off the Fishmarket. There was no boat at the Vantt landing and no boy to run a message and no rope when they wanted rope, and everybody was very, very sorry.

Four Knights rowed themselves to High Isle that morning in a hired boat with nobody to hire, and went round and round in the Lullwater for twenty minutes while the fish market watched, and one of them lost a pole.

Nobody laughed out loud. Everybody laughed for the rest of the week.

---

Temple Isle has no bridge to it, so half of Purewater was afloat that morning, gunwale to gunwale in a slow ring off the steps, families standing up in the boats and children on shoulders and somebody working the outside of the ring selling something hot out of a tub.

The Sacred Confluence came down off the temple in shallow terraces to a pool at the waterline and a broad stair of wet green stone. Nerissa's novices worked the running order with white rods and no deference to anybody. The houses went down by precedence, the head of each with his champion, stripped to the waist for the water, and were put under by a priestess who was not gentle about it.

The di Teufels were called near the end.

The Baron came down the green stone with his shirt off and there it was. A flat black chain, close to the throat, and something small on it under the hollow of his collarbone.

Behind him, bare-headed in the light for the second time in his life, came the boy who was too thin for the body he was in, and who walked like somebody being walked.

The stair packed solid behind them with forty people trying to see.

A body-length off the Baron's left shoulder, going down the steps with his head turned the other way, was Nus.

---

The priestess put the Baron under with a hand flat on the back of his neck and held him there a good deal longer than that man had been held anywhere in nine weeks.

He came up streaming. Somebody put a cloth in his hands. The whole stair closed in behind to get the boy in next, and for about four seconds there was nothing on those steps but wet backs and elbows.

Nus went past him on the step above, close as a man passing in a corridor, and his hand went across the Baron's throat and off again and he kept walking.

Nothing came away in his fist.

Nothing happened either. The Baron did not turn round. He had a cloth over his face and a boy had just had his fingers on the thing at his throat, and he was drying his eyes.

Kag was twenty-five metres off in a hired skiff and she was the only person on that water who had seen it, and she saw why.

There was no clasp on it. It was a closed ring, worn short and tight at the collarbone, and it had been on that man since before any of this started. It did not lift and it did not come over a head. It came off with a cut, or it did not come off.

Nus got to the top of the stair, turned, and found her across the water.

She shook her head once. That was the whole conversation.

Something went out of his shoulders and he turned and went up between the temple wall and the crowd and did not look back.

---

On the stair the priestess had the champion.

She put her hand flat on the back of the boy's neck the way she had done it forty times that morning and walked him down into the pool, and the water went over him.

And she took her hand off him early.

Not much. A count or two, where every other man on that stair had got the full measure and a bit more for being important. She stepped back off the bottom step with her skirts dragging and looked up the terraces at Nerissa and waited there to be told something.

Nerissa did not tell her anything. Nerissa said the words over the boy from where she stood, in the same voice she had used all morning, and the white rods moved the next house down.

The boy came up out of the Lady's water and stood there dripping and did not wipe his face and did not blink.

The Baron already had his shirt on.

---

The champion took the sand at noon and won it in under a minute.

He put a grown knight of the King's Assay down twice and then broke his leg in three places with the flat of the thing, and the crowd on the slope went quiet instead of loud, because it was not sport and everybody could tell. The helm never came off. The man he broke was alive and would never walk right.

Kag slept through it in the room over the chandler's and woke in the middle of the afternoon to a boy sitting on the end of the bed with his feet not reaching the floor.

---

Gardwen went into the sea at dusk off the seaward rocks at High Isle and left her boots on a flat stone above the tideline, tied together at the tops, side by side, the way you leave them at a bathing place.

The ship lit up at nine. Lanterns on deck, four of them, then six. Men out along the pier with boat-hooks going down into the water by the mooring chains, and a light held over the side and walked the whole length of the hull twice. A voice giving orders. Not shouting.

They were at it the better part of half an hour and they never brought anything up.

Kag sat down out of the wind on the seaward side and watched a wolf.

It was a long time. The tide went all the way out and the rocks came up black and streaming. Nus fell asleep sitting up at midnight and woke with a jerk every ten minutes. The ship's lanterns went from six back down to two.

The wolf did not move for three hours.

Then, past two, she came off the high rock in four jumps and went into the shallows to her chest and stood facing out with her head low, and made a sound with her mouth shut.

Forty feet out, something changed shape on the water.

She came in on her side doing a stroke with one arm that was not a stroke any more, and the wolf went out to her and got a mouthful of her shirt and hauled, and Kag went in off the rocks to her thighs and took the rest.

She was cold past shivering. Her lips were grey-blue and her hands were dead white and the rocks had opened her up the length of one shin.

She could not talk for a while.

Then she got hold of Kag's collar with a hand that could not grip and said it straight into her face.

"He *begs*."
