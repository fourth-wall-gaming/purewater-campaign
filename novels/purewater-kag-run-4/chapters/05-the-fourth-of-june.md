# Chapter 5 — The Fourth of June

The Forge Quarter starts before the sun does.

It is High Isle's eastern point and it is hammers, a dozen of them going at different speeds out of different sheds, and under that the bellows-roar and the shriek of something being drawn. Coal smoke and hot iron and quench-water, and from the tanneries at the north end a thing that gets into the back of the throat and stays. Grit on everything. It is the only quarter in Purewater where the water runs black and the dwarves who run half of it have been complaining about that for two hundred years.

Kag took a bench outside a cookshop at six with a view of three sheds and a bowl, and sent Pell in with the rubbing.

He was good at it. He went in with his hands empty and said it the same way every time — *Kag'd like to know who cut this, please* — and showed the slip and did not touch anything. Three men sent him away. The fourth did not.

---

The fourth was a shed at the end of the founders' lane on the water. Orsk & Daughter, cutters and engravers. Four generations of it.

Old Orsk was seventy and did not come out from the back any more. It was the daughter who came to the door with the rubbing in her hand, held up close to her eyes, and she was fifty-odd and had arms like a cooper's and she was not pleased.

Her name was Hild.

She looked down the lane and found Kag on the bench and jerked her head, which meant come.

"Where's the knife," Hild said.

"On a floor."

"Whose floor."

"A girl at the Sylph's Embrace. He opened her from the collarbone to the hip with it and went out of a window."

Hild stood in her own doorway with the slip in her fingers and did not say anything for a bit.

"Girl live?"

"She lived."

"Right." Hild turned the slip over. "Then you can have it."

---

She did not hand it over straight away. She went to the ledger first.

It was a proper one, chained to the bench, and it had four generations in it, and she ran a finger down a column and her face changed.

"I said nine weeks," she said. "That's wrong. I was going off the job and not off the book."

She turned the ledger round so Kag could see it and tapped the line.

*4 June. Mark cut to drawing, pommel, fine. 2s 6d. Paid whole.*

"Four months back," Hild said.

Kag looked at the date for a while.

The di Teufel column had come down the Anminster road nine weeks ago with four hundred men and every bell in three villages going. The mark on that pommel had been cut in this shed four months before that.

"Tell me about him," Kag said.

"Purewater man. Fifty-five or thereabouts. Ink on the middle finger, not the first, which is a man who's held a pen fifty years." Hild shut the ledger. "Paid cash. In forty years I've had maybe three men pay cash for engraving."

"What did he look like."

"He'd cut the buttons off his coat."

"He'd what."

"Cut them off. Both rows. There was thread where they'd been and he'd not tidied it." Hild put her hands flat on the bench. "I thought he'd fallen on hard times and was ashamed of it, and I was sorry for him, and I did him a good job for two and six."

She looked up.

"He was frightened the whole time he was in this shed."

---

The Pearl went dark at ten that morning.

Marisette shut all eleven houses and she did it in one hour and she did not explain it to anybody, and by noon it was the only thing anybody in Purewater was talking about and the Fishmarket bridge was carrying ten times its usual traffic because half the island had nowhere to be.

Kag costed it on the ice-house step before the bells had finished.

Eleven houses. Four hundred and eleven people on that island who worked in them or off them — the girls, the cooks, the linen women, the boatmen who did nothing but the Pearl run, the boy who did the lamps, the four fiddlers, the woman who sold hot ones on the quay at two in the morning to people coming out. Four hundred and eleven, and she knew the number because she had the Pearl's traffic in the back of her book for five years and she had never once had a reason to add it up before.

A day of it was a hundred and thirty pounds off the island. A week of it was what the whole occupation had cost the Shoals since the column came down the road.

She brought it to Crowbill and he read it standing up and then he sat down, which he did not do.

"That's more in a night than they've cost me all week," he said.

"Yes."

"So what's the answer."

"You know the answer."

"Say it anyway."

"The answer is you go to the Baron with a purse and you settle," Kag said. "You pay him to keep his boys off the stalls and you pay Marisette's losses for a fortnight out of the same purse, and the island opens tomorrow and everything in my book comes right inside a month. That's the arithmetic. I did it twice."

Crowbill waited.

"And I'll not do it," Kag said.

"No?"

"He hasn't got four hundred men. He's got twenty-five tents and eleven of them are cold and he can't move a sack of flour in this city without hiring a Purewater man to do it. He's not an army sat on us. He's a bluff, and the price of a bluff goes up every single time somebody pays it, and if you pay this one you'll be paying the next one at Michaelmas and the one after at Candlemas, and by the spring this isn't your city."

She shut the book.

"You asked me for a number. That's the number. The number says settle. I'm telling you the number's wrong and I'm telling you why, and that's the whole of what you pay me for and you've never once let me do it before today."

Crowbill sat for a long while.

Then he got up and opened a drawer that Kag had never seen open and took out a slate with names on it.

"Right," he said. "Then you'd better know what I've got."

He set one price, at the end of it, and it was the only one.

"The twenty-five stays in this yard. Not on a bridge. Not to Marisette. Not to a Sword of the Lake with a nice face. The day that gets out is the day they find out we've counted them, and then they have to either go or make it true, and neither of those is a thing I want happening on a Thursday."

---

The presentation of the entrants was that afternoon on the competitors' ground and half the city half-watched it.

Every entrant goes bare-headed in front of the Crown's representative for the space of a bow. It is ceremony and it is boring and the Baron could not avoid it without forfeiting his entry.

So the champion came out in harness with the rest of them and the helm came off for about four seconds.

He was seventeen. He was too thin for the armour he was in and he had the white skin of a thing kept somewhere without light, and he stood in daylight in front of several hundred people and he did not blink.

Thirty yards away on the ground an elf-woman with a wolf at her heel made a sound Kag heard over the top of four hundred other people.

Kag got to her before Magda did.

"That's my brother," Gardwen said.

"How long."

"Ten years. They took him at seven." She was not crying and her hands were shaking and she had hold of the rail. "He's been thirty yards from me since Monday."

"What do you want."

Gardwen said what she wanted. She wanted the Baron's son, taken, held, and traded.

"No," Kag said.

"Then—"

"No, and I'll tell you why, and then I'll tell you what I'll do instead." Kag put her back against the rail so she was looking the other way, at the crowd, which is how you talk to somebody on a ground with four hundred people on it. "You take a lordling as a hostage and you have started a war, and the war happens on the Shoals and on the Lullwater and in the low canals where four thousand people live on planks, and none of them have ever heard your brother's name."

"That's your answer."

"That's the cost, and you asked me the wrong question. Here's mine." Kag looked at the ground. "I'll bring your brother to Crowbill. Not the lordling. Him. And Crowbill can hide a thing in this city that a Baron cannot find with four hundred men, and he has been doing it since before you were born."

Gardwen looked at her for a long moment.

"Nobody in this city has offered me anything," she said.

"Nobody in this city has asked you a question either," Kag said. "I'm asking. What does he answer to."

And Gardwen told her his name, which was the first time Kag had heard it.
