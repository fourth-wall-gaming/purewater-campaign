# Chapter 5 — The Fourth of June

KAG WENT UP TO THE THIRD FLOOR AT CONSTANTINE'S EXPECTING HORROR and found instead the specific, domestic, unbearable thing that always turns out to be worse: a room nobody had cleaned, because nobody cleans a room while a girl is dying in the one next door.

A basin with the water still in it. A shift over the back of a chair. Somebody's slippers under the bed, side by side, the way you leave them when you expect to want them again.

She didn't touch anything for ten minutes. She got down on the boards with the lamp on the floor and looked along them at eye height, which is a trick she had off a man who salvaged, and then she put her shoulder behind the armoire and walked it out from the wall and put it back.

It was lying against the skirting where it had gone.

Eleven inches, and it was not a weapon. The back edge was barbed the whole length of it — not serrated, *barbed*, small swept-back hooks like fish-hooks — and those are for opening a thing slowly and holding it open while you work, and the moment Kag understood what she was looking at she had to put her hand flat on the boards for a second.

There was a groove down the flat. The grip was wound with something dark that was not leather and she elected, as a matter of policy, not to think about that today.

And set into the pommel, small, and beautifully done by somebody who had been paid properly for it: a ring with a line through it and three strokes off the line.

She did not pick it up.

This was the single best decision she made all week and she made it for entirely unglamorous reasons — she was a debt collector, and a debt collector knows that the moment a thing is in your hand it is *yours*, and that a knife on a floor in a house with a witness in the doorway is worth ten of the same knife in a thief's pocket four days later. So she got down with her cheek nearly on the boards and turned it a quarter-turn with the back of her own knife, so the pommel came up into the lamplight, and she tore a leaf off the back of Constantine's ledger and took a stub of charcoal out of her coat and took a rubbing.

Four goes, because the boards weren't flat. The fourth came out clean.

"You'll want the grate," Constantine said from the door.

---

There were three pieces in the grate and they came up in three pieces and a great deal of black that went to nothing between her fingers.

What survived was the middle. Two palm-sized pieces of heavy paper, scorched brown at the margins and still cream in the centre, and a third the size of a playing card, and there was writing on them. Not printing — a dense sloping hand in brown ink with the letters run together, and in among the lines, drawn small and exact, figures. One of them was a ring with a line through it and three strokes off the line, twice over, and the rest of them Kag had never seen and did not like.

The paper was warm.

There was no fire in that grate and had not been for two days.

"Whose hand?" said Constantine.

"Not his."

"Not whose?"

"Not a nineteen-year-old's." Kag turned the biggest piece to the lamp. "That's a man who's written this sort of thing a hundred times and doesn't have to think about the shape of his letters. That's fifty years of holding a pen."

And Constantine — forty-six, forearms out, proprietor of a house of other people's daughters for twenty years — went to the washstand and came back with a pen and an ink bottle and the house ledger, the actual one, the one she did standing up, and opened it on the bed and began to write, fast, in a clear round hand.

"Half three or thereabouts. The twenty-first." The nib scratched. "The knife where it lies, behind the armoire, against the skirting, not moved. Three pieces of burnt paper out of the grate, taken by—"

She stopped.

"Kag."

"Kag what?"

"Kag of no house," Kag said. "It's the only one I've got."

Constantine wrote it and dated it and blotted it with the heel of her hand, and it was, although neither of them knew it that night, the most dangerous object anybody in Purewater would produce that week.

"Come back in daylight and I'll countersign the paper."

---

The Forge Quarter starts before the sun does.

It is High Isle's eastern point and it is hammers, a dozen of them going at different speeds out of different sheds, and under that the bellows-roar and the shriek of something being drawn. Coal smoke, hot iron, quench-water, and from the tanneries at the north end a thing that gets into the back of your throat and lives there. Grit on everything. It is the only quarter in Purewater where the water runs black, and the dwarves who run half of it have been complaining about that for two hundred years and about six other things for considerably longer.

Kag took a cookshop bench at six with a bowl and a view of three sheds and sent Pell in with the rubbing.

He was *good* at it. Hands empty, same words every time — *Kag'd like to know who cut this, please* — and he never touched anything. Three men sent him off. The fourth didn't.

The fourth was Orsk & Daughter, cutters and engravers, four generations on the water at the end of the founders' lane. Old Orsk was seventy and didn't come out from the back any more. It was the daughter who came to the door with the rubbing held up close to her eyes, fifty-odd, arms like a cooper's, and she was not pleased.

Her name was Hild.

"Where's the knife?"

"On a floor."

"Whose floor?"

"A girl's, at the Sylph's Embrace. He opened her from the collarbone to the hip with it and went out of a window."

Hild stood in her own doorway with the slip in her fingers for a long moment.

"Girl live?"

"She lived."

"Right." Hild turned the slip over. "Then you can have it."

---

She went to the ledger first, because dwarves go to the ledger first. It was chained to the bench and it had four generations in it and she ran a blunt finger down a column and her whole face changed.

"I said nine weeks. That's wrong, I was going off the job and not off the book."

She turned it round and tapped the line.

*4 June. Mark cut to drawing, pommel, fine. 2s 6d. Paid whole.*

Four months.

Kag looked at that date for a long time, and the bellows went next door, and the whole shed breathed in.

The column had come down the Anminster road nine weeks ago with four hundred men that weren't four hundred men and every bell in three villages going. That mark had been cut in this shed *four months before that*. Before the Baron was a rumour. Before anybody in Purewater had heard the name di Teufel said in anger.

Which meant it had not come into the city. It had been waiting here.

"Tell me about him."

"Purewater man. Fifty-five or so. Ink on the middle finger and not the first, which is a man who's held a pen fifty years." Hild shut the book. "Paid cash. In forty years I've had perhaps three men pay cash for engraving."

"What did he look like?"

"He'd cut the buttons off his coat."

"He'd *what*?"

"Cut them off. Both rows. There was thread where they'd been and he'd not tidied it." Hild put both hands flat on the bench. "I thought he'd come down in the world and was ashamed of it, and I was sorry for him, and I did him a lovely job for two and six."

She looked up.

"He was frightened the entire time he was in this shed."

---

The Pearl went dark at ten that morning and it took Marisette one hour.

By noon it was the only subject in the city, and the Fishmarket bridge was carrying ten times its usual traffic because half of an island had nowhere to be, and Kag costed it on the ice-house step before the bells had finished.

Eleven houses. Four hundred and eleven people on that island who lived off them — the girls, the cooks, the linen women, the boatmen who did nothing all year but the Pearl run, the boy who did the lamps, the four fiddlers, the woman who sold hot pastries on the quay at two in the morning to people coming out warm and generous. Four hundred and eleven, and she had the number because she had five years of that island's traffic in the back of her book and had never once had a reason to add it up.

A hundred and thirty pounds a day off the island. A week of it cost more than the entire occupation had cost the Shoals since the column came down the road.

She brought it to Crowbill, and he read it standing up, and then he sat down, which he never did.

"That's more in one night than they've cost me all week."

"Yes."

"So what's the answer?"

"You know the answer."

"Say it anyway."

"The answer is you go to the Baron with a purse and you settle." Kag kept her eyes on the page. "You pay him to keep his boys off the stalls and you cover Marisette's losses for a fortnight out of the same purse, and the island opens tomorrow and everything in my book comes right inside a month. That's the arithmetic and I did it twice and it's not close."

Crowbill waited. He was exceptionally good at waiting.

"And I'll not do it," Kag said.

"No?"

"He hasn't got four hundred men. He's got twenty-five tents and eleven of them are cold and he cannot move a sack of flour across this city without hiring one of us to carry it." She closed the book. "He is not an army sitting on us. He's a *bluff*. And the price of a bluff goes up every single time somebody pays it. You settle this one and you'll be paying the next at Michaelmas and a bigger one at Candlemas, and by the spring this is not your city, it's his, and you'll have bought that yourself with your own money."

And then, because she had come this far and there was no sense stopping:

"You asked me for a number. That's the number, and the number says settle, and I'm telling you the number's wrong and I'm telling you why. That is the whole of what I'm actually for, and in nine years you have never once let me do it."

Crowbill sat there a long while.

Then he got up and opened a drawer that Kag had never seen open in her life, and took out a slate with names on it, and turned it round.

There was one price, and he set it at the end.

"The twenty-five stays in this yard. Not on a bridge, not to Marisette, not to some Sword of the Lake with a nice face and a sad story. The day that gets out is the day they find out we've counted them — and then they have to either leave or make it true, and I don't want either of those happening on a Thursday."

---

The presentation of the entrants was that afternoon and half the city half-watched it, because by long form every competitor goes bare-headed in front of the Crown's representative for the space of a bow, and it is ceremony, and it is boring, and the Baron could not skip it without forfeiting his entry.

So they walked the champion out in harness with the rest of them, and the helm came off for about four seconds.

He was seventeen. He was far too thin for the armour he was in and he had the white skin of something kept a long time without light, and he stood in the sun in front of several hundred people and did not blink, and Kag — who was there for entirely mercenary reasons, counting colours and working out which houses had money — felt the back of her neck go cold for no reason she could have named.

Thirty yards away on the competitors' ground, an elf with a wolf at her heel made a noise that Kag heard over four hundred other people.

She got there before the old woman did.

"That's my brother," Gardwen said. She had both hands on the rail. She wasn't crying. "Ten years. They took him at seven. He's been thirty yards from me since Monday."

"What do you want?"

And Gardwen told her, fast, all of it in one breath: the Baron's son, taken, held, and traded, tonight, and she would do it herself if nobody would help her.

"No," Kag said.

"Then—"

"No, and I'll tell you why, and then I'll tell you what I'll do instead." Kag put her back against the rail so she was facing the crowd, because that is how you have a conversation on a ground with four hundred people on it. "You take a lordling hostage and you've started a war, and the war doesn't happen to you. It happens on the Shoals and the Lullwater and in the low canals where four thousand people live on planks, and not one of them has ever heard your brother's name."

"That's your answer."

"That's the *cost*. You asked me the wrong question." Kag looked out at the ground. "Here's mine. I'll bring your brother to Crowbill. Not the lordling — him. And Crowbill can hide a thing in this city that a Baron couldn't find with four hundred men, which as it happens he hasn't got, and he's been doing it since before either of us was born."

Gardwen looked at her for a long moment, and something in her face came apart and was put back differently.

"Nobody in this city has offered me anything," she said.

"Nobody in this city has asked you a question either." Kag shrugged. "I'm asking. What does he answer to?"

And the woman from the woods told her his name, and it was Cailan, and it was the first time anybody in Purewater had said it out loud in ten years.
