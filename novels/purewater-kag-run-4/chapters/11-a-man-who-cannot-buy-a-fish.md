# Chapter 11 — A Man Who Cannot Buy a Fish

BLAU HAD NOT BEEN FRIGHTENED OF ANYTHING SINCE HE WAS NINETEEN YEARS OLD, and he understood perfectly well that this was not courage — courage is a thing that costs you something every time you spend it — but an absence, burned out of him along with most of the left side of his face in a farmhouse that no longer existed, in a war that nobody in Purewater had ever heard of, on an afternoon he had stopped being able to remember the smell of about a decade ago and which he did not miss.

What the fire had actually taken was his belief that noise achieves anything.

Everybody else he had ever worked with shouted. Shouting was the whole industry. Shouting at the men, shouting at the locals, shouting at the boy who fetched the horses, shouting — God help them all — at Hanzo di Teufel, which was a thing four separate men had tried in nineteen years and which had worked precisely never. Blau did not shout and had not shouted since he was nineteen, and the effect of that on a household was extraordinary and had been quietly making him indispensable for two decades.

He was not loyal to the Baron. He wanted to be clear about that with himself, periodically, the way you check a knot.

He was loyal to finishing things.

---

On the Friday morning he came ashore at half seven and discovered, standing on the Fishmarket bridge with a purse in his hand, that he could not buy a fish.

It was not a scene. That was the thing about it and he admired it, distantly, the way he admired most competent work. The woman behind the trestle weighed whiting for a man on his left, and finished, and started on somebody else, and the queue simply rearranged itself around him the way water goes round a post. Nobody refused him. Nobody looked at him. Two porters had stopped to watch and were pretending they had not.

He had been standing there ten minutes.

Behind him his two Knights were grey in the face, and one of them had his hand on the rail, and neither of them had eaten since Wednesday.

Blau ran the situation the way he ran everything, which was as a list of facts with no adjectives attached.

The young master had walked off his father's ship on Tuesday night after a child handed him a bundle at a landing, and had not come back. That was four days. The Baron did not yet know, because Blau had not told him, because telling him was what made a mess expensive, and because the version currently circulating in the taverns — that Lord Santo had ridden out of Purewater at midnight with a Pearl girl in his brother's coat, witnessed from two bridges by about sixty people who had no reason on earth to lie about it — was the most useful thing that had happened to Blau in a fortnight and he had no intention of disturbing it.

He was, he acknowledged, entirely prepared to believe it himself. The boy had done less plausible things.

What he was not prepared to do was leave a knife lying in a canal.

---

He had hired eight of them off that bridge on Wednesday, out of his own purse, and told each one they were the only one, which was a routine precaution and which had held for about four hours on the grounds that you cannot hire eight people off one bridge and have nobody notice. He knew that when he did it. The eight were not for information. The eight were to find out who came and bought them, and by Thursday evening he knew exactly who had — because six of his slates had started coming back with the same flavour of nothing on them, which is the signature of a professional, and there was only one of those in this city.

Crowbill. Fine. That told him what he needed, which was the shape of the thing he was standing in.

The dredgers were the part he was actually pleased with.

Ten sovereigns a boat, out of his own purse again, to every dredger on that water — *any canal, any depth, no questions asked or answered* — and a drawing of the blade done himself with a fine nib, because he had held that knife on four separate occasions and could draw it with his eyes shut. The Dredgers were Guild to nobody and answered to nobody, which meant Crowbill had never been able to buy them in thirty years and neither had anybody else, and Blau had walked in and bought all of them in a morning with money.

He did not care about the body. A body in this city is a problem for the person who put it there. He cared about eleven inches of barbed iron with his employer's *sigil on the pommel*, sitting in the mud of a canal or, far worse, in somebody's kitchen drawer — because a body is an accusation against one man and that knife is an accusation against a house.

Nineteen years of cleaning up after the young master, and the boy had managed, on his way out of the world, to leave behind the only object that could hang the family.

Blau had a certain professional respect for that. It was the most effective thing Santo di Teufel had ever done.

---

The half-orc came over the bridge toward him at about twenty to eight and he watched her come the whole way.

Everybody else on that bridge had spent the morning being extremely apologetic at him. This one walked straight up, which meant she wanted something, which meant she was the first useful thing on this island in four days.

"I need to hire somebody in Purewater," he said. "Nobody in Purewater will take my money."

He turned his hands over, empty, which he did deliberately, because he had learned a long time ago that an empty hand is worth more than a threat to anybody who is any good.

"You're the first person in two days who's walked toward me. What will it cost?"

She told him she was Crowbill's and that he was therefore talking to Crowbill, and Blau said *good* and meant it, because a man with a price is a considerably simpler proposition than a man with feelings.

And then he laid it out and watched her take it without a flicker.

Six boats. Tonight, after the closing, off High Isle's seaward pier on the ebb, out through the Mouth and east along the coast — not up the Anminster road, which he did not explain and which she did not ask about. Twenty-two people and their gear. Cash on the stones before anything went aboard.

"And there's one more thing that comes with us," he said. "It goes in the bottom of a boat, it doesn't get opened, and nobody puts a hand on it but me."

In eleven minutes of talking on that bridge he did not once say *my lord*, or *the Baron*, or *di Teufel*, and he did not name anybody he worked for at all.

He noticed himself not doing it, afterwards, on the water, and turned it over for a while, and let it go.

---

Here is what nobody in Purewater knew, and what Blau had told exactly no one in nineteen years, because telling is how people lose things.

He had watched Hanzo di Teufel seat a spirit in a living body eleven times.

He had held the lamp. He had carried the case. He had put the boy back in the hold afterwards and washed the floor, and he had stood four feet away for every step of it because somebody always has to be holding something, and he had no ear for the working whatsoever and had known that about himself since he was twenty. He could not have said one word of it if you put a knife in him.

But he knew the *order*. He knew what the boy looked like on the second morning as against the third. He knew the step that had to be done every single day at first light or the edges of the seat worked loose, because he had watched it done at first light every single day for ten years, in ports and in fields and once in the back of a moving wagon.

And the man who had that knowledge in his hands did not need to be a mage.

He needed a vessel, and a chain, and a boat.

---

The bill came back at noon and it was the single most impressive thing Blau encountered in the whole of that miserable week, and he did not realise until much later that it had been built specifically to interrogate him.

It was itemised. Hulls, at forty-five the boat, because a boat that goes out past the bar at night with armed strangers in it may not come back and is therefore sold and not hired — which was correct, and which he had never seen a boatman have the nerve to write down. Polers at eight *the man*. Passage at four *the head*. One item carried in the bilge, not opened, at the contractor's risk, priced on its own, because whoever wrote it had understood inside about four seconds that it was the only line he could not do without.

Six hundred, and he would have paid nine.

And the passage line said, in words, in a boy's flat recitation on a landing step: *twenty-two heads at four the head, every head the same rate, servant, soldier, and his lordship.*

Blau read that back three times in his head and felt something he had not felt in a very long time, which was mild, genuine amusement.

Four shillings. They had priced Hanzo di Teufel, Baron of Teufel, at four shillings and put him on a list between a cook and a sergeant.

He could have let it stand. He thought about letting it stand for perhaps two seconds, and then he corrected it, because he was a professional and a professional does not go out on a night tide with a manifest that says twenty-two when it says twenty-one, and because in nineteen years he had never once knowingly left a wrong number on a piece of paper.

"Agreed, the whole of it, six hundred, cash on the stones at dusk," he told the boy in the green cap. "And tell them one line's wrong. Twenty-one heads, not twenty-two." A beat. "And the item goes in the boat first, not last."

The boy said it back twice, which was good practice, and ran off north with it.

Blau watched him go and then went and stood at the end of the Guild landing for a while, looking at the water, at a city that would not sell him bread, which he was leaving tonight with everything that mattered in the bottom of a boat.

He had not eaten in two days himself. He had not really noticed.

It did not once occur to him — and this was the only genuine mistake he made all week, and it was the same mistake every single person in that city made about her — that the woman who had written him that bill had written it in order to *ask him a question*, and that he had just answered it.
