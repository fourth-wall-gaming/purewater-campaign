# Chapter 13 — He Begs

GARDWEN HAD BEEN IN THE TREES, and she had watched them take him, and she had not come out — and everything she had done in the ten years since had been an argument with that one night, conducted alone, in the open, in weather, against a version of herself who was nine years old and would not accept any of it.

She had buried the steading. She had spoken the rites over people she had known her whole life and could no longer recognise. She had walked out of the deep woods at seventeen with a wolf who chose her and a conviction that everybody she met treated as a kindness they were not going to argue with, which was that her brother had survived, and was alive, and was somewhere, and was seven.

And on Wednesday afternoon, on a competitors' ground in a city built on water, a boy had been walked out in harness and the helm had come off for four seconds and she had been right for ten years and it had not helped at all.

That was the part nobody warns you about either. Being right is not the same as getting him back. Being right is just the moment you stop having anywhere to put the hope.

---

So on Thursday evening she went down to the seaward rocks at High Isle and took her boots off.

She set them side by side on a flat stone above the tideline and tied them together at the tops, the way you do at a bathing place, and she remembered doing it and thought it was a strange thing to be tidy about, and did it anyway.

Ruhi came to the edge of the water and stopped, because Ruhi had spent four years going everywhere and would not go there.

"Stay," Gardwen said, which she had never once had to say to that animal.

The wolf sat down on the high rock with her forelegs braced and did not make a sound, and Gardwen went into the October sea off the north-east of a city with a hundred and forty tents of enemy eleven miles east of her, entirely alone, on the grounds that her brother was in a ship and nobody was doing anything about it.

She was, in her own considered opinion, a fucking idiot. She had thought so at the time. She went anyway.

---

The cold takes your breath in a fist and then it takes your hands, in that order, and after about an hour it starts taking your judgement, which is the dangerous one.

She got to the stern chains and got her forearms over them and hung there in the dark under the counter of the Baron's ship with the water going up and down her by a foot and a half, and she found the one piece of good fortune available that night, which was that the hull was *warm* down there, because the galley flue ran down the stern and the planking above the waterline had heat in it.

Warm planking carries sound.

And so a druid of the deep woods, hanging off a mooring chain in the sea with her lips going blue, put her ear against two inches of oak and listened to the most important quarter of an hour in the entire history of her family.

---

He came down at about nine.

She knew it was him because the whole ship changed — the small noises of twenty people stopped for a moment, the way a house stops when the master comes in — and then there were boots on a companionway and a door, and then a voice.

And here is the thing she was not remotely prepared for.

He was *kind*.

That is the word. She used it twice when she finally told it and she never afterwards found a better one and she hated it every time. Baron Hanzo di Teufel went down below the waterline of his own ship to a boy he had stolen at seven years old, and he sat with him for a quarter of an hour, and he talked to him low and steady and gently, the way you settle a horse that has been frightened by weather. There was no cruelty in it anywhere. It would have been so much easier if there had been.

He said the boy's name. He said *Cailan*, out loud, more than once, which meant that somewhere in ten years he had bothered to keep it, which meant he had chosen to.

And then he said the other name, three times, and Gardwen got it wrong because of the water and the oak, but what she heard was *Thuban*.

And something answered him.

Not a seventeen-year-old. It was a grown man's voice and it came out of that chest wrong, the way a note comes out of a cracked bell, and what it was doing — underneath the Baron's voice, continuously, the entire quarter of an hour, never once stopping —

— was asking to be let go.

Not screaming. Not threatening. Asking. Over and over, patiently, the way a man in a cell asks for water, and the Baron talked gently across the top of it and did not acknowledge it even once, because he had been doing this every night for ten years and had long ago stopped hearing it.

Gardwen hung on a chain in the black sea and listened to her brother's body beg.

---

She lost the last of it, which was the part about what would be wanted tomorrow, because the tide came up and she could not get her ear back onto the planking and her hands had stopped being hands.

That was when she slipped, and the chain rang.

Lanterns came out along the pier, four of them and then six. Men with boat-hooks going down into the water by the mooring chains. A light held over the side and walked the whole length of the hull, twice, and a voice giving orders that never once rose.

She went under the counter and stayed there for two hours in the cold with hooks going past her, holding on with her forearms because her fingers had finished, and thinking about absolutely nothing at all except the next breath, which is what the sea does to you and is, on balance, a mercy.

---

And after he had gone, when it was just the water and the ship's timbers and the ordinary noise of a vessel at night — she heard the other thing.

Somebody in there was crying.

A boy. Not the other one. A boy, seventeen, on his own, in the dark, below a waterline, after his father had gone up the companionway and shut the door.

He was in there the whole time.

He had been in there the whole time, for ten years, listening to all of it.

---

She did not remember the swim back and she did not remember the rocks.

What she remembered afterwards, and what she never told anybody except once, badly, on a wash-room floor, was the wolf coming down off the high rock in four jumps and going into the shallows to her chest and getting a mouthful of her shirt, and an enormous grey shape coming off the stones into the water to her thighs to take the rest of her weight, and somebody saying *I've got her*.

And then Magda — sixty-odd years old and a head and a half shorter than any of them — carrying her up Constantine's water steps herself, and refusing, with a flat and appalling calm, to give her to anybody else on the way.

They got her onto the wash-room floor with every blanket in the house and the firebox roaring, and Constantine did it slowly, because she had seen what hot does to men pulled out of the Mouth in February.

Magda sat on the boards with Gardwen's head on her thigh and rubbed her hands between her own, hard, the way you bring a lamb round, and said the entire time, in a completely level voice, that she was a bloody fool, and that she would be up in an hour, and that this was nothing.

She did not believe one word of it. Gardwen could hear that she did not.

It was better than an hour before the shaking started, which Constantine said was the good sign.

---

She got hold of the half-orc's collar with a hand that could not grip and said it straight into her face, because it had to come out first, before the cold, before the names, before any of the rest of it.

"He *begs*."

A beat.

And then, when her mouth would work again, the rest — the quarter of an hour, the kindness, the voice out of the cracked bell, the boy crying on his own in the dark afterwards.

"He calls him by his name," she said. "Cailan. He says it every night."

She lay on her back on a wash-room floor with a wolf across her feet and her eyes shut and made the one deduction that none of the rest of them had got to, and made it out loud, slowly, because her mouth was still bad.

"If they take him — wherever he goes, the chain goes on him. In a cell, or a box in some clerk's cupboard, or on a body. And there's nobody holding the other end. And whatever that is in my brother has been asking to be let out for ten years."

The firebox ticked.

"So it comes off first," Gardwen said. "Then you can do whatever you like with him."
