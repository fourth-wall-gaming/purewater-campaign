# Chapter 11 — Crowbill's Town

Mariarta copied the invoice out in her own hand at Marisette's low table, a good round hand with no flourishes, every line in the same order. She copied the struck line too. *Twenty-two heads*, ruled through, and *twenty-one* written above it.

"A copy in my hand is better than the thing itself," she said. "If I had the original off your table you'd have missed it and come looking, and he'll know that. A woman who copied what she saw is a woman who couldn't take it."

She capped the ink and found the last hole herself.

"He'll ask why I brought it to him. And the answer is that his officers have run fourteen nights of credit at the Promise of Heaven and I have it all written down, and if that household goes east on a tide tonight I'm carrying the lot."

She folded it small.

"Captain Atarer has never been in a house on this island and never will be. He doesn't drink and he doesn't sit down." She looked up. "But I've been asked to the closing by two of his officers, and he'll be stood at the back of his own men on the north terrace. I've watched him stand there four days running."

---

High Isle's seaward pier at dusk was black stone, four boat-lengths long, a ship lying alongside with two lights, and the ebb already running.

Six boats came in out of the dark at ten past six and tied up.

Kag did not hide. She stood on the stones in the open with a lamp beside her, because she was the contractor and there was six hundred to be handed over before anything went in a boat.

They came off at twenty to seven, and they came off well. No shouting. Bundles first, then people, in ones and twos, down the plank and along the stones in a line that did not bunch.

Twenty-one of them. She counted.

Blau came down third and walked straight to her and counted six hundred into her hands in coin, on the stones, in front of his own people, and did not hurry it and did not ask her to count it after.

"Boats look right," he said.

The last thing off that ship came up out of the hold on ropes. A chest, near seven feet, rope beckets on both ends. It took four of them and they struggled with it and it was far heavier than a chest that size. It did not shift inside and it made no sound at all. The underside was wet and steaming in the cold, because it had spent its life below a waterline.

Blau put both hands on it going down the plank and did not take them off until it was in the bilge of the second boat.

Kag's boat. Kag's man on the rope.

---

The skiff went off the end of the pier like a man ferrying a message about boats. Four minutes across the point. A girl with a tray crossing a slope in the dark. A cup offered at an elbow.

They loaded well. Bundles down, people after, weight spread, a man of hers in each stern saying *further forward* and *sit down* in the voice you use on people who do not know boats.

The ebb was going hard. Seven o'clock came and went.

Five boats loaded. The sixth took the last four and their gear, and that was twenty-one.

There was nothing at all on the path up from the terraces.

"Right," Blau said, not loudly, to the whole pier. "Cast off."

Kag put her head up and walked back along the stones with her hand raised, the way a man does when he has heard something over water.

"You've been paid," Blau said. "You want me on these stones another ten minutes and I've no idea why, and I'd rather not find out on a falling tide."

And then everybody else on the pier heard it too.

Boots on stone, up on the path — one man, running, and running the way a man runs when he is not thinking about the ground.

Baron Hanzo di Teufel came off the path at a dead run in the clothes he had stood in front of a Prince in an hour before. No harness. No sword. No household. One shoe gone somewhere. He was fifty-one and he had run the better part of a mile.

He stopped dead at the head of the line of boats.

He was not looking at Blau. He was looking at the second boat and the wet black chest in the bottom of it.

---

"Take it out of the boat," he said. His voice was wrecked from the running and it was not loud. "Put it back aboard and I will not have heard any of this."

"No, my lord."

"You'd never have got him home. You've been telling him he was going home for ten years and the boy believes it and you don't."

Blau got out of the boat and put himself between the Baron and the second boat and put a hand flat on his chest, and Hanzo walked into it and stopped.

Twenty-one people in five boats did not move, and not one of them said a word, because they were his household and he was still their Baron and none of them had worked out yet which way it went.

Then Hanzo looked past Blau's shoulder at the chest lying in the bilge with the water running under it, and he said a name.

Not loudly. Low and steady and kind, the voice you use on a horse, and it carried the whole length of that pier because everything else on it had gone silent.

"*Thuban.*"

The chest moved.

Once. A single heavy shift, and the boat under it dipped and came back and the water slapped along the stones, and both of Crowbill's men in that boat were holding their poles badly.

He said it again and added three words in a language that put the hair up on her arms.

Something inside seven feet of wet oak took hold of the lid from underneath.

---

Kag went round the outside of the lamps with the cutter in her left hand and the thumb loop on.

His eyes came off the chest and went over her once and went straight back to the boat. A dock half-orc moving on a dock. He had not got room in his head that night for whatever she was, and he never had had, and it was the first time in her life it was worth anything.

She took three steps and was on him.

Left hand inside the open collar. Her fingers got under the ring — there was the slack, half a finger's worth where nine weeks of this city had taken the flesh off him — and she hauled it up off the skin and held it there, and he came back against her like a sack.

The tongue went under. Steel between iron and throat.

One. Two. Three.

The ring did not part.

What it did was bite. There was a bright notch cut in the black iron the width of a fingernail, silver in the lamplight, and the wheel sat in the bottom of it and the ring was still a ring.

*Men have stood there sawing at a thing for a minute and a half and been killed doing it.*

Down the pier, Blau turned round. In the second boat the lid of the chest lifted three inches and stopped, held from underneath.

And the Baron of Teufel, hanging off her left hand with his windpipe half shut, got both arms up in front of him and started to say something that was not in any language spoken on that water.

She tried to put her head into the back of his skull and could not get it past her own arm.

He got the words out.

---

Everything stopped except her eyes and her ears.

Her left fist was inside his collar with the ring hauled up off his throat and her right thumb was through the loop with the wheel in its notch, and that was where they were, and they were not going anywhere, and neither was she.

Out on the black water somebody said one quiet word and nothing happened to him at all. He did not even turn his head towards it.

And then four crossbows went off out of the dark at about nine feet, because the standing order had been *not unless a man starts casting, and then until he stops*.

Two of them found him and both found the same place.

Baron Hanzo di Teufel stopped being a man mid-word. He went down instantly and completely, straight down, and he did not fall away from her, because her fist was still shut in his collar. He hung off her frozen arm with his whole weight on her knuckles and his chin on her wrist.

The ring was six inches under her eyes with one bright notch in it.

She could not turn the wheel. She could not let go. She could not say a word.

---

The skiff came in out of the dark on the seaward side without a lamp, close enough that the ship's lights picked it up, and a figure in a plain coat stood up in the stern in front of twenty-one people, Blau, six boat crews and whatever was standing on the stones in a wet shift.

Three words, and a hand cut sideways through the air.

It went out of her all at once and it was horrible, and she and the Baron went down onto the wet stone together in a heap with her fist still shut in his collar.

She got a knee under her.

Three turns. On the third the wheel went through and the notch became a gap, and the ring sprang — it sprang, like a thing that had been under tension for ten years — and the two cut ends jumped apart under her thumb and it came off the dead man's throat into her left hand.

There was a locket on it the size of a thumbnail. It was warm. It was much warmer than anything on that pier.

Above her the thing standing in the wet shift made a sound. Not a cry and not a word. Something going out of a chest, all of it, the way a man breathes out at the end of a very long day, and it went on longer than any lungs that size should hold.

Then the grown man's voice said one thing, and it was not to the pier and not to the dead man and not to anybody who had ever been afraid of it.

"Hold him. He'll not know how to stand."

The boy folded.

She got an arm up and took about half of him and he came down onto the stones across her knees, seventeen years old and eight stone of nothing, and his eyes were open and moving and he was looking at the lamps and the boats and the sky like somebody who had never seen any of it.

He made a noise. It was a boy's noise.

And behind her on the stones Gardwen came apart entirely, and Magda let her go, and she got there on her hands and knees.

---

Nobody in the boats had drawn a blade. There was not a single sword out on that water.

Twenty-one people sat in five boats with every possession they owned under the thwarts, on a running ebb, forty feet out from a pier, and the poles were in Purewater hands and not one of them could crew anything.

Two were standing. One of those was being sick over the side. An older man in the third boat had taken his hat off, which nobody had told him to do.

Blau came down the pier. He was not looking at his Baron and he was not looking at the boy.

"That," he said, "is mine."

"Since when."

"Since he was forty-four and I was nineteen." He did not move. "I have watched that man seat a spirit in a living body eleven times and I have carried that locket in my own hand while he slept. There is not another person on this earth who knows what you are holding."

A beat.

"And you are going to need to know, tonight, because that boy is not finished."

---

He put his hands behind his back to be tied, which was the last unnerving thing he did.

"He was in that boy ten years. A seat that deep is a hole cut in a person, and it does not close because the tenant walked out of it, and there is a step my lord did every single morning of those ten years to keep the edges of it from working loose."

The ebb ran along the stones.

"Nobody is going to do that tomorrow morning. Or the morning after. That is the free part, because you'd have found it out yourself by Thursday and by Thursday would be too late."

For the rest of it — what the thing on the chain was, what it did to whoever carried it, what had to be done to the boy before the fourth morning — he wanted one boat, one man on the pole, and the sea.

"And I don't want the locket. You'd not give it me and I've stopped wanting things I can't have."

---

She said the other thing loud enough for five boats and it carried a long way on water.

Nobody argued with any of it. The older man in the third boat put his hat back on.

Then Crowbill's men leaned on the poles, because they had been paid six hundred in coin on those stones to take twenty-one people east on the ebb, and Crowbill's people do what Crowbill said.

The five boats went out past the pier head into the black and the sound of them went with them, and inside two minutes High Isle's seaward pier was as quiet as it had been at six o'clock.

---

They brought him wrapped in a boat sail and he weighed almost nothing, which was the last thing she learned about Hanzo di Teufel.

Crowbill came down to the water himself, which he had not done once all week. He looked at the sail and at the man tied in the bottom of the boat and at the black ring lying open in her palm with the small warm thing on it and at a seventeen-year-old asleep against his sister with Magda's coat over him.

"Right," he said.

The body went in the ice-house on the stone under sacking, because the run goes out over the bar at four every morning of the year, and both of them would leave the city the same way inside two days and neither would ever be found.

He took the six hundred off her, counted it, and put two of every five back in her hands without comment, which was not a thing he had ever done in front of witnesses.

Then he stopped at the top of the steps.

"They heard you on that pier. Twenty-six people, and five of them were mine, and mine will tell it."

He almost smiled.

"Go and sleep, Kag. You're going to be hearing that one back off strangers by Friday."
