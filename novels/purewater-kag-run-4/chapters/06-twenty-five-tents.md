# Chapter 6 — Twenty-Five Tents

KAG STEPPED OFF A BOAT ONTO GROUND FOR THE FIRST TIME IN ELEVEN YEARS and very nearly fell over, because the world had stopped moving up and down and nobody had warned her that it would.

Grass. Mud. A hedge.

And the smell, which was the second shock and somehow larger than the first: horse, and woodsmoke, and latrine, and cut grass, and not one single note of tar or fish or lye anywhere in it. She stood at the top of the Vantt landing with her boots in actual earth and had the strangest four seconds of the decade.

It had taken until noon and four fares, the long way round, east across the Mouth with the sea on the right.

---

Rew's chandlery was a long shed at the landing, and Rew was sixty and had a bad hip and one look at the size of her told him exactly whose rope he had been buying for nine years.

He gave her an hour, as advertised, and he gave it to her by talking while he worked, so that anybody looking up from the meadow saw a chandler and a large customer discussing cordage, which they were, for most of it.

And the meadow was below the house, laid out in rows, in full view of the shed door.

So she counted, because that was why she had come.

**Twenty-five tents. Forty-one horses. One farrier.**

She counted it three times and it did not change.

---

*Four hundred*, Tarrent had said, cheerfully, with his hand on his belt, on a bridge, in front of six carts and an old man. *About four hundred camped out east.*

There was a servants' tail and a cook-line and a string of remounts, and generously — very generously — you might get to a hundred and fifty souls on that meadow, of whom perhaps ninety could hold a sword.

Not four hundred. Not a third of it.

And the entire city of Purewater — forty thousand people, eleven bridges gone quiet, a rope-seller who had stopped asking for credit, an eighty-year-old woman short on her ice for the first time in her life — had spent nine weeks being occupied by a number that nobody had ever counted, because there is no road east and nobody goes, and four hundred is not a fact, it is a rumour in harness.

She wanted to be closer. She wanted the inside of the rows and she wanted to know which of those tents were cold.

"Can I pass for a labour hand?" she said. "Somebody who's supposed to be there?"

---

Rew looked at her for a while, and then at the meadow, and then did the sum she had been hoping he would do.

"They're taking hands on," he said. "Have been since yesterday. Local men, by the day, cash at dusk. Mucking out, water, wood, moving feed. There's eleven or twelve down there already and half of them came off my landing this morning."

He scratched his jaw.

"Nobody's checking anything. There's no clerk and no list. A big lad turns up at the horse-lines with his sleeves up and starts shifting something heavy, and the only question anybody asks is *whose barrow is that*."

He went back to the rope.

"Which is not to say I'd advise it."

---

Here is the thing, and it had taken forty years to be worth anything.

She would not have to act.

She had spent her entire life being looked at exactly once by people deciding what she was, and every single one of them had got it wrong in precisely the same direction — muscle, hire, thing-that-lifts — and it had cost her a trade, a name, and every door in Purewater that opens on charm. Forty years of it. She had never once been able to use it for anything.

There was a water butt by the shed that took two men.

She put it on one shoulder, got a hand on the rim, and walked down the slope into the meadow at the exact speed of a woman who has been told to put something somewhere and would rather not have been.

And the camp let her in the way a gate doesn't.

A man at the cook-line looked up, saw a barrel and an arm holding it, and went back to his bowl. Somebody else said "over by the lines" without checking whether she had been asking. A boy with an armful of tack changed direction so as not to get in her way and did not look at her face, because nobody looks at the face of a thing that is carrying something heavy.

Nobody had ever made it that easy. Nobody had ever made *anything* that easy.

She walked into an enemy camp in daylight with a barrel on her shoulder, and the only thing that got her in was the thing she had hated for forty years.

---

Between two and three the meadow was dead. Rew had been right. The farrier's fire was banked and the tent rows were empty and there was a queue at the cook-tent forty yards off with every voice in the camp in it.

So she walked the lines with a barrel on her shoulder and counted properly.

**Twenty-five tents in rows, and eleven of them cold.** No gear out, no boots at the flaps, guy lines slack. On a freezing October afternoon with every chimney in Purewater going. Those men were in the city.

Forty-one horses, and eleven of those lame or resting, and the farrier with a pile of thrown shoes beside him that said he had been losing a fight since Anminster.

And down at the end, past the picket line, two young men in Purewater clothes were shovelling out a horse-line into a barrow.

---

She put the butt down where a butt might go, straightened up, and looked at the sky like a woman whose back hurt. Which it did.

The one who knew her kept shovelling and spoke to the muck.

"Don't." Barely moving his mouth. "He's been there since eleven."

Twenty feet off, a knight sat on a tack-box picking at a stitched seam on his sword-grip and had not looked up.

"You all right?"

"We've not been hit." Shovel. Shovel. "We've not been let go either. They took the boats off us at four and they took *us* at four and nobody's said a word about wages or when."

Two more loads. The other lad had not looked up once.

"Tam," he said eventually. Giving her his name, because he knew she would need it, and because she was the only person who had asked him anything all day.

And then, much quieter and a great deal faster:

"We were tied up asleep. There was a boat came down the channel about four. One man, poling it like he'd never held a pole, and he went past us *soaked through*. Head to foot. Coat and all, in October."

Shovel.

"Didn't stop. Went east, up the far side."

Shovel.

He straightened to shift the barrow, and while his back was to the knight he said the last of it to the sky, in the same voice he'd have used about the weather.

"Kag, we never said we saw anything. Nobody asked us. They just came and got us."

---

Twenty feet away the man on the tack-box had stopped picking at the seam.

So Kag stretched — which at her height takes a while and involves a good deal of shoulder — and then turned her head and cleared one nostril onto the grass, thoroughly, with a finger, in the manner of a woman who has never once in her life considered that anybody might be watching.

Then she looked at the sky again, spat, put the butt up on the other shoulder, and walked off up the lines.

It worked because it was always going to work. The knight watched about four seconds of it and went back to the seam, and by the time she was past the farrier's fire he had forgotten anything had happened, because nothing had: a labourer took a break, was disgusting, and went back to work.

She had been invisible for the first time in her life, it had cost her nothing, and she did not much like how it felt.

---

Rew did not answer straight away when she asked him to keep an eye on the two boys. He finished the coil, hung it, and started another one.

"I'll tell you if something happens to them." Carefully, and the care was the point. "I'll not go *asking*. I'm a chandler with a bad hip, and I sell tar to the man in the big tent and rope to your lot, and the day either of them thinks I'm doing anything else I'm finished at sixty with nothing."

He tied off the coil.

"But I'm at that landing every day and I see who goes down the lines and who comes back up. And if two Purewater lads stop coming back up, I'll know within the hour."

He looked at her properly for the first time since she had arrived.

"And I'll get word to Hessa the same day. Not to you — to Hessa, through the rope order, the way it's always gone. You never came out here and I never saw you and there's nothing on any paper anywhere."

A pause.

"That's the whole of what I'll do. Don't come back here, Kag."

It was not unfriendly. It was a man telling her the exact size of what he had, which was the most honest thing anybody had said to her in two days.

---

Hessa listened to the whole of it without writing anything down, which from her was alarming.

Then she said: "Say the numbers again."

"Twenty-five tents. Eleven cold. Forty-one horses, eleven of them lame."

"And no second company."

"No second company. There never was one. The one on the bridge told me four hundred, and I wrote four hundred down, and I built two days of work on it."

She sat back.

"That's the best thing anybody's brought him this year, and yesterday's four sheets was the best thing anybody'd brought him in a year, so I'm going to have to think of a new way of saying it."

---

Then the rest of it, and she went quiet for that.

"East," she repeated. "Four in the morning, soaked, poling like a farmhand, going *east*."

"East is the ship."

"East is the ship." She looked at the table. "And a Pearl boy running off the Pearl at four in the morning does not go east. There's nothing east for a Pearl boy. He goes home, or he goes to ground on the Shoals with people who'd hide him, and either way he goes *west*."

She got there about a second after Kag had.

"So it wasn't Randall."

---

And then the other end of it arrived, and took the temperature of that back room down several degrees.

"Kag. Your witness is standing in a horse-line on Vantt's meadow with a man watching him, and he can't come here, and you can't go back there, and Rew won't ask a single question."

She counted it off on her fingers, which she never did.

Marisette had a man in a bag on the strength of three women's memory of a face, on an island she had shut, with the whole water saying *that poor boy* sadly.

"He's the boss," Kag said. "We tell him first. Then if he's willing, I'll go and tell her."

A beat.

"I've always wanted to visit the Pearl."

Hessa did not laugh, but something happened at the corner of her mouth.

"You'd be the first of us through that door who wasn't delivering something."

---

Crowbill was not on the roof. Hessa sent two boys and he was in the back of the chandlery inside twenty minutes, which was the fastest Kag had ever seen him move and was a piece of information all by itself.

He heard the whole thing standing up.

The numbers first. He took those with nothing on his face at all, and at the end of them he said "Twenty-five," once, to himself, and then nothing for a while.

Then the rest. The wet man. East. The hour. Six soldiers taking the only two witnesses off the water before breakfast.

When she was done he asked exactly three questions.

"The lad who told you — would he say it to somebody who wasn't you?"

"Don't know. He's frightened and he's in a field."

"Mm. Second. Does Marisette know any part of this?"

"No."

"Third." He looked up. "Does anybody else know you were out east today?"

"Rew. And he'd rather chew his own leg off."

He thought about it for a long time. Long enough that Hessa went back to work and the light through the shutter moved.

"Go and tell her. Tonight, before she does anything."

He held up a hand before Kag had moved.

"And understand what you're carrying, because it isn't mercy and I'd rather you didn't sell it as mercy. She's about to kill a Pearl boy for something a di Teufel did, in front of her own people, on the word of three women who love him."

"Got it, boss." A beat. "Thank you."
