# Chapter 8 — Nine Steps

The Lily Garden's back water-stair was four feet of wet stone and nine steps down to a dead end, with a gate at the top you could lift off its pins with one hand.

Hesper's skiff was at the foot of it with a line out and a rod propped in it, and Hesper was a shape in a coat doing nothing at all.

Tess came down at the bell in Emmeralda's grey, sat on the fourth step with her back three-quarters to the gate, and stopped moving in a way that actual people cannot do.

Deri and Fen were on the stone at the top, one either side, forty feet apart, arguing about a horse.

Then it was the waiting, which is the part nobody writes down. The water slapped. Somebody two houses over was having the same argument they had had the night before. The wet line came up out of the dark from Hesper's hand to Kag's and she could feel the boat breathe on it.

Full dark. She could see all of it.

---

Twenty minutes past the bell a skiff came up the canal from the seaward end with no lamp on it, moving slow, and there were three people in it.

Two of them were poling, one fore and one aft, standing up and easy with it, which meant they were Purewater men, because nobody out of a country with roads stands up in a boat.

The third sat amidships with a cloak over his head.

At twenty feet the cloak came off, because he could not help wanting to see the steps.

Nineteen. Dark hair. A plain brown thing over something that had cost a great deal more, and one hand flat inside the front of it.

The man on the aft pole was Tam.

Tam, off the Guild landing, lifted at four in the morning three days before and not seen on that water since, standing up in a boat poling the Baron's son to a dead-end stair and looking back over his shoulder the whole way up the canal.

Kag pulled the line.

---

It came tight and somewhere below her in the dark Hesper said one word, not loudly, the way you say somebody's name across a room.

Tess went over sideways on the fourth step with her cheek on the stone. Out. Breathing.

Santo did not.

He stood there a second and a half looking at a girl who had just gone to sleep at his feet at the bottom of a dead-end stair, and then he ran, and he ran the wrong way. Down, at the water, because the top of the steps had two men on it and water had been the way out of everything his whole life.

Kag came down nine wet steps.

He got one foot onto the gunwale of Hesper's skiff and the skiff did what a skiff does under one foot, and then there was a forearm across his chest and that was the whole of it. Ten stone of nineteen-year-old in a very good coat. He kicked once and stopped.

The letter came out of the front of his coat with the comb still folded in the spoiled page.

Hesper had already sat back down and picked up the rod.

Six feet away, Tam stood in the other boat with the pole in his hands and his mouth open.

He had known Kag eleven years.

Kag winked at him over the top of Santo's head, and Tam shut his mouth and after a moment jerked his chin about a quarter of an inch and turned his face out to the canal like a man who has come out to look at the weather.

---

The room they had ready at the Mother of Pearl was off the landing with a stone floor and a drain in the middle of it, which is where they washed linen, and there was one chair carried in from somewhere.

Santo went into it hard enough that it moved.

Marisette came in behind and did not hurry and sat down on a stool by the wall and arranged her skirts as though it were any other evening in any other room in her house. Fen put a lamp on the floor. The light went up the walls.

Emmeralda walked the four doors herself.

She came down in the big coat with her hair off at the jaw and asked to be let in and Kag opened the door for her.

He heard the door and looked up expecting Marisette.

His whole face changed. He came forward against the cord as far as it let him and made a sound through the gag that had a shape to it and the shape was her name, and he did it three times. He thought the letter had worked.

Emmeralda stood in the doorway and looked at him with the lamp on him.

"Take that out of his mouth."

They took it out. He worked his jaw twice.

"Em." His voice had not finished breaking. "I got your letter."

Then he waited. He waited the way you wait for somebody to pass you something at a table.

She crossed the stone and stopped a yard in front of the chair.

"It wasn't meant to be like that," he said, low and quick, as though the two of them were alone and the rest were furniture he had not got round to. "You know it wasn't."

She went on looking at him.

"I can still take you out of here. Tonight. I've the boat."

Emmeralda unfastened the coat.

She did not do it fast and she did not do it for effect. She opened it and held it open and stood there in a shift in a cold room, and the line he had put down her went from her collarbone to her hip, closed now, and pink, and perfectly straight, and there was a great deal of it.

He turned his head away.

She waited. He turned his head away again and she waited again, and the third time he looked, because there was nowhere in that room that was not her.

Then she closed the coat and held it shut at the throat with one hand the way she had all day and walked out past Kag.

She stopped in the doorway with her back to the room.

"He's about five foot nine."

---

Crowbill was in the back of an ice-house on the Shoals at two in the morning with a lamp and a blanket over his knees, because that was where he was at two in the morning.

He heard all of it. He did not ask a question until she had stopped.

"When Marisette has him in a chair, I want the asking," he said. "The Baron on a boat and gone inside the week, and not one word said out loud about anybody's face."

A beat.

"And you'll be the one who tells the two of them that. Not me."

---

She told them. She told them the whole of it, and Marisette held her glass halfway up for about as long as it takes a cat to cross a room and then drank it.

"Eleven houses are going to ask me why we opened and nothing was said," Marisette said. "And I shall have to let them think what they think."

Emmeralda did not move off the wall through any of it.

Later, in the ice-house again, Kag gave Crowbill the other half.

"He's not going to trade for that boy," she said. "He's got a champion and a city to take and one son he's never once been seen to like, and if we hand the boy back the only thing we've bought is his attention. He'll not pay. He'll remember."

"Then what."

"Then Marisette gets her answers out of him, and then he's fish food, and the twelve of us know he rode out of town with a girl — which two bridges of people watched happen on Tuesday night and every one of them believes, because Randall wore the coat and Tess wore the grey and nobody had to be told to lie about anything."

Crowbill sat with it while the ice ticked.

"The run goes out over the bar at four every morning of the year with nobody watching it, because it's ice," he said at last. "But the pots are off the Lost Isle and the men who lift them are mine and they don't look in."

He trimmed the lamp without looking at it.

"And you'll do it yourself. Not Deri. Not Fen. Not some fellow I'd have to pay and then worry about."

He was entirely pleasant about it, the way he had been all week.

"Eleven people can carry a story about a boy who ran off. Twelve people cannot carry a murder. So it's one pair of hands, and they're yours."
