# Chapter 8 — Nine Steps

KAG HAD NEVER IN HER LIFE RUN ANYTHING, and standing at the top of the Lily Garden's back water-stair in the dark with a wet rope in her hand and six people's evenings arranged around a thing she had thought of on Tuesday, she discovered that the sensation was not power at all. It was closer to being very cold. Every part of it was somebody else's body. Tess on the fourth step in Emmeralda's grey. Hesper eight feet below in a boat with a rod propped up, pretending to fish, in a coat and a hat and a complete absence of anything describable. Deri and Fen on the stone at the top, forty feet apart, arguing quietly about a horse in the way of two married men who had been told to look bored and were overdoing it.

The whole plan was made of people, and people are not rope, and you cannot test them beforehand.

The stair itself was four feet of wet stone and nine steps down to a dead end, with a gate at the top you could lift off its pins with one hand, and there was no lamp past the gate, which was the entire reason it had been chosen.

And then it was the waiting, which is the part nobody puts in the stories.

The water slapped. Somebody two houses over was having precisely the same argument they had been having the night before. The wet line came up out of the dark from Hesper's hand to hers and she could feel the boat breathe on it, once every few seconds, like something asleep.

Full dark, and she could see all of it, because she always could.

---

Twenty minutes past the bell a skiff came up from the seaward end with no lamp on it, moving slow, three people in it.

Two of them were poling, one fore and one aft, standing up and easy with it — Purewater men, because nobody out of a country with roads stands up in a boat — and the third sat amidships with a cloak up over his head.

At twenty feet the cloak came off, because he could not help wanting to see the steps.

Nineteen. Dark hair. A plain brown thing over something that had cost a great deal more, and one hand flat inside the front of it.

And the man on the aft pole was Tam.

Tam. Off the Guild landing, four o'clock Thursday morning, not seen on this water since, with a wife who had stood on wet stone since six. Standing up in that boat poling the Baron's son to a dead-end stair and looking back over his shoulder the whole way up the canal like a man being marched.

There is a particular kind of anger that arrives without heat and simply rearranges what you are prepared to do.

Kag pulled the line.

---

It came tight, and somewhere below her in the dark Hesper said one word — not loudly, the way you say a name across a room — and Tess went over sideways on the fourth step with her cheek on the stone, out, breathing, exactly as advertised.

Santo di Teufel did not.

He stood there a second and a half looking at a girl who had just fallen asleep at his feet at the bottom of a dead-end stairwell, and then he ran, and he ran the *wrong way* — down, at the water, because the top of the steps had two men on it and because water had been the way out of everything his entire life.

Kag came down nine wet steps.

He got one foot onto the gunwale of Hesper's skiff and the skiff did what a skiff does under one foot, and then there was a forearm across his chest and that was the whole of it. Ten stone of nineteen-year-old in a very good coat. He kicked once and gave it up, and the letter came out of the front of his coat with the comb still folded in the spoiled page.

Hesper had already sat back down and picked up the rod.

Six feet away Tam stood in the other boat with the pole in his hands and his mouth open, and he had known Kag eleven years.

She winked at him over the top of the boy's head.

Tam shut his mouth, and after a moment jerked his chin about a quarter of an inch, and turned his face out to the canal like a man who has come out to look at the weather.

---

They had a room ready off the landing at the Mother of Pearl with a stone floor and a drain in the middle of it, because it was where they washed the linen, and one chair carried in from somewhere else.

He went into it hard enough that it moved.

Marisette came in behind and did not hurry and sat down on a stool by the wall and arranged her skirts as though it were any other Thursday in any other room in her house. Fen put the lamp on the floor and the light went up the walls, which made it worse, and Kag noticed him do it and thought: *you have done this before.*

Emmeralda walked the four doors herself.

She came in the big coat with her hair off at the jaw, and asked to be let in, and Kag looked at her for a second and then opened the door, and that was a decision that Kag would turn over a great many times afterwards and never once regret.

He heard the door and looked up expecting the madame.

And his whole face changed — not fear, the other thing, the worse thing — and he came forward against the cord as far as it let him and made a sound through the gag that had a shape to it, and the shape was her name, and he did it three times.

He thought the letter had worked.

He thought she had come.

Emmeralda stood in the doorway and looked at him with the lamp on him, and then she said, quite evenly, "Take that out of his mouth."

---

They took it out and he worked his jaw twice and there was a red line across both cheeks.

"Em." His voice had not finished breaking, which was the single most obscene thing in that room. "I got your letter."

Then he waited. He genuinely *waited*, with his hands tied behind a chair on a wet stone floor, the way you wait for somebody to pass you the salt.

She crossed the stone and stopped a yard in front of him.

"It wasn't meant to be like that," he said, low and fast, as though the two of them were alone and everybody else were furniture he hadn't got round to. "You know it wasn't."

She went on looking at him.

"I can still take you out of here. Tonight. I've got the boat."

And Emmeralda unfastened the coat.

She did not do it quickly and she did not do it for effect. She opened it and held it open and stood there in a shift in a cold room, and the line he had put down her went from her collarbone to her hip, closed now, and pink, and perfectly straight, and there was a very great deal of it.

He turned his head away.

She waited.

He turned his head away again, and she waited again, and the third time he looked, because there was nowhere in that room that was not her.

Then she closed the coat and held it shut at the throat with one hand, the way she had all day, and walked out past Kag and stopped in the doorway with her back to the room.

"He's about five foot nine," she said, and went up the stairs.

---

Crowbill was in the back of an ice-house on the Shoals at two in the morning with a lamp and a blanket over his knees, because that is where Crowbill was at two in the morning, and he heard the whole of it and did not interrupt once.

Then he named his price, and it was not what she had been braced for.

"When Marisette has him in a chair, I want the asking. The Baron on a boat and gone inside the week, and not one word said out loud about anybody's face." A beat. "And you'll be the one who tells the two of them that. Not me."

So Kag went back and told them, whole, including the part that took everything away from them, and Marisette held her glass halfway up for about as long as it takes a cat to cross a room and then drank it.

"Eleven houses are going to ask me why we opened and nothing was said," she said. "And I shall have to let them think what they think."

Emmeralda did not come off the wall through any of it.

---

And later, in the ice-house again, Kag gave him the other half, and this was the part where she stopped being a person who costs things and became something she did not have a word for.

"He's not going to trade for that boy," she said. "He's got a champion and a city to take and one son he has never in nine weeks been seen to like, and if we hand the boy back the only thing we've bought is his attention. He won't pay. He'll remember. And then in about a month he'll come and find every single person who touched it, starting with the ones who can't afford a door."

"Then what?"

"Then Marisette gets her answers out of him. And then he's fish food. And the twelve of us go on knowing that he rode out of this city with a girl — which two bridges of people watched happen on Tuesday night, and every one of them believes it, because Randall wore the coat and Tess wore the grey and nobody had to be told to lie about anything."

The ice ticked and settled somewhere behind him in the dark.

"Not the Lullwater," Crowbill said at last. "The pots are off the Lost Isle and the men who lift them are mine and they don't look in."

He trimmed the lamp without looking at it.

"And you'll do it yourself. Not Deri. Not Fen. Not some fellow I'd have to pay and then worry about for eleven years." He was entirely pleasant about it, the way he had been all week. "Eleven people can carry a story about a boy who ran off with a girl. Twelve people cannot carry a murder. So it's one pair of hands, and they're yours."

Kag said of course, and that she didn't mind that at all, and gave him the best orc smile she had.

She had never killed anybody in her life.
