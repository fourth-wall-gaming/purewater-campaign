# Chapter 2 — Who Mended Her

PELL WAS ELEVEN YEARS OLD AND HE HAD WORKED OUT, at about the age of six, in the way that children of the quays work things out — silently, completely, and without ever telling an adult they have done it — that the only protection available to a person like him was being useful. Not being liked. Not being pitied, which got you a heel of bread on a Tuesday and nothing on a Wednesday. *Useful*. Useful was a thing you could be on purpose, every day, and the terrible beauty of it was that it worked, it had never once failed him, and he had therefore never had to find out what he was without it.

So when Kag sent for him at half seven in the morning he ran the whole way, and arrived with his shirt off one shoulder, and stood in front of her breathing like a dog.

"You want me to find out who cut her," Pell said.

"No."

He waited. He was very good at waiting. It was one of his things.

"Everybody on this water is asking who cut her," Kag said. She was sitting on the ice-house step with a bowl on her knee and she had the look she got when she was doing sums, which Pell privately thought made her look like she was in pain. "Forty people asking the same question, and by dinner they'll all have been told the same three lies. I don't want it."

"What, then?"

"Go to the Pearl. In at the tradesmen's door at Constantine's, and ask the cook, and if the cook won't have you, ask whoever's at the wash. And what you ask them is this." She put the bowl down. "Who *mended* her."

Pell looked at her.

"Say it back."

"Who mended her."

"Not who cut her."

"Who mended her."

"That's the whole of it. Don't ask them anything else. Don't tell them who sent you unless they ask twice — and if they ask twice, tell them, because it's a house, and houses find out."

"Why that one?" He was already moving his weight to the balls of his feet.

"Because a girl opened up like that bleeds out before dawn," Kag said, "and it is eight in the morning and she is alive. Which means somebody in that house did a thing in the dark that a surgeon could not do. And whoever that is, is the only person in Purewater who was in the room."

Pell was off the step.

"Eat something first."

He came back for the bowl and took it with him, which he would remember for a long time, because nobody else in his life had ever made him take the food.

---

The Pearl at eight in the morning smelled of cold lamp oil and last night, and he went in past two of Marisette's men on the step who looked straight through him, because men on a step always did, and that was his entire profession.

The cook was a Lullwater woman called Bett with forearms like a bargee and she was crying into a pan of onions and pretending it was the onions.

"Who mended her," Pell said.

And Bett stopped, and put the knife down, and looked at him properly, which never happened, and said: "That's a better question than the Watch asked."

---

He had it inside the hour, and he had it whole, because once Bett started she didn't stop and because a kitchen is where a house actually keeps its information.

An elf-girl had come in off the Anminster road on Monday with an old woman, the pair of them looking for work for Tourney week, and Constantine had put the old one on the front door and the elf on the water-stair. The old one was called Magda and was about sixty and had a face like a gatepost. The elf was called Gardwen and she had come down out of the deep woods somewhere and she had a *wolf*, an actual wolf, that lay across the second-floor landing and did not let anybody past it, and Constantine had let the wolf stay, which told you everything about how that week was going.

And when the screaming started the elf had gone up.

"She was in there an hour with her hands on her," Bett said. "And the girl stopped dying. That's all I can tell you and it's all I'm going to say about it, and if you want more you can ask the elf, except she hasn't come out of that room in two days, not to eat, not for anything."

"Did the old woman see him?"

"The old woman was on the front door the whole night." Bett went back to the onions. "He went out of a first-floor window rather than come past her, and that is the most sense that little bastard showed all night."

---

Pell ran it back across two ferries and told it to Kag on the ice-house step, and Kag wrote it in the book — *Gardwen — elf — wolf — was in the room* — and then did a thing she had never done in the fourteen months he had been running for her.

She read it back to him and asked if he'd got it right.

"That's what she said."

"Word for word?"

"Word for word."

"Then you've done a thing this morning that four hundred people couldn't," Kag said, still looking at the page. "They all went and asked who did it. You're the only one in this city who went and asked who saved her."

A beat.

"I'd have thought of it eventually," Pell said, which was a lie, and they both knew it, and Kag let him have it.

He did not go home that night and he did not eat again until nine, because he spent the whole of the afternoon on the Pearl quay on his own initiative, sitting on a bollard opposite the green shutters, watching.

And at one o'clock a girl came out with a basket of linen, and went down the water-stair, and came back up without it, and sat down on the step where she could see the front door. She was about thirteen. She was an elf, and thin as a rope, and barefoot on cold stone.

She didn't read. She didn't eat. She didn't speak to the men on the step.

She just watched the door, for twenty minutes, then for an hour, then for as long as Pell stayed, which was until the lamps went on.

He didn't know her name yet. He'd learn it inside two days and it would turn out to be Ila, and long afterwards, when he was a grown man with a boat of his own and a very great deal of money and three children who thought he was boring, he would still not be able to explain why that afternoon was the one he remembered.

She was waiting for somebody to come and ask her something.

Nobody did.
