# Chapter 4 — The Mother of Pearl

Marisette kept eleven houses and did not own one of them and every proprietor on that island answered to her. She saw Kag at dusk in an upstairs room with one window open onto the water, and she stayed sitting while Kag stood, which she did with everybody.

Kag put it on the table. Twenty-five tents. Eleven cold. Forty-one horses and a wagon with the grass grown up round the axle. No quartermaster. Nine weeks and not one bill presented anywhere in the city.

Marisette let all of it come out and did not write any of it down.

"And what do you want," she said.

"Nothing."

"Everybody wants something."

"I've been asked for a number and I got a better one than I was asked for," Kag said. "You'll hear it inside a week off somebody who'll charge you. You can have it now off me for nothing."

"Whose instruction is that."

"Crowbill's."

The lamp was on the low table between them and Marisette moved it about an inch, which put the light on both of their faces instead of one.

"No it isn't," she said.

Kag did not say anything.

"Sit down," Marisette said.

---

She sat, which put her eyes about level with the madame's.

"He didn't tell you to bring me that. He told you to cost the city and he told you not to do anything about it, and you have been doing something about it since Tuesday morning." Marisette poured two glasses and did not push either of them anywhere. "I have run this island for twenty-two years on knowing which of my girls is lying to me and why. You are very bad at it and the reason you are very bad at it is that you don't do it much."

"No."

"No." Marisette drank. "Why bring it to me."

"Because eleven houses shut is the largest thing that's happened in this city this week and it hasn't happened yet," Kag said. "And when it happens I'd like you to already know what he's actually got out there, because everybody else is going to tell you four hundred."

Marisette put the glass down.

"There is a man in a cellar under this building," she said.

---

He was a Sword of the Lake and his name was Sten and he had been in the cellar since Tuesday afternoon, and the reason he was in the cellar was that he had a beard and a coat and had been seen on Pearl Quay on the wrong night.

"He's not your man," Kag said.

"You've not seen him."

"I don't need to. Whoever went out that window was a client, in the house, paid for the evening, in the room. He wasn't on the quay. He was upstairs, and he took a room, and there'll be a name in the book for the room and it won't be his own." Kag turned her hands over. "Your man in the cellar came to the quay after the screaming started. Everybody came to the quay after the screaming started. That's what a quay is for."

Marisette looked at the table.

"Bring him up," she said, to the girl on the stairs.

---

They gave Sten his falchion back at the water door and he stood there a while and then he thanked Kag, which she had not expected, and then he went and sat down on the steps because his legs were not good.

Kag went up to Constantine's afterwards and Constantine let her in because Marisette had sent word.

The room was on the third floor and it was shut up and it had not been cleaned, because nobody cleans a room while a girl is dying in the next one.

She did not touch anything for the first ten minutes. She got down on the boards with the lamp on the floor and looked along them, and then she moved the armoire out from the wall with her shoulder and put it back.

The knife was against the skirting where it had gone.

It was about eleven inches and it was not a weapon. The back edge was barbed the whole length of it, small swept-back hooks like fish-hooks, which are for opening a thing slowly and holding it open while you work. There was a groove down the flat. The grip was wound with something dark that was not leather.

There was a mark set into the pommel. A ring with a line through it and three strokes off the line. It had been cut small and it had been cut beautifully by somebody who had been paid properly for it.

Kag did not pick it up.

She got down with her cheek near the floor and turned it a quarter-turn with the back of her own knife, so the pommel came into the lamplight, and then she took a leaf off the back of Constantine's ledger and a stub of charcoal and took a rubbing.

It took four goes because the boards were not flat. The fourth one came out clean.

"You'll want the grate," Constantine said from the door.

---

There were three pieces in the grate and they came up in three pieces and a great deal of black that went to nothing between the fingers.

What survived was the middle. Two palm-sized pieces of heavy paper scorched brown at the margins and cream in the centre, and a third the size of a playing card. There was writing on them. Not printing. A dense sloping hand in brown ink with the letters run together, and in among the lines were figures drawn small and exact, and one of the figures was a ring with a line through it and three strokes off the line, twice over.

The paper was warm. There was no fire in the grate and had not been for two days.

"Whose hand," Constantine said.

"Not his."

"Not whose."

"Not a nineteen-year-old's," Kag said. "That's a man who's written this sort of thing a lot of times and doesn't have to think about the shape of the letters. That's fifty years of holding a pen."

Constantine went to the washstand and came back with a pen and ink and the house ledger, the real one, the one she did standing up, and opened it on the bed and started writing fast in a clear round hand.

"Half three or thereabouts," she said, out loud, as she went. "Twenty-first. The knife where it lies, behind the armoire, against the skirting, not moved. The paper out of the grate, three pieces, taken by—"

She stopped.

"Kag."

"Kag what."

"Kag of no house," Kag said. "It's the only one I've got."

Constantine wrote it and dated it and blotted it with the heel of her hand.

"Come back in daylight and I'll countersign the paper," she said.

Kag went down the stairs past a landing with a wolf on it. The wolf lifted her head and looked at her and put it down again.

In the boat going south she had a rubbing of a mark, three pieces of a working in a stranger's hand, and one fact that was worth more than either of them.

It came in with them. And somebody in this city had made it to order.

Which meant a workshop. A drawing handed over a counter. A price paid, and a man who took it.
