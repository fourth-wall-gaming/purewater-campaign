# Chapter 4 — The Mother of Pearl

MARISETTE HAD GOVERNED ELEVEN HOUSES FOR TWENTY-TWO YEARS on nothing whatsoever except the settled expectation, held by everybody including her rivals, that she would still be there afterwards — and she knew precisely how thin that was, which was the difference between her and every proprietor who had ever tried to take the island off her. Authority, in her considered professional opinion, was a rumour you maintained. You maintained it by sitting when other people stood, by never once being seen to hurry, and by making sure that on the two or three occasions in a decade when somebody genuinely needed to find out what you were, you were worse than they had imagined.

She had a girl in a room four doors away with a wound running from her collarbone to her hip.

So the rumour was, at present, costing her a great deal to maintain.

The half-orc arrived at dusk and was shown up, and Marisette stayed sitting, as she did with everybody, and watched her fill the doorway and then decline to fill the room, which was interesting.

She talked for four minutes without being interrupted. Twenty-five tents. Eleven of them cold on a freezing morning. Forty-one horses and a wagon with grass grown up round the axle. No quartermaster. Nine weeks in this city and not one bill presented to anybody, anywhere, for anything.

Marisette let all of it come out and wrote none of it down.

"And what do you want?"

"Nothing."

"Everybody wants something."

"I've been asked for a number," the half-orc said, "and I came back with a better one than I was asked for. You'll hear it inside a week off somebody who'll charge you for it. You can have it now, off me, for nothing."

"On whose instruction?"

"Crowbill's."

The lamp was on the low table between them. Marisette moved it perhaps an inch, which put the light on both their faces instead of only one, and she did that out of pure habit and in no expectation of learning anything.

"No it isn't," she said.

And she watched the whole thing happen across that enormous grey face — the beat where a lie is abandoned, which most people cover and this woman simply did not — and Marisette, who had been lied to professionally by experts on four continents, thought: *oh, you poor creature, you are not built for this at all.*

"Sit down," she said.

---

She poured two glasses and pushed neither of them anywhere.

"He did not tell you to bring me that. He told you to cost the city and he told you not to do anything about it, and you have been doing something about it since Tuesday morning." She drank. "I have run this island for twenty-two years on knowing which of my girls is lying to me and exactly why. You are very bad at it, and the reason you are very bad at it is that you almost never do it."

"No."

"No." A beat. "So why bring it to me?"

"Because eleven houses shut is the largest thing that is going to happen in this city this week and it hasn't happened yet."

Marisette put the glass down.

She had told nobody. She had decided it at three that afternoon, alone, in this room, with the ledgers open and a headache coming on, and she had told not one living soul — not Constantine, not her own girls, not the man who did her accounts.

"Go on," she said.

"When it happens, everybody is going to tell you he's got four hundred men," Kag said. "They'll tell you it because they've heard it, and you'll make your arrangements around it, and your arrangements will be wrong. He's got a hundred and forty and he can't get bread out to them. That's the whole of what I came to say and I'd like it to reach you before the rest of it does."

And this, Marisette reflected later, was the moment the evening turned, and it turned on something so unfashionable she could hardly bring herself to name it, which was that the woman had walked across a city in the dark to give her a thing she could have sold.

"There is a man in a cellar under this building," Marisette said.

---

His name was Sten and he was a Sword of the Lake and he had been down there since Tuesday afternoon, and the reason he was down there was that he had a beard and a dark coat and had been standing on Pearl Quay on the wrong night among two hundred other people who also had beards.

Hesper had taken him off the street without a sound and without a mark on him. Hesper did most things that way.

"He's not your man," Kag said.

"You have not seen him."

"I don't need to see him. Whoever went out that window was a *client*." The half-orc counted it off on the table without touching anything, and her hands were the size of dinner plates and they were extremely precise. "In the house. Paid for the evening. Took a room upstairs and there'll be a name in your book for that room and it won't be the one he was born with. He was never on the quay. Your man in the cellar came to the quay after the screaming started, and so did four hundred other people, because that is what a quay is *for*."

Marisette looked at the table for slightly longer than she would have liked.

Twenty-two years, and it had taken a debt collector eleven seconds.

"Bring him up," she said to the girl on the stairs.

---

They gave Sten his falchion back at the water door and he stood there a while because his legs weren't right, and he thanked Kag, which she plainly had not expected and which she handled about as gracefully as a dropped crate.

Marisette watched the pair of them from the window and worked out what she was going to do about it.

She had, at that point, a fairly complete inventory of the woman: employed muscle, Crowbill's, thirties, no house, no family anybody knew of, feared on eight bridges, and — this was the operative fact — invisible. Not physically. Seven feet of half-orc is not invisible. *Socially*. Nobody in this city had ever addressed a question to her that wasn't an instruction, and the woman had gone on being extremely good at something nobody had ever asked her to do, for years, on her own, out of some private and entirely unrewarded sense of professional pride.

Marisette knew exactly what that was like. She had done twelve years of it herself in rooms she did not discuss.

"Send her up to Constantine's," she told the girl. "Tell Constantine she's to have the room, and the lamp, and anything in it she wants."

"The room, madame?"

"The room."

She did not go up herself. She had not been able to go into that room since Tuesday and she was not going to start being honest about why at this hour of the evening.

But she stood at the window a good while after the boat had gone, with the glass in her hand, doing sums of her own — eleven houses, four hundred and eleven people, the Governor, the Baron, a man with a face everybody in the city had now heard about, and a girl upstairs who had cut off all her hair with a kitchen knife on Tuesday and had not spoken since.

And somewhere underneath all of that, filed where she filed everything she intended to use later:

*The big one thinks. Nobody has noticed. Do not be the third person in this city to make that mistake.*
