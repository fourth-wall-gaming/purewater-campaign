# Chapter 10 — An Itemised Bill

They got her warm on Constantine's wash-room floor with every blanket in the house and the firebox roaring, and Constantine did it slowly, the way you do it for men out of the Mouth in February, because she had seen what hot does to them.

Magda sat on the boards with Gardwen's head on her thigh and rubbed her hands between her own and said the whole time in a level voice that she was a bloody fool and would be up in an hour.

It took better than an hour before the shaking started, which Constantine said was the good sign.

Gardwen talked in pieces with her eyes shut.

She had got to the chains at the stern, under the counter, where the hull was warm because the galley flue ran down. The reinforcement went about a quarter of an hour. The Baron talked. Low and steady and kind, she said, and she used that word twice.

The thing that answered him was not a seventeen-year-old. It was a grown man's voice out of a boy's chest and it was asking to be let go. Not once. Over and over, the whole quarter of an hour, underneath everything else, the way a man asks for water.

"He calls him by his name. Cailan. He says it every night."

She had lost the last of it when the tide came up and she could not get her ear back on the planking. That was when she slipped and the chain rang and the lanterns came out, and she went under the counter and stayed there in the cold for two hours with boat-hooks going past her.

"And after he'd gone." Her mouth had trouble with it. "When it was just the water. Somebody in there was crying. A boy. Not the other one. A boy."

Her eyes opened.

"He's in there the whole time. He hears all of it."

---

The wide grey man came ashore at half seven in the morning and went up and down the Fishmarket bridge asking after Kag by name, and he asked politely.

Kag went and met him. He was standing in the middle of the bridge at a fish stall with two Dragon Knights behind him and a purse in his hand, and nobody was serving him.

It was not a scene. The woman behind the trestle weighed whiting for a man on his left and then started on somebody else, and the queue rearranged itself around him the way water goes round a post. He had been there ten minutes. The most dangerous man on the island, and he could not buy a fish.

"I need to hire somebody in Purewater," Blau said. "Nobody in Purewater will take my money."

He turned his hands over, empty.

"You're the first person in two days who's walked toward me. What will it cost?"

---

It came out at six boats, tonight, after the closing, off High Isle's seaward pier on the ebb, out through the Mouth and east along the coast. Twenty-two people and their gear. Cash on the stones before anything loaded.

"And there's one more thing that comes with us," Blau said. "It goes in the bottom of a boat, it doesn't get opened, and nobody puts a hand on it but me."

In eleven minutes of talking he never once said *my lord*, or *the Baron*, or *di Teufel*, or named anybody he worked for at all.

---

Kag found the man who was following her in about a minute.

He was two boats back and he had never done it before in his life. He changed ferries when she changed ferries. He got out at the Fishmarket steps and could not think of anything to do there, so he bought a fish he did not want and stood holding it.

It was Dess. Rope and cordage, third stall along, eleven shillings short on Monday because four of them had walked off with sixty feet of his best line.

She came up behind him on the steps and cleared her throat and he went four inches straight up and the fish went in the water.

"That was my dinner," Dess said, and then shut his eyes.

It took ten seconds after that. A shilling a day and sixpence for anything written down. He was to say where she went and who she talked to and whether she went on the water at night. He gave it up so fast it was clear he had been sick about it since noon.

"There's eight of us," he said. "He hired eight off the bridge out of his own purse and he told every one of us not to tell the others."

Kag took the slate off him and wrote it out for him in a hand better than his and worse than a clerk's.

*Went Pearl about 3. Sylph's Embrace. Big argument with the woman Constantine out in the street, heard by all. Left angry. Fishmarket. Ate. Did not go on the water.*

Nothing on it was a lie. That was the trick of it.

"Six o'clock," Dess said, patting his coat. "Under the third arch of the Gate of Triumph bridge, on the water. He doesn't come himself. There's a woman in a boat."

---

The woman in the boat was fifty-odd and built like the winch she sat next to, with cloth wound round her head and her boots off and a pipe that did not come out of her mouth for conversation.

Her name was Ossa Kyne and she saw Kag coming a long time before Kag got there, because she had spent thirty-five years looking at water for a living.

"No bucket tonight," she said. "So say it out loud or clear off."

Kag laughed at herself and asked if the woman knew her.

"Everybody on this water knows you. You're Crowbill's counting." Ossa thumbed the pipe down. "And you're the one that stood on the Fishmarket bridge on Monday and told four of the Baron's men to their faces they were thieves. My sister's boy was on that bridge. He's told it about nine hundred times and it gets better every time."

She did not do favours. She said so. Then she answered the question anyway, for her sister's boy.

"He's not looking for a man. Not once did he say a man, nor a body, nor a size, nor a night." She handed over a folded square of paper out of an oilcloth. "He's looking for a knife."

It was a drawing. A single blade near enough to life size, done in ink with a fine nib, barbed along the back, and a mark near the ricasso rendered with more care than the rest of it.

Underneath, in a small tidy hand: *any canal, any depth. Ten sovereigns. No questions asked or answered.*

"He gave one of those to every dredger on the water," Ossa said.

Ten sovereigns was more than that woman made in a season.

---

Kag had never lifted the knife. She had taken a rubbing off the floor and left the thing where it lay.

So it was still there. Four nights on, against the skirting on the third floor at the Sylph's, in a house the grey man had walked through that afternoon, with the blood gone from black and tacky to black and dry.

She took it out in a doubled linen off Constantine's press, tied at both ends with kitchen twine, and it went in the bottom of the pack under the bedroll and weighed nothing at all.

And Constantine had written that room up in her own house ledger on the night, standing up, in a clear round hand, and had told Kag to come back in daylight so she could countersign.

---

Temerach Nebulo was at the back corner table of the Moist Oyster with her back to the wall and a mail coat across her knees, going along the rings one at a time, while at the fire end Thorne told forty people about an elf who had put four arrows in the middle at two hundred paces that morning.

She put the tool down flat on the table, which from a woman who had not stopped for the whole of Thorne's story was a considerable amount of attention.

"Say the girl's name or don't, I don't need it yet. The scroll. How much of it is there, is it burnt, and how do you know the hand is his?"

Kag told her.

"Who saw it come out of the grate besides you."

Constantine. And it was in her house ledger, written that night, standing up.

"And the mark on the knife?"

Orsk & Daughter. Hild's book, chained to the bench, four generations. Cut to a stranger's drawing on the fourth of June, four months before the column came down the Anminster road.

Something happened behind the elf's face and was gone before it could be named.

"Where is the knife now?"

"We have it. Happy to hand it all over to you if you ask."

Temerach did not say yes.

"A Purewater thief walks into a tavern and offers a King's officer the one thing she has wanted for four years, for nothing at all." Her eyes came up. "What do you want?"

"I want the fucker gone. He's a scourge on this city and I resent his men strutting round like they own the place."

The elf nodded once, because it matched what three separate people had told her about Kag before supper.

"Good. That I can work with. A man who wants paying I have to think about."

---

In Hild's shed at seven in the morning the two women who kept the books established inside ninety seconds that they liked each other, and then they went at it like a stocktake.

Constantine described a clerk's hand. Hild described a callus. Temerach said almost nothing and wrote almost everything.

Then Hild put both hands flat on the bench and said no.

"I'll not swear a Baron bought it. A Baron didn't buy it. A Purewater man of about fifty-five bought it, with ink on his middle finger and the buttons cut off his coat so there'd be nothing on him to know him by, and he stood where you're stood and he was frightened the whole time."

She looked up.

"My great-grandfather started that book. Nobody has ever put a thing in it that wasn't so."

Temerach, who had not moved from the door, let out a breath that was almost certainly relief.

"Good. That's the only kind of witness worth having." And then: "Would you know him again?"

Hild went to the back of the shed and was gone a while and came back with a smaller book, unchained, the instrument book, and turned the pages with her thumb.

"I've made for him. Twice. A pair of calipers and a brass rule with a Guild stamp on the tang." She turned it round. "I never knew him in the shed in June because he wasn't in his coat. That's why he cut the buttons off. It's not the buttons. It's the indigo."

The page had a name on it and a Guild, and the date was two years old, and the hand was Hild's own.

Ivo Calder. Tide Master, Watercrafters Guild. Assessor by appointment to the city of Purewater.

"That's the man who signed off the Lady's fouling as a false reading in the spring," Constantine said, to nobody.

Kag stood at the back by the quench tub and let the honest people work.

---

The offer came back off the boy in the green cap at ten past twelve and Pell had it word-perfect because he had made him say it twice.

"He says: agreed, the whole of it, six hundred, cash on the stones at dusk." A breath. "And he says one line's wrong. *Twenty-one heads, not twenty-two.* And he says the item goes in the boat first, not last."

Pell looked up.

"He didn't say anything about the money at all."

She had priced it on the floor of the counting room with her back to the wall and the book on her knee, and what came out of it was not a price. It was an invoice.

Six hulls at forty-five the boat, because a boat that goes out past the bar at night with armed strangers in it may not come back, so it is sold and not hired. Twelve polers at eight the man, per man. Passage, twenty-two heads, four the head — itemised, every head the same rate, servant, soldier and his lordship, and the line said so in words. One item carried in the bilge, not opened, at our risk. Short notice, night tide, no return cargo.

Six hundred.

Crowbill had read it standing up all the way to the bottom and then gone back to the third line and read that one again.

"If that man is going in a boat tonight, this comes back with nothing said about that line, because it's four shillings and an insult and he's not got time for either."

He had looked up.

"And if it comes back corrected, we've got him."
