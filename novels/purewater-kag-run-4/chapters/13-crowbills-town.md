# Chapter 13 — Crowbill's Town

KAG STOOD ON HIGH ISLE'S SEAWARD PIER AT DUSK IN THE OPEN with a lamp beside her and six hundred sovereigns about to be counted into her hands, and the single strangest thing about the most dangerous evening of her life was that she was not hiding from anybody, because she was the contractor, and the contractor stands where the contractor stands.

Hild had put a tool in her hand at six o'clock, black, no shine on it anywhere, made that afternoon at speed and tempered properly and fitted to her own palm off a string measurement taken across the bench. A little hooked thing with a hardened wheel and a flat tongue in front of the wheel. Four hundred years old, that idea, and it exists because old women's fingers swell and the ring has to come off and you cannot have it come off with the finger.

*The tongue goes under, between the ring and the flesh, and the wheel rides on the tongue, and it cuts metal and it cannot cut meat, because there's steel in the way.*

And: *Three turns. Not two, and not until it feels right, because it never feels right, and men have stood there sawing at a thing for a minute and a half and been killed doing it.*

Count them out loud if you have to, the dwarf had said.

---

They came off the ship at twenty to seven and they came off *well*, which told Kag a great deal about who had actually been running that vessel for two days. No shouting. Bundles first, then people, in ones and twos, down the plank and along the stones in a line that did not bunch.

Twenty-one of them. She counted, because of course she counted.

Blau came down third and walked straight to her and counted six hundred into her hands in coin, on the stones, in front of his own people, and took his time over it, and did not ask her to count it after.

"Boats look right," he said.

The last thing off that ship came up out of the hold on ropes: a chest, near seven feet, rope beckets at both ends, and it took four of them and they struggled with it and it was very much heavier than a chest that size. It did not shift inside. It made no sound at all. The underside was wet and steaming faintly in the cold, because it had spent its whole life below a waterline.

Blau put both hands on it going down the plank and did not take them off until it was in the bilge of the second boat.

Kag's boat. Kag's man on the rope.

And a skiff went off the end of that pier like a man carrying a message about boats, and four minutes later a girl with a tray crossed a slope in the dark and offered a cup at a woman's elbow, and Mariarta of the Promise of Heaven put a copy of Kag's own handwriting into the hand of a mercenary captain who had been standing at the back of his own men for four days running, waiting to be told what was happening.

---

Seven o'clock came and went, and five boats were loaded, and the sixth took the last four and their gear, and there was nothing on the path up from the terraces. Nothing at all. No lamp, no runner, no sound.

Blau came down the stones to the head of the line and put his hand on the second boat's gunwale, above the chest.

"Right," he said, not loudly, to the whole pier. "Cast off."

And Kag, who could not think of anything clever, put her head up and walked back along the stones with her hand raised, the way a person does when they have heard something over water.

"You've been paid," Blau said. He did not move off the gunwale. "You want me on these stones another ten minutes and I've no idea why, and I'd rather not find out on a falling tide."

He straightened up. "Cast off. All of them."

And then everybody else on that pier heard it too.

Boots on stone, up on the path from the terraces. One man, running, and running the way a man runs when he is not thinking about the ground at all. Then a second set and a third, a long way behind and losing.

Baron Hanzo di Teufel came off the path onto the pier at a dead run in the clothes he had stood in front of a Prince in an hour before. No harness, no sword, no household, one shoe gone somewhere on that hill, and he was fifty-one years old and he had run the better part of a mile.

He stopped dead at the head of the line.

He was not looking at Blau at all.

He was looking at the second boat, and the wet black chest lying in the bottom of it, with two of Crowbill's men either side holding water with their poles.

---

"Take it out of the boat." His voice was wrecked from the running and it was not loud. "Put it back aboard, and I will not have heard any of this."

"No, my lord."

"..."

"You'd never have got him home." Blau had not moved. There was no relish in it and no apology either. "You've been telling him he was going home for ten years and the boy believes it, and you don't."

Twenty-one people sat in five boats and did not move and did not say one word, because they were his household and he was still their Baron, and not one of them had worked out which way this went.

Blau got out of the boat and put himself on the stones between the two of them and put a hand flat on the Baron's chest, and Hanzo walked into it and stopped.

And then he looked past Blau's shoulder at the chest, and said a name.

Not loudly. Low and steady and kind — the exact voice Gardwen had described through two inches of oak, the voice you use on a horse — and it carried the whole length of that pier because every other sound on it had stopped.

"*Thuban.*"

The chest moved.

Once. A single heavy shift, like something settling, and the boat under it dipped and came back and the water slapped along the stones, and both of Crowbill's men in that boat were suddenly holding their poles very badly indeed.

He said it again, and added three words in a language that put the hair up on Kag's arms.

And something inside seven feet of wet oak took hold of the lid from underneath.

---

She went round the outside of the lamps with the tool already in her left hand and the thumb loop on, and the Baron of Teufel saw her coming and dismissed her.

His eyes came off the chest and went over her once — the size, the leather, the club, the lamp behind — and went straight back to the boat.

A dock half-orc, moving on a dock. He did not have room in his head that night for whatever she was, and he never had had, and neither had anybody else in thirty years, and it was the first time in her entire life that it had been worth anything at all.

Three steps and she was on him.

Her left hand went inside the open collar and her fingers got under the ring — and there was the slack, half a finger's worth, where nine weeks of this city had taken the flesh off him — and she hauled it up off the skin and held it there, and he came back against her like a sack, because he was fifty-one and he had just run a mile.

The tongue went under. Steel between iron and throat, exactly as the dwarf had said.

One. Two. Three.

And the ring did not part.

What it did was bite. A bright notch cut in the black iron the width of a fingernail, silver in the lamplight, and the wheel sitting in the bottom of it, and the ring still a ring.

Down the pier Blau turned round. In the boat the lid of the chest lifted three inches and stopped, held from underneath.

And the Baron, hanging off her left hand with his windpipe half shut, got both arms up in front of him and began to say something that was not in any language ever spoken on that water.

She tried to get her head into the back of his skull and could not do it past her own arm.

He finished the words.

---

Everything stopped except her eyes and her ears.

Her left fist was inside his collar with the ring hauled up off his throat and her right thumb was through the loop with the wheel in its notch, and that is where they stayed, and they were not going anywhere, and neither was she. The shout she had been going to make got as far as the back of her teeth and stopped there.

Out on the black water somebody said one quiet word and nothing whatsoever happened to him. He did not even turn his head towards it.

And then four crossbows went off out of the dark at about nine feet.

They fired because Crowbill had given four men one instruction on Thursday afternoon — *not unless a man starts casting, and then until he stops* — and because Crowbill's people do exactly what Crowbill said, which is the entire reason he had lasted thirty years and the entire reason it had been no use to her ten seconds earlier.

Two of them found him. Both of them found the same place.

Hanzo di Teufel stopped being a man in the middle of a word. He went down instantly and completely and straight down, and he did not fall away from her, because her fist was shut in his collar, so he hung there off her frozen arm with his whole weight on her knuckles and his chin on her wrist.

The ring was six inches under her eyes with one bright notch in it.

She could not turn the wheel. She could not let go. She could not say a word.

---

The skiff came in out of the dark on the seaward side without a lamp, close enough that the ship's two lights picked it up, and a figure in a plain coat stood up in the stern in front of twenty-one people, Blau, six boat crews and whatever was standing in the second boat.

Three words, and a hand cut sideways through the air.

Hesper had spent an entire career being nobody, on the settled understanding that a described intelligencer is a dead one, and they broke that in front of twenty-six witnesses for a half-orc they had known for four days, and then sat back down and let the boat back away as though nothing had occurred.

It went out of Kag all at once and it was horrible, and she and the Baron of Teufel went down onto the wet stone together in a heap with her fist still shut in his collar.

She got a knee under her.

Three turns.

On the third the wheel went through and the notch became a gap, and the ring *sprang* — sprang, like a thing that had been under tension for ten years — and the two cut ends jumped apart under her thumb and it came off the dead man's throat into her left hand.

There was a locket on it the size of a thumbnail.

It was warm. It was very much warmer than anything else on that pier.

---

Above her, the thing standing in the wet shift made a sound.

Not a cry and not a word. Something going out of a chest, all of it, the way a man breathes out at the end of a very long day, and it went on a great deal longer than any lungs that size had any business holding.

And then the grown man's voice said one thing, and it was not to the pier and not to the dead man and not to anybody who had ever been afraid of it.

"Hold him. He'll not know how to stand."

Ten years a prisoner in somebody else's bones, begging every night to be let out, and the last thing Thuban Eta did on his way out of the world was worry about the boy.

The boy folded.

Kag got an arm up and took about half of him and he came down across her knees on the stones, seventeen years old and eight stone of nothing, and his eyes were open and moving and he was looking at the lamps and the boats and the sky like somebody who had never seen any of it in his life.

He made a noise. It was a boy's noise.

And behind her on the stones Gardwen came apart entirely, and Magda let her go, and the woman from the woods got there on her hands and knees.

---

Nobody in those boats drew a blade. There was not one sword out on that water.

Twenty-one people sat in five boats with everything they owned under the thwarts on a running ebb, forty feet off a pier, and the poles were in Purewater hands, and not a single one of them could crew anything. Two were standing. One of those was being sick over the side. An older man in the third boat had taken his hat off, and nobody had told him to.

They were waiting to be told what they were now, because the man who told them that was face down under Kag's knee with the back of his head opened up, and the man who was going to tell them next was standing eleven feet away and had not said anything for a minute and a half.

Blau came down the stones. He was not looking at his Baron and he was not looking at the boy.

"That," he said, "is mine."

"Since when, you cold-faced bastard?"

"Since he was forty-four and I was nineteen." He put his hands behind his back, which was somehow worse than reaching for something. "I have watched that man seat a spirit in a living body eleven times, and I have carried that locket in my own hand while he slept. There is not another person on this earth who knows what you are holding."

A beat.

"And you are going to need to know, tonight, because that boy is not finished."

---

He sold it for a boat, a man on the pole, and the sea, and he did not ask for the locket, and he did not haggle, and he put his own hands out to be tied.

What he gave for nothing, because they would have found it out by Thursday and Thursday would have been too late, was this: a seat ten years deep is a hole cut in a person, and it does not close because the tenant has walked out of it, and there is a step that was done at first light every single morning for ten years to keep the edges of it from working loose.

Nobody was going to do that on Wednesday.

Four mornings. That was the clock now.

---

They brought the Baron up wrapped in a boat sail and he weighed almost nothing at all, which was the last thing Kag ever learned about Hanzo di Teufel.

Crowbill came down to the water himself, which he had not done once all week. He looked at the sail, and at the man tied in the bottom of the boat, and at the black ring lying open in her palm with the small warm thing on it, and at a seventeen-year-old asleep against his sister under Magda's coat.

"Right," he said.

The body went into the ice-house on the stone under sacking, because the run goes out over the bar at four every morning of the year, and both di Teufels would leave Purewater the same way inside two days and neither of them would ever be found.

He took the six hundred off her and counted it, and then put two of every five back into her hands without a word, which he had never once done in front of witnesses.

And at the top of the steps he stopped.

"They heard you on that pier. Twenty-six people, and five of them were mine, and mine will tell it."

*This is Crowbill's fucking town.* She had said it out loud, in the dark, standing over a dead nobleman with a cut ring in her fist, to twenty-one people in boats who had nowhere on earth to go.

He almost smiled.

"Go and sleep, Kag. You'll be hearing that one back off strangers by Friday."

She went home over four bridges in the dark and did not meet one person who knew her name, and every single one of them stepped out of her way, the way they always had.

They had been doing it for thirty years because she was seven feet tall.

They would go on doing it now for entirely different reasons, and it was going to take her about a month to notice, and rather longer than that to decide whether she minded.
