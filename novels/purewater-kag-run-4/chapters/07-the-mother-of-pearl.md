# Chapter 7 — The Mother of Pearl

MARISETTE HAD BEEN SITTING IN THE SAME CHAIR SINCE FOUR O'CLOCK THAT MORNING, in good cloth that was not louder than the room, with her hands still — and every other soul in that house had been on their feet for fourteen hours, and that was the entire arrangement, that was what twenty-two years of governing eleven houses on nothing but the settled expectation that she would still be there afterwards actually looked like from the inside. You sit. You do not hurry. And on the two or three occasions in a decade when somebody needs to find out what you are, you turn out to be worse than they imagined.

She had a girl four doors away with a wound running from her collarbone to her hip.

And she had a man in her cellar, taken off the quay before light on the word of three women who had known him since he was a child and who had all, independently, wept while giving it.

So the rumour was, at present, costing her rather more than she had ever paid for it.

---

Sten came up at dusk and said there was a half-orc at the back lane who would not go away, and that the half-orc was Crowbill's, and had brought an eleven-year-old boy with no shoes on.

"She says it's about the fella in the bag."

"Then bring her up."

---

The woman filled the doorway and then, interestingly, declined to fill the room.

She stood where Sten put her — feet together, hands where they could be seen, eyes up. Like a soldier. And waited.

Hesper was against the wall by the shutter in plain dark clothes with empty hands, and had not made a sound since the stair, and the half-orc had not seen them and would not until they chose to move.

Marisette looked at her for a long moment, and what she was doing was not sizing her up. It was something more careful than that.

"Sit down."

She indicated the chair with two fingers.

"You have been standing since you came in and it is making my girls nervous." A pause, and the corner of her mouth moved. "Also you are blocking most of the light and I am too old to squint."

There was a cup on the table by the chair that had been poured before she came up.

"Sten says you know something about the man in my cellar." She folded her hands. "Take your time. Nobody is going anywhere tonight, and I have had a very long day, so you may have as much of it as you like."

She smiled, and it was genuinely warm, and there was not one single thing in that room that was not comfortable.

The half-orc did not sit and did not touch the cup.

Marisette noticed both and mentioned neither.

---

She gave it the way you would read out a tally. In order. No colour anywhere in it, which after fourteen hours of a house full of people with colour was very nearly restful.

A man in a boat at four in the morning. One man, alone, poling it like he had never held a pole in his life, soaked head to foot — coat and all, in October. Past two moored lighters with two Purewater lads asleep aboard.

Not stopping.

*East. Up the far side.*

A witness's name. Where he had been. That an hour later six of the Baron's men had come onto the Guild landing and taken both of them off the water, and that they were at that moment shovelling out a horse-line on the Vantt meadow with a man sitting twenty feet away who was neither helping nor leaving, and that they had not been hit, not been charged, not been paid and not been let go.

And then she stopped.

That was the whole of it, and she did not ask for anything.

---

Marisette did not move at all.

It was Hesper who spoke, from the wall, mild and unhurried and entirely without weight.

"East is the ship."

"Yes," Marisette said.

"A boy off this island does not go east." Hesper had not moved. "He goes to the Shoals, or he goes to the Promise of Heaven where forty women would put him under a floor. There is nothing east for a Pearl boy but open water and a private pier."

---

And then the half-orc apologised for the light, and sat.

It took her a moment to do it and it took eight feet of her out of the middle of a small room, and Marisette watched her do it and felt something in her own shoulders ease very slightly.

"Because we don't want you making a mistake by killing a boy from the Pearl when the cutter's a di Teufel." A beat. "Professional courtesy. Ma'am."

"Professional courtesy," Marisette repeated.

"Ma'am."

"Mm." She turned the phrase over once. "I have been in this trade forty-one years and that is the first time anybody has used those two words to me and meant them."

---

She did not thank her. She did something better and a great deal faster.

"Hesper. Go down and let him out. Now, before we say another word, because I would rather he spent no further minutes in there on my account."

Hesper was already off the wall.

"Take him up to the kitchen, not out the front. Get Ilsa to look at his wrists, and if there is a mark on him anywhere I want to know which of my people put it there before you come back up."

At the door, Hesper paused.

"And tell him?"

"Tell him the truth." Flatly. "That I had him taken on the word of three women who love him. That I was wrong. That I will say so in this house and on that quay and anywhere else he wants me to, and that he may have anything he asks me for by way of it."

A pause.

"And that I am sorry, in those words. And that he does not have to accept it."

The door closed.

---

Then it was the two of them and a lamp and a cup nobody had drunk.

"A di Teufel," Marisette said. "You said the cutter's a di Teufel. Crowbill sent you here to tell me that."

"He did not."

And Marisette, who had been lied to professionally by experts on four continents and had spent twenty-two years knowing which of her girls was lying and exactly why, had it inside half a second — and what surprised her was not the lie.

It was that the woman had gone to the trouble.

She laughed. Once, and not unkindly.

Then she leaned over and turned the lamp *up* — which put the light on both of them instead of only on the half-orc — and sat back.

"Mm."

She let that be all of it, for long enough that the music starting up down the quay became audible through the shutter.

"He's a careful man," she said. "I've always liked him."

She stood, which meant the half-orc stood, and went to the shutter and opened it a crack. All the lamps lit out there now, the whole necklace of them, and somebody tuning something badly.

Behind her a woman of seven feet was putting a chair back exactly where she had found it, which nobody who came into that room ever did.

---

"There was chalk on the floor of that room. Under a rug." She was not looking at her. "Corners on it. Half finished."

A beat.

"And a knife behind the armoire. Constantine's left it where it fell, which is more sense than the council's got between them."

She closed the shutter.

"Somebody in this city made that. Or moved it. Or was offered it and said no."

A beat.

"You strike me as a woman who knows where things come from."

"I am. Very much so." No hesitation anywhere in it. "Would you like us to look into this matter?"

Marisette turned round.

"Yes."

She looked at her for a second.

"Not for me. Don't bring it to me — I've enough that I can't use." A pause. "Bring it to him, and let him decide what it's worth."

---

She opened the door.

Down the passage there was noise coming up from the kitchen. A great many voices at once, and a woman crying, and somebody laughing with it, which is what that sounds like when it goes right.

"Sten will put you across. Take the boy home."

At the head of the stair she stopped.

"Kag."

It was the first time she had used it.

"If anyone on this island is ever unhelpful to you, say my name to them once."

Then she went down toward the kitchen and the noise, and did not look back, and that is a thing she had done perhaps four times in twenty-two years and never once for somebody who had come in through the back lane.
