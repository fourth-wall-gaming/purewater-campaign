# Chapter 2 — What Do You Think We've Been Eating

CROWBILL HAD BEEN USING THE FLAT LEAD ROOF OVER THE COOKSHOP OVENS FOR THIRTY YEARS, which meant that in October it was the warmest place in Purewater and smelled of mutton fat and woodsmoke, and that every single person who worked for him had at some point in their life climbed that stair with their heart going and been told to sit down by a man eating out of a paper who did not look up.

He did not look up.

There were two men at the far end by the stair-head who were exactly what everybody pictures when they picture Crowbill's — thick, quiet, and entirely unnecessary at that height, which was the point of them.

"Deller and Sabbe," he said. "Who's the third?"

And Kag, who had been up the stair for perhaps four seconds and had a whole opening prepared, threw the opening in the canal for the second time that day.

"Tally-clerk on the middle jetty."

"Mm." He ate. "Go on, then."

So she gave him all of it. The pattern. The bridge. The word *asking* rather than *short* — because that was the whole of it and it had taken her the length of a jetty to understand why it mattered. Two men standing on the crown of a bridge doing nothing whatsoever while six carts went single file round them and nobody said a word. No quartermaster. No requisition. Nine weeks and not one bill presented to anybody for anything.

Twenty-five more of them coming over the water before dark. Four hundred east.

And the column in today.

Crowbill ate his dinner out of the paper and listened to the whole thing without one interruption, which she had not expected and which was considerably harder to talk into than an argument would have been.

At the end of it he folded the paper over.

"Cost it," he said. "The whole city, not your bridges. By Friday."

"You've got two men who do that."

"I've got two men who *count*." He wiped his hands. "That is not the same thing and you have known it wasn't since you were about fifteen, and it is why I am asking you and not them."

She went down the stair with her ears hot, and he did not say goodnight, because he never did.

---

Her place was a room over a chandler's on the Lullwater, up an outside stair, one window on the water and a door that opened outward — because a door that opens inward is a door you can be pushed through, and she had known that since she was nineteen and had never once lived behind another kind.

There was a bed she'd had made specially, because nothing in this city is made for a woman of that size and she had got tired of asking. A stove. And one whole wall of shelf with five years of ledgers on it in order.

It took four hours. She did it properly, with the eel-woman's fish eaten cold at the table and the window open to the smell of tar, and it was the happiest she had been all year, which she would not have admitted to a living soul.

---

The costing was the easy half and it was very much worse than she had expected.

Eleven bridges she could name where they had been standing. Per bridge, per day, you could price the traffic that was not crossing, the trade going the long way round, and the cut that therefore did not come. The barrel off Deller's boy was trivially small. The *queue behind it* was not. Six carts turned round on one bridge in one hour, and she had watched three of them do it while a soldier told her about a woman in Anminster.

Then the second-order line, which was the one that mattered and which she wrote on a fresh leaf because it deserved the room.

Three shortfalls this week becomes eleven next week and thirty the week after — not because anybody is dishonest, but because the question Deller asked is a *good question*, and questions travel a great deal faster than fish.

She drew it as a curve. Then she sat and looked at the curve for a while with the window open and the tar coming in off the water, and did not enjoy it.

And the Pearl on top of it, because she had been asked for the whole city and the Pearl is a quarter of everything. If the Pearl ever shut, the eleven bridges would stop mattering by the same afternoon.

She underlined that once, on the left-hand page, and did not think about it again for two days, and when she did think about it again it would be while standing in a room with eleven houses dark outside the window.

---

The second half of the evening she spent on a question nobody had asked her, because she had put it in herself and because it was the sort of thing you put in when a man has called you *sweetheart* on a bridge in front of six carts.

*How dangerous are they, actually?* Not as a column. As individuals, on this water, at night. What happens if one of them goes in the river. What happens if two of them do, quietly, and it is never spoken of.

She took her time over it and she was honest, which is a harder discipline with a page than with a person.

And on the left-hand page, underlined once, in her own hand, she wrote down what a body at the Guild landing costs and who it gets charged to, and the figure had four thousand people in it, and she would be very glad of that page before the week was out.
