# Chapter 9 — The Fourth of June

HILD OF ORSK & DAUGHTER PUT ONE BLUNT FINGER ON THE RUBBING AND SAID *THAT'S MINE*, and she did not sound frightened and she did not sound sorry — she sounded like a craftsman looking at her own work lying somewhere it had no business being, which is a particular kind of anger and takes about forty years to develop.

Pell had got her inside an hour and a half.

He was extremely good at it. He went in with his hands empty and said it the same way every time — *Kag'd like to know who cut this, please* — and showed the slip and touched nothing. Three men sent him off. The fourth came to the door herself with the rubbing held up close to her eyes, looked down the founders' lane, found Kag on the cookshop bench, and jerked her head once.

Inside it was hot and dark with four hundred pounds of copper sheet stacked against one wall.

"Pommel-cut, nine weeks back. Somebody's drawing, not mine."

"Nobody's dead," Kag said. "Close, though. A lordling tried to gut a girl at the Sylph's Embrace last night and got interrupted, and a healer saved her." A beat. "Let me guess. The dragonshits."

Hild listened to the whole of it without moving.

"Girl live?"

"She lived."

"Right." She turned the slip over in her hands. "Then you can have it."

---

She went to the ledger first, because dwarves go to the ledger first. Chained to the bench, four generations in it. She ran a finger down a column, and her face changed.

"I said nine weeks. That's wrong — I was going off the job and not off the book."

She turned it round and tapped the line.

*4 June.*

"Four months back."

They both looked at that for a second.

"Nobody in this city had heard of any baron in June," Hild said.

---

"And no. Not one of theirs, or not that I could tell, and I'd have noticed."

She handed the slip over at last.

"Purewater man. Fifties, grey, dressed like a steward — *good* clothes, not gentleman's clothes, the sort a big house puts its people in. Came in on a Tuesday, put the drawing on that bench, paid the whole lot up front in city coin, and asked me how long."

A shrug.

"Never gave a name. Never had to — he paid up front and it's a pommel-cut. It's an afternoon's work." She nodded at the slip. "He came back nine days later and collected and I've not seen him since. And that's his drawing, not my copy. He left it."

A beat.

"Which I thought was careless at the time."

Kag unfolded it, and there was the mark — and around it, in a dense sloping hand in brown ink, four lines of writing.

The same hand as the three burnt pieces in her shirt.

---

"Anything distinctive about him? Something I could put the word out on."

Hild thought about it properly, which took a while, and she thought about *hands*, because that is what forty years at a bench does to a person.

"Nothing off his face. Grey, clean-shaven, ordinary. You'd walk past him." She held her own hands up. "His hands, though. Big writing callus, here — second finger, right hand, built up hard. That's not a servant's hand. That's a man who writes all day."

She lowered them.

"And this is the bit I've thought about since, because I didn't like it at the time."

She tapped her own chest.

"His coat had been re-buttoned."

She saw the question and answered it before it came.

"Buttons don't sit where the old ones did. Cloth gets a shine and a dent where a button's sat five, six years, and his new ones were a half-inch off the marks all the way down. Somebody had taken a set off that coat and put plain ones on, and not long before, because the old dents hadn't relaxed."

She let it stand.

"Now. A man takes the buttons off a coat for one reason."

Behind her somebody dropped something metal and swore in Dwarvish and nobody reacted.

"Whatever house dresses its people well enough to have its own buttons — that's who he came from. And he knew it, and he did something about it before he walked in here." A beat. "And he still didn't think to take the drawing home."

---

"Thank you. We'll remember this."

Hild waved it off with the back of her hand.

"Don't remember it. That's how it starts." She was already turning back to the bench. "A girl lived. That'll do me."

And at the door, without looking round:

"If you find him — you'll not bring it back through here."

"No."

"Good."

---

Hessa had the tally out before Kag had sat down, because she had been at it since six as well.

"Say it."

The workshop. The date. The buttons. She wrote all of it and reacted to none of it, and at *fourth of June* her pen stopped for about half a second and then carried on.

Then the Pearl, and she put the pen down.

She did not need Kag's book. She had her own and she had been at it two hours.

"Eleven bridges. Forty-one a day, on your own figures, and getting worse on your curve." She turned the page. "Pearl. Eleven houses, four hundred and eleven people, the boats, the drink, the card-rooms, and everything that gets said in a room on that island and comes back to us." She tapped the column. "Four hundred and six a day. On the first day. It goes up, because the boats that served it are idle too."

She sat back.

"Ten times the bridges. In one morning."

A beat.

"And it wasn't the Knights that did it. It was *her*, and she did it on purpose, and she'll hold it as long as she can stand to."

The room was quiet for a while. Somebody was moving barrels in the yard.

"Right," Hessa said. "I'll say it, because you're going to have to write it down and I'd rather you heard it out loud first."

Flatly. No weight on it at all.

"The cheapest thing he can do is settle with the Baron."

---

She had shut eleven houses on one sentence, and the sentence was *until the man who cut Emmeralda is in a cell*.

Four hundred and eleven people. Three days of her own money, and counting.

And on the far side of High Isle, on a ship that steamed where its hull met the water, behind twenty men and a pier no ferryman would row past, there was a nineteen-year-old with rings on three fingers who had stood on Pearl Quay in daylight and told an entire street he would burn that house with the girls inside it.

Thirty witnesses. Some of them had it by nightfall as *the girls inside and the doors nailed*, which is how it goes.

He would come back for her. He had said so in front of a crowd.

---

The presentation was on Temple Isle on the eve of the Tourney, and by long form every entrant in every event is walked out in front of the Crown's man, names read, colours shown, and each of them **bare-headed for the space of a bow**.

Kag had very nearly forgotten it, and should not have.

She went to say goodbye to the elf in Constantine's yard and found her sitting where she had been sitting for two days, looking at a gate, with a wolf beside her that would not drink out of the canal.

"Hey. Girl."

Gardwen did not look up straight away. When she did it was slow, like somebody surfacing.

"Cheer up. Go and see the presentation — they walk the lot of them out on Temple Isle this afternoon, colours and names and bowing. Half the city goes. It's free." Kag shrugged the coat on. "Might do you good. You've been sat in a yard looking at a gate for two days."

"...There's a presentation?"

"Every entrant. Bare-headed for the bow, that's the form of it. My mother's people used to do the same at the beast-fairs, only with fewer bows and a lot more shouting."

And Gardwen looked at the gate, and then at the wolf, and then — for the first time since Kag had met her — at *herself*: the state of her hands, the front of her, two days of it.

"I'd have to wash."

"You would."

Something that was almost a laugh came out of her.

"All right."

From the kitchen door, with a pan in her hand and not the slightest interest in pretending she had not been listening to every word:

"Good. I'll come."

---

Six hundred people on the grass on Temple Isle, and the di Teufel colours came out about two-thirds down the list.

The Baron first, bare-headed on purpose — uncovered and unhurried, letting six hundred people look at him for as long as they liked. And the crowd looked.

Then the champion.

Walked out in full harness with a man at each shoulder. The herald read something. And the helm came off for the space of a bow.

He was too thin for it.

That was what everybody on that grass saw and nobody remarked on, because armour never fits anybody and nobody cares. Seventeen, perhaps. A long neck. And he held the bow about a second longer than the form required, and one of the men at his shoulder put a hand flat on the backplate to bring him up.

Then the helm went back on and it was over, and the herald was already on the next name, and six hundred people clapped the way a crowd claps at the forty-first name on a list.

---

Forty yards up the slope, Gardwen had not moved.

She had both hands on the rope. She was not shaking and she was not making a sound and she had not taken a breath since the helm came off, and Magda had a hand on her upper arm and was saying something into the side of her head.

And that was genuinely all Kag could see.

Nine years of reading people for money — across a bench, across a bridge, across a table with a lamp on it — and she stood forty yards from a woman having the worst four seconds of her life and got absolutely nothing at all.
