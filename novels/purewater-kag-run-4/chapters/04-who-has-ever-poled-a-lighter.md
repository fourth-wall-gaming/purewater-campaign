# Chapter 4 — Who On That Column Has Ever Poled a Lighter

KAG WENT UP THE STAIR TO THE LEAD ROOF A LITTLE BEFORE MIDNIGHT with a proposal about boats, and came down about forty minutes later having been taken apart, reassembled, and handed a city — and the remarkable thing about the whole encounter, which she would turn over for years, was that the man barely said forty words and she did nearly all of the work herself, which was of course the point and had been the point every time he had ever done it to anybody.

There was a lamp and a pot of something. He did not look up when she came off the stair.

She did not sell it. She costed it, the way Hessa had told her to, and she was afterwards quite certain that Hessa had known exactly what would happen if she did.

**One.** Eleven bridges, the curve, the Pearl on top. He had read the four sheets. He did not mention them, then or ever.

**Two.** There was nothing to sell them. No quartermaster, no requisition, nine weeks of nobody presenting a bill. She had tried it that morning on a bridge and been laughed at by a man who then called her *sweetheart*, and she said so out loud, because he would find out anyway and because being wrong in your own words is very much cheaper than being caught in somebody else's.

**Three** — and here she slowed down, because this was the piece she had got to at about four o'clock that afternoon with the window open and the tar coming in.

They could not come into this city. Nobody can. There is no road past Caravan Square and there never has been, which is the only reason Purewater has ever been worth anything to anybody. Four hundred of them were east at the Vantt place with the horses. Twenty were on a ship at the seaward pier. And for nine weeks they had been a column that *moved* — and as of noon that day, they had stopped.

A static four hundred eat. Every loaf, every barrel, every bolt of canvas, every nail. And every message between that estate and that ship has to cross water.

"They don't have to pay us," Kag said. "They have to *come through* us."

A beat.

"Those are different problems and we've been solving the wrong one."

Then the lighters — *not sunk*, and she said that part plainly and first, because a body at the Guild landing gets answered at the Fishmarket and she had put a figure on that at one o'clock in the afternoon and the figure had four thousand people in it.

---

"Are they local or out-of-towners?" she asked. "The lads on the lighters."

"I don't know," Crowbill said.

He said it without a trace of embarrassment, which from a man who knew things for a living was worth noticing and which she did notice.

"Started this morning. Nothing on this water is a day old to me." He turned the cup a half-turn on the lead. "It's the right question and it's the first one. Everything else is downstream of it."

Then he sat back against the chimney-stack and let her get there herself, because that is how he had taught everybody who had ever worked for him anything at all.

"Nine weeks on the road," he said. "Four abreast. Horses, farriers, armourers. They came four hundred miles overland out of the east."

A gull went over. He watched it.

"Ask yourself who on that column has ever poled a lighter."

---

And there it was.

It had been sitting in front of her since Sim said it in the Oyster and she had walked straight past it, because she had been thinking about hulls.

Not one of them can crew a boat. Cavalry out of a country with roads, in a city that has none, and the two things they need moved between a ship at the seaward pier and four hundred men east of the isles are food and messages, and both of those go on water, and there is not a man in that entire company who can do it.

So they hired.

They hired in a city where every boatman on every stair either pays Crowbill or knows somebody who does. They hired in a hurry, that morning. And the two lads sleeping on those lighters because it was warmer than a tent were almost certainly *from here*.

"Yep." Kag heard herself say it before she had decided to. "Boss. You're a fucking genius."

"Mm," said Crowbill, in the tone of a man being told it was raining.

---

"Who's running the whisper network? If I go round asking it won't be subtle."

"There isn't a network." No modesty in it whatsoever. "There's boys and there's ferrymen and there's me. Everything that happens on this water gets seen by somebody who is either eleven years old or holding a pole, and both of them will talk to me for a copper, and neither of them knows they're doing anything."

He nodded down at the stair-head, where a boy was sitting.

"That's Pell. He ran for you this morning. There's about forty of him."

Then he held up a finger, which for Crowbill was a lecture.

"**Hessa**, for the boys. She pays them, she knows which ones can count and which ones make things up, and she'll have four of them on the Guild landing by first light asking nothing and watching everything. Names, faces, whether they go home at the end of a shift and which stair they go home *to*."

Second finger.

"**Kerrin.** Brown coat, dockside, entirely forgettable. He'll sit in the Oyster and the Siren's Call for two days and come back with who those boys' mothers are. He follows and he does not close, ever, and if he's made he buys them a drink and comes home, and I have never had to explain that to him twice."

His hand dropped.

"Use them both. Tell Hessa I said so and she'll believe you, because she'll have already worked out why."

---

"I find it hard not to be loud, boss," Kag said. "I wanted to get a sense of the land before I brought anything to you. But yeah. I'll work on that."

And he looked at her for a moment with what was, on him, something close to fondness — the way a man looks at a heron that has just done something herons do.

"You can't be quiet."

He said it as a simple fact about the world, like the tide.

"You're eight foot of you and everybody on this water knows your face. Trying to be quiet is a waste of a good afternoon."

He drained the cup.

"So be loud where it's *useful*. You standing on a bridge saying the word out loud in front of forty people — that's not a mistake. That's a thing I can't buy. Half the Shoals has told that story today and every one of them stood a bit straighter telling it."

He got up and brushed the lead dust off his coat.

"What I want is you choosing *when*. That's all. Loud on purpose is a tool."

A beat.

"Loud because a man called you sweetheart is a man knowing where to find you."

And he went down the stair without saying goodnight, because he never did.

---

Hessa took about ninety seconds and one sentence of explanation, and by the time Kag had finished she was already working out which four boys and had opinions about two of them.

"Not Pell." She was writing while she said it. "Pell's yours now, and they'll have seen him run to you."

*Pell's yours now*, said while writing something else, in a back room behind a chandlery, at midnight.

That was the whole ceremony. Crowbill had nodded at a stair-head and Hessa had said four words without looking up, and nobody had made anything of it, and Kag went down the fondamenta afterwards in the cold trying to work out why her chest felt strange.

It meant she paid him. It meant she did not lend him out. It meant that if he got picked up it was her problem and nobody else's.

He was eleven, or said he was. He could count to a hundred and could not read. He slept in the gap over the cookshop ovens on the Lullwater along with two other boys and, in winter, sometimes four, and Crowbill had never once mentioned that he allowed it, because everybody understood that mentioning it would end it. He had taken to standing about four feet behind her left shoulder when she was stopped, which he had copied off somebody, and which she had not told him to stop doing.

It was the first thing she had ever been given that breathed.

"I'll put Jeb and the Coyle girl on the landing from first light," Hessa said, still writing, "and they'll be selling cockles. Because they will actually be selling cockles."
