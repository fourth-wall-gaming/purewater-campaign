# Chapter 8 — What Colour Is the Rug

PELL WOKE UP THE WAY BOYS DO, all at once and in the middle of a sentence, in the bottom of Sten's boatman's skiff at the Lullwater stair with a stranger's coat over him.

"—she's not dead because of a woman."

Kag stopped with one foot on the stone.

He rubbed his face and got it into order, because he had been carrying it since noon and nobody had asked him for it.

"I went and I asked like you said. Not who cut her. Who *mended* her." He was extremely pleased with himself and trying not to show it. "Kitchen girl at the Lily Garden said. There's a woman been taken on at the Sylph's this week — foreign, elf, come in off the landward road. And she's got a *wolf*."

He held his hands out to show how big, which was not accurate.

"They put her on the water-stair all night and when the screaming started she was up them stairs before anybody. Kitchen girl says she went in the room and put everybody out and shut the door, and when Sister Ansel come at half four the girl was already stopped bleeding."

He looked up.

"Kitchen girl says it weren't stitching. She says nobody stops that with stitching."

A shrug, because he was eleven and that was the end of what he knew.

"That's all I got. Is it good?"

It was good.

It was the second time that day that the thing she needed had come from somebody nobody was looking at.

---

The hall of the Sylph's Embrace smelled of lye. Somebody had been scrubbing the flags with a will since before dark and had not got all of it up.

Constantine came out of the kitchen with her sleeves past the elbow and a cloth in her hand. She had not slept. She looked at eight feet of half-orc in her doorway with two of Marisette's men standing behind it, and her face did not change by one degree.

"No."

She said it the way you say it to rain.

"I've had the Watch, I've had the Order, I've had four of Marisette's and a man from the council's clerk, and every one of them wanted to see the room and not one of them wanted to see *her*." The cloth went over her shoulder. "So. No."

She did not move out of the doorway.

"Who are you?"

"Kag. Crowbill's."

"Mm." Something shifted, fractionally. Not warmer. "Then you'll want the knife."

She had already worked it out and did not look pleased about being right.

"Go on, then. What's it going to cost me, having you upstairs?"

"Nothing. I'll be quick. I just need to find out where it came from — maybe take a rubbing or two, to check details."

A beat.

"People misjudge me, you know. I hate it when they do that."

---

Constantine looked at her for a second longer than was comfortable.

"Mm," she said.

Then she took the cloth off her shoulder, folded it, and put it down on the hall table, which was a thing she did not need to do and which took four seconds.

"One condition. You don't go in there thinking about a knife."

She picked up a lamp.

"You go in remembering there was a girl on that floor. Everybody's been up those stairs today and not one of them has been able to tell me what colour the rug is."

She was already going up.

"Sister Ansel's asleep in the chair and Emmeralda's under. You wake either of them and we're done."

---

First floor, the end door. A day, and nobody had touched anything, because the woman who owned that house had more sense than the entire city council and had kept it shut.

The lamp went down on the floor where the light was useful, which was somebody's habit from the night before.

The bed shoved back against the wall. A window, closed now, and latched. Two glasses on the sill and one of them still full.

A rug kicked half over, and under the edge of it, chalk — big, and the part you could see had *corners* on it.

Bare boards where the rug was not, scrubbed hard and not scrubbed enough, and a long dark stain in the grain running downhill toward the window with two handprints in it at the top.

A man's coat over the back of the chair by the washstand. Good cloth. Too warm for the season.

And the armoire, standing a foot out from the wall where two people had walked it out the night before.

The rug was green. A dark green, with a border, and worn through in one place by the foot of the bed where somebody had stood to dress for fifteen years.

---

She did not pick the knife up.

She got down on the boards with her cheek nearly on the floor and worked it out from where it lay, and when she moved it she moved it with the back of a knife of her own — a quarter-turn, so the pommel came into the lamplight.

Paper off the back of Constantine's ledger. A stub of charcoal. The rubbing took four goes, because the boards were not flat, and the fourth came out clean.

What anybody could have seen: about eleven inches, barbed down one edge — not serrated, *barbed*, small swept-back hooks like fish-hooks, which are not for killing anything and are for opening something slowly and holding it open while you work. A groove down the flat. The grip wound with something dark that was not leather and that she elected not to think about. Blood gone black and tacky on the hooks.

And set into the pommel, small and beautifully done: a ring with a line through it and three strokes off the line.

What she could *not* do was price it, and that stopped her flat.

She had valued nine years of cargo. Salt fish, hemp, tallow, ironmongery, contraband glass, one shipment of stolen brass fittings that took a week. She could put a figure on almost anything that crossed that water to within about ten per cent and had never once been badly wrong.

She had no comparables for this. None. It sat outside every single thing she knew, which had not happened to her since she was nineteen.

---

Nobody had looked in the fireplace.

Four people up those stairs in a day and not one of them had looked in the grate, and what came out of it came out in three pieces and a great deal of black that went to nothing between her fingers: two palm-sized pieces of heavy paper scorched brown at the margins and still cream in the centre, and a third the size of a playing card.

Written on. Not printed — a dense sloping hand in brown ink with the letters run together, and among the lines, drawn small and exact, figures. One of them was a ring with a line through it and three strokes off the line, twice over.

The same mark as the pommel.

And the hand was a grown man's. Confident, fast, practised. Not the hand of a nineteen-year-old who has never had to do anything twice.

Constantine held them to the hall lamp and turned one over and her face did not change.

"I've had four people up those stairs today and not one of them looked in the fireplace."

She handed them back.

"Keep it. If it's in my house it's evidence against my house, and I've got a ledger for the rest."

Then, flatly, without looking up:

"Write down that you took it and when. I'll countersign. I'm not having a thing turn up in a year that nobody can account for."

And that — a scrubbed hall at nine o'clock at night, a woman with her sleeves past her elbows and a ledger she did standing up, and a line of provenance written in two hands — was the most dangerous object anybody in Purewater produced that week, and neither of them knew it.

---

"I'd like to meet the healer."

That got the first proper pause of the evening out of her.

"Would you."

She pushed off the door and picked the lamp back up and stood there weighing it, and what she was weighing was not Kag.

"She's been awake thirty hours and she's got a wolf in my yard that won't drink out of the canal."

A beat.

"Wrong sort of elf."

And then, after a second, and plainly finding it difficult:

"Gardwen."
