Purewater is built on water and is entirely unembarrassed about it.

There is no ground. There are buildings, and there is water, and between them there are ledges a yard wide with no rail and a drop into a channel that has been carrying the city's traffic, its cargo, its gossip and its dead since before anybody kept a record. Houses come straight up out of it. Doors open onto steps that go down under the surface and keep going. Everything that matters travels by flat-bottomed boat, poled and rowed and shouted at, and every single journey costs a fare.

Anybody raised there can handle a boat. It is like walking, and nobody remarks on it, and no child has ever been praised for it.

The other half of that — the half nobody in Purewater ever bothers to say out loud, because saying it would be like announcing that water is wet — is that nobody *else* can. A stranger in that city cannot move a sack of flour across it, cannot get to the temple, cannot send a message, cannot flee, without hiring somebody who was born there.

There are no horses either. The land stops at Caravan Square and does not resume. A column coming down the Anminster road arrives at the gate and then has to be taken to pieces and put into boats in public, slowly, in front of everybody, one crate at a time, which is the most exposed a great house ever gets and the reason great houses hate the place.

Once there were Dragon Kings. They are gone and every child knows it. What is left of them is old armour on border lords, a name on a barony, and a small number of men who would like the old power back badly enough to pay whatever it costs and to let somebody else pay most of it.

The Lake Lady's Tourney comes round once a year — archery, single combat, and a scramble across the canals — and it is older than the Governor's office, and for the week of it the city fills up and the law goes quiet and everybody makes a year's money in six days.

This year Baron Hanzo di Teufel came down the road for it, with Dragon Knights at his back and a champion nobody had been permitted to see.

None of that is what this book is about.

This book is about a half-orc who collected debts on the Shoals bridges, and who found three accounts short in one week for the same reason, and who — because nobody had ever asked her opinion about anything in her entire life, and she had one — went and had a look.
