Purewater is built on water and admits it.

There is no ground and there are no roads. Houses come straight up out of the
channel with no bank and no shore, and the doors open onto steps that go down
under the surface. What traffic matters goes by boat. The rest goes over a
bridge or along a ledge a yard wide with no rail, and everything costs a fare.

Anybody raised in that city can handle a boat. It is like walking. The other
half of that, which nobody in Purewater ever bothers to say out loud, is that
nobody else can — and so a stranger in this city cannot move a sack of flour
across it without hiring somebody who was born here.

There are no horses either. The land stops at Caravan Square. A column coming
down the Anminster road gets as far as the gate and then has to be put in boats
in public, one piece at a time.

The Lake Lady's Tourney comes once a year — archery, single combat, and a
scramble across the canals — and for the week of it the city fills up and the
law goes quiet.

This year Baron Hanzo di Teufel came for it, with Dragon Knights, and a champion
nobody had been allowed to see.

None of that is what this book is about.

It is about a half-orc who did collections on the Shoals bridges, and who found
three accounts short in one week for one reason, and who went and looked.
