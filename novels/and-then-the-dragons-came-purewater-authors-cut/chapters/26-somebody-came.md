# Chapter 26 — Somebody Came

NERISSA LOOKED AT THE CHANNEL AS THEY CAME DOWN THE TEMPLE STAIR AND SAID
"*it's thin*," in the voice of a woman reporting a fact she wishes she could not
feel.

It took forty minutes to get everything to Lost Isle.

The boy, carried, awake, and very frightened. Gardwen beside him the whole way
with her hand in his. Ruhi in the bottom of the boat. Hesper with a box within a
box. Nerissa, who had left her own Tourney running behind her without a backward
glance. Twelve Swords. And Ravella, with four of hers in the water alongside the
whole way, which no boatman in that city has ever had happen to them.

The drowned street. The swept stair. The bones laid out on the stone with the
wire cut off the wrists and a borrowed axe across them.

Hesper set the locket down at the head of the bones and opened the outer box and
then, with a great deal of care, the inner one.

The thing inside it moved.

---

They put the boy down on the stone at the top of the stair, and Gardwen held
him, and Thuban Eta came up into his face for what everybody there understood
was the last time.

He looked at the bones. Then at the locket. Then at Magda.

"You came back," he said.

"Aye."

"Then I am ready, and I have been ready for a thousand years, and I would like
you to be quick — because he is very tired and I have taken enough of his life."

---

She did it exactly as she had done it before, because it had been right before.

She said the name. **Thuban Eta.** Out loud, first, so that everyone on that
rock had to hear it.

She told the tale — the valley he would not burn, the argument he won in front
of men who killed him for it, the boat in the dark, the wire on the wrists, the
water — in a market-day voice, to a priestess and a mage and a druid and a wolf
and twelve Swords of the Lake and a whole Court listening from underneath.

She named the wrong out loud, so that it went into the air where it could be
heard: *this was murder, and it was cowardice, and no one ever answered for it.*

And then she did the part that costs.

She went down two steps into the black water in a novice's vestment she had no
right to, up to her knees, and stood exactly where nine men had stood over a
bound knight a thousand years ago and done nothing.

And she did not look away.

*"You went in here and nobody came. I came. You're not theirs and you're not his
and you were never Valhalla's, because they cheated you of the death that buys
it.*

*"You're hers. You've always been hers. And she takes the ones nobody sings
about — but only when somebody finally brings them.*

***"Mother of the darkness. This is Thuban Eta and I have brought him. He's
yours. Take him home."***

---

The locket opened on the stone by itself.

Not dramatically. It simply came apart, the way a shell does when whatever was
holding it shut has stopped bothering.

And the water went warm.

It came up past her knees like a draught from an open door in a cold house, and
every lamp on that island bent toward it at once, and under the surface
something enormous and patient turned over and paid attention.

On the stone, the boy arched once against Gardwen's arms and made a sound that
was two voices, and then one voice, and then no voice at all.

And something went down that stair.

She did not see it. She *felt* it go past her in the water — unhurried, the way
a big fish goes past your legs in a river — and there was a moment, exactly one,
where she had the absolutely unmistakable sensation of being **looked at** by
something that was grateful and was not going to say so, because it was a
soldier.

Then it was gone.

The bones on the stone settled very slightly, the way bones do.

And the water was just water.

---

Silence.

Gardwen's brother was limp in her arms with his eyes open. Breathing.

Then he said, in a boy's voice with nobody behind it:

"...it's quiet."

---

Magda came up out of the water and her legs did not work properly and she did
not much care.

She got to them on her knees on the wet stone and put her arms round both of
them at once — the girl who had been carrying ten years of a dead brother, and
the boy who had just had a thousand-year-old man taken out of him — and held on.

And then, after seventy years of not doing this in front of anybody, she came
apart completely.

It was not graceful. It was the ugly kind, from the chest, the kind that hurts
your throat. A great old woman in a stolen vestment howling on a rock in a
drowned street with her face in a druid's hair and one hand knotted in a boy's
shirt.

She had killed eleven people in two days. She has never been able to say whether
that is what it was about. Probably it was not only that.

Gardwen got hold of her arm with her free hand and did not let go of it.

The boy did not understand who she was at all. He put his head against her
anyway, because she was warm and because there was nothing left in him to do
anything else with.

Ruhi shoved her whole head under her elbow and stayed there.

---

Nobody on that island said a word.

Nerissa turned her back. Not away from them — she turned and faced the others
and *made them stop looking*, which is the single kindest thing she did all
week.

Hesper sat down heavily on the step with the empty locket in his hands and
stared at nothing.

Ravella went to the edge of the stair, and crouched, and said something down
into the water that was not in any language anybody there knew — and something
answered, and this time everybody heard it.

And Corvin — twenty-two, who had been sick against a wall for her — cried openly
and did not bother to hide it.

---

It took a while.

When she finally got her breath the light had moved on the water, and the boy
was asleep against Gardwen with his mouth open, and Gardwen had not moved a
muscle in case it woke him.

She looked at Magda over the top of his head. Her face was absolutely wrecked
and about ten years younger.

"Magda," she said. "I don't know his name."

A pause.

"He was seven. We called him Bird. I don't know what his *name* is."

"Call him Bird, then."

Gardwen looked down at him.

"It'd be a lot better than anything those bastards called him," Magda said. "And
he'll pick his own when he's ready. That's how it works — you get given one, and
then one day you take one.

"Nobody's given him anything since he was seven."

She nodded, and did not trust herself to say anything, and put her cheek back
down on the top of his head.
