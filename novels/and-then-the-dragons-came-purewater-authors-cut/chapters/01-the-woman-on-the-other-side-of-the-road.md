# Chapter 1 — The Woman on the Other Side of the Road

THE MEN CAME IN THE NIGHT WITH FIRE AND STEEL AND THEY DID IT WITH THE
particular unhurried savagery that you only ever see from the world of men,
and when they had finished they went away again, and Gardwen was eleven years
old and alive in a wood with nine people to bury and no one to help her do it.

She counted them in the morning. That is the part she has never told anyone
properly, because it does not sound like grief when you say it out loud, it
sounds like bookkeeping. But she counted them, all of them, going along the
line of what the fire had left, and she counted eight and she counted them
again and there were still eight.

Her brother was seven. They had taken him alive and they had taken only him,
out of nine people, and they had burned the rest so that nobody would ever be
able to do what she had just done and arrive at the number.

She spent a decade after that being the person who hid. That is how she thinks
of it. Not the survivor, not the last of them — the one who was small enough to
get into a hollow and stay there while it happened. The old ways came to her in
that wood and blessed her and taught her to close a wound and read a track and
walk through a forest without moving a leaf, and none of it ever once addressed
itself to the question of the boy.

Ten years of that colour of scale. She picked it up in four different
territories and lost it again every time.

And then, three days ago, she found a column of them on the north road going
somewhere at last instead of nowhere, twenty-odd men and a covered litter, and
she had followed it here, eating their dust, sleeping when they slept, and she
had come out of the trees at the last to find that the road went into a ford
and the ford went into a queue and the queue was full of people.

That stopped her.

Ten years in the wilderness will do that. She stood at the edge of the churned
mud and looked at forty strangers standing in it, farmers and carters and a
family with a goat and three nuns and a troupe of tumblers, and understood with
a lurch that she was going to have to stand among them.

She could see how it landed on them. The eyes going and coming back. The
mutters. The way the whole queue had quietly arranged itself into a shape with
a hole in it where she and the wolf were standing, an eight-foot circle of very
polite terror that moved when she moved.

Fine. Let them.

She put her hand into Ruhi's ruff and looked north up the road, where the last
of the dragon-scale was going away at a trot, and she made herself think about
the litter and not about the people.

Which is when she noticed that one pair of eyes had not gone away.

On the other side of the road, an ancient, tiny woman was straightening up out
of the sort of deep crouch that old peasant women rest in. She was cloaked and
mud-hemmed and leaning on a staff, and her face was so deeply wrinkled and
mottled by the passage of time that it was hard to put a number on her, and she
was looking at Gardwen the way you look at a word you know you have read
somewhere before.

Gardwen reached out. It cost nothing and she did it without thinking, a small
casting she had used a thousand times, a flick of attention at the shape of a
stranger's heart —

— and it went into the old woman and hit something like a steel trap and came
straight back at her.

She had never had that happen. Not once, in ten years.

The crone was already walking. She came across the mud on the wolf's side,
which is the wrong side, which is the side nobody uses, and she did not slow
down at twelve feet when Ruhi rose off her haunches and turned to face her
square and every conversation within thirty feet stopped.

Gardwen's hand went inside her cloak. Not fast. The way you put a hand on a
door latch in a house you don't trust.

The old woman stopped at an arm's length outside what the wolf considered hers.
She nodded, once — the nod you'd give a neighbour over a fence — and looked
Gardwen straight in the eye, and asked her question like a woman finally
getting to the end of something that had been bothering her for years.

"Who are you?"

And Gardwen, who had answers ready for *what do you want* and for *get that
animal away from me* and for four or five other things besides, found that she
had nothing prepared for that at all.
