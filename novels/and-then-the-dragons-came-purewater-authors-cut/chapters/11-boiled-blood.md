# Chapter 11 — Boiled Blood

ANN YANNICK HAD WASHED FOR THIS QUARTER FOR TWENTY-TWO YEARS AND IN THAT
time she had done a house with a consumptive in it, a birth-bed that went
wrong, and a man who lost a hand at the rope-walk, and she could tell you the
difference between all three by the shape of the stain.

That is not a boast. It is simply what the work is. You get a shape to it —
where it came from, how it lay, whether he thrashed. Blood is a language and
she has been reading it since she was nineteen.

For four mornings she had been washing something she could not read at all, and
her husband did not want to hear about it, and there was nobody else.

She was up to her forearms when the shadow fell across the door.

"If it's for the rope-walk you're a day early."

Behind her, on the end copper — the one furthest from the door, on its own —
there was a load going that was not like the others, with the lid half over it.
The water in it had been changed recently; there was a grey slick round the rim
where it had been tipped out and the drain channel by her feet ran the wrong
colour and had not been sluiced. And there was a stack of clean folded sheets
by the door with a cloth over them, and the cloth did not quite cover the
corner of the top one, and that corner had a stain in it that had been boiled at
and had not come out. It had gone the brown that blood goes when you cook it
into cloth.

The old woman in the doorway was looking at it.

Then she came in out of the sun, took the bench by the door where the draught
is, and sat down the way a woman of seventy sits down — all at once, with a
noise.

And then she did not say anything.

She looked at her boots. She rubbed her knee. She observed, once, to nobody in
particular, that the steam was pleasant on the joints.

The young one who had come with her stayed outside in the yard with her back to
the wall, which Ann noted and did not comment on.

Copper. Lift. Turn. Copper.

Four days of it, with a husband who says take the coin.

It took a long while. The old woman got most of the way through not asking.

Then Ann lifted a load, dropped it, wiped her forehead with the back of her
wrist, and said it to the copper rather than to her:

"Do you know what boiled blood smells like?"

The old woman said nothing.

"'Course you do. You've a face like you've buried people."

And out it came.

"Four days I've had that. Every morning. One bag, and it's all sheets, and every
one of them's the same.

"It's not a wound. This is the *same shape* every day. Same place on the sheet.
Same size. Like it's been let out of him careful, in the same spot, and mopped,
and put back.

"And there's something else in it that doesn't come out. Not blood. Blood boils
out with lye and time. That doesn't. It goes *grey*, and it smells like the
forge, and my hands were numb to the elbow the first day and I've worn gloves
since."

She put the paddle down.

"They pay double. My man says take it. And I lie there at night thinking —
whoever's in that ship, he's still bleeding tomorrow. Four days. Somebody's
keeping him alive to do it again."

---

And then the old woman made a joke about it.

She meant well. Ann is prepared to say that now, at a distance. She arranged her
face into something sympathetic first, which was worse than not trying — her
face does not know how to be that, and what it produced was closer to
indigestion — and then she said something about honest work, and how most folk
pay double to keep blood *off* their sheets, not on.

It is the sort of thing that is funny in a byre at four in the morning with
people who have been up all night with a bad calving.

It is not funny in a wash-house.

It landed in the steam and died there.

Ann Yannick's face closed like a door. She picked the paddle back up and the
rhythm changed — shorter, harder, the movements of a woman who has remembered
she is working.

"You're not from the yards," she said.

Not hostile. Worse: careful.

"You came in with a young one who's stood outside my door instead of coming in,
and you sat down and let me talk, and you've asked me nothing, which is how the
Watch does it and how Crowbill's do it and how a certain sort of woman from the
Pearl does it. And now you've made a joke about blood.

"So I'll ask, since you won't. Who are you, and why do you care what's in my
coppers?"

---

Here is what saved it, and Ann has thought about this since.

The old woman did not try the joke again, and she did not try charm, which she
has none of. She told the truth, flatly, and let Ann decide about it.

North. Cold winds and sharp ice. *I joke about blood because I've seen and
cleaned up my fair share.* And: *the men on that boat are up to some evil shit
that might need stopping. Sounds like you're thinking the same way.*

Ann looked at her for a long moment through the steam. She has been lied to by
better liars than that, and she did not find what she was checking for.

"North," she said. "Aye. I thought there was something in the vowels."

She worked for a bit.

"Evil shit that might need stopping," she repeated, and something that was not
quite a laugh came out of her. "My man says I'm being a woman about it."

She lifted a sheet out, wrung it, dropped it into the rinse.

"Three things, then. And I'll deny every one of them if you ever say I said it.

"**One.** It's not a sick lad. Sick men sweat, and they piss the bed, and they
stink of the sickness. There's none of that. Whoever's in that bed isn't ill
and isn't dying and isn't getting better. He's being *kept*.

"**Two.** Second morning, the bag came down heavier and there was a shirt in it.
Not linen — a fighting man's under-shirt, quilted, for wearing under scale." She
paused. "The arming-points were cut. Not untied. *Cut*, all of them, front and
back, the way you do when you're getting something off a man who isn't helping
you.

"**Three.**"

She stopped paddling.

"The knight who hands the bag down. Big fellow, white eyebrows, no colour in him
at all — he's the one that walked Renko off the pier. Yesterday he asks me, all
friendly, whether I ever wash for the houses on the Pearl. Whether I know
anyone that does."

She looked straight at her.

"I said no. But he wasn't asking about laundry, was he."

---

The old woman asked her about Marisette then, which Ann thought was a strange
turn until she understood that it was not.

"Everyone knows Marisette. She's not on this side of the water but she doesn't
need to be." She turned a load. "What do I think of her. She's not *kind*. Don't
let anyone tell you she is. She'll take a girl's whole life apart to keep her
house standing and she'll do the sums out loud while she does it.

"But she's straight. That's rarer. She says a price and it's the price. And when
Ganny Corliss's daughter got herself in trouble with a Watercrafter's son two
winters back — a Forge Quarter girl, mind, nothing of hers, no reason at all —
Marisette had the Guild sat down and settled inside a fortnight, and the girl
kept the child and the money, and the boy's father still can't say her name
without going purple."

She wrung out the sheet.

"So. She's a hard woman who looks after women. There's worse to be."

Then her hands went still in the water, and she arrived at it.

"You *are* off the Pearl."

She looked at the door, at the young one standing in the yard.

"And that white-haired bastard was asking me yesterday about women who do
laundry for the Pearl houses. He wasn't hunting a washerwoman. He's hunting
something that went *from* here *to* there. Or from there to here."

She pulled her hands out of the copper and dried them on her apron, and she was
not calm any more.

"What did you people *do?*"

---

The old woman told her that too, more or less. And then she said the thing that
made up Ann Yannick's mind for her, and she said it standing in a doorway with
the steam going past her, in the flat voice of a woman reading a list:

"I got here yesterday — my loyalty's to Hel and that girl standing outside and
no one else.

"These men are stone killers. The albino worse than most… so be careful what you
tell that one.

"At some point, you have to choose sides."

A beat.

"Which part of the boy are they bleeding?"

That is the whole of it. Four sentences, no persuasion in any of them, and the
last one asked as though the answer were already agreed and only the detail
outstanding.

Ann has thought since about why that worked when the joke did not, and she has
concluded that it is because the old woman did not once ask her to be brave. She
told her what the men were, and warned her about the worst of them, and then
named the thing plainly — *at some point, you have to choose sides* — and left it
sitting there, and went straight on to the practical question as if Ann had
already chosen.

Which, of course, she had. Four mornings ago. She simply had not said it to
anybody.

Ann took a clean sheet off the folded stack and shook it out flat across the
bench between them and put her hand down on it.

"Sheet goes on the bed this way — hem to the head, you can tell from the wear
where a man's heels go. So." She laid her palm high on it, a little left of the
middle. "It's *here*. Every morning, four days. About the size of my two hands
together."

She spread her fingers.

"Upper body. Left side. Chest, or just under the arm — high enough that if he
was on his back it ran sideways, because the edge of it's always feathered
toward the outside. Same spot, same size, and it's *sharp*-edged, not spread.
That's not a wound that's healing and breaking open. A healing wound weeps
different every day, wider then narrower, and the shape wanders. This doesn't
wander.

"Somebody opens the same place, the same way, every morning. And then somebody
else changes the bed.

"And the grey. That's not in the blood. It's *round* it — you get the red out
and the grey stays behind in a ring, like a scorch. That's what numbs your
hands."

She folded the sheet and squared it off on the stack and stood there with both
hands on it.

"You said choose sides."

She went to the end copper and lifted the lid off and started fishing the load
out into a basket with the paddle, and slapped it down wet.

"They're due at the sixth bell for that. They'll get it. I'll smile at the
white-haired bastard and take his coin."

She looked up.

"But you tell Marisette that Ann Yannick washes for that ship every morning,
and she'll go on doing it, and she'll go on *looking*. And if that boy's still
bleeding on the day of the Tourney, I want somebody to have done something
about it."

The old woman put a hand on her shoulder on the way out. Not a pat. She took
the weight of it, once, the way you grip a man's arm before he goes over a
wall.

Ann reached up and put her wet hand over it and held it there a moment, and
neither of them said anything, because there was nothing that would have
improved it.
