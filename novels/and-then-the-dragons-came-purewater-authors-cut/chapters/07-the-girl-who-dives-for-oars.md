# Chapter 7 — The Girl Who Dives for Oars

ILA WAS FROM THE SHOALS AND SHE HAD BEEN IN THE WATER BEFORE SHE COULD
walk, which is the ordinary condition of tide-flat children and not, as
Constantine kept insisting to people, some sort of accomplishment.

She dived for lost oars for a copper. She had once gone under the Lullwater
bridge on a bet and come up the other side, and Constantine had taken the skin
off her for it, and she had done it again the following week to establish the
principle.

She was thirteen. She had been in that house for two years. Emmeralda had
taught her to read the price list and then, when that turned out to be dull,
had taught her to read properly, and had been extremely rude about her
handwriting, and had given her a hair ribbon at midwinter that Ila kept in a
tin.

That night two of the women were holding her by the arms in the kitchen so that
she would not go upstairs and sit with Emmeralda, and she was fighting them
silently, which is a great deal worse to watch than shouting.

---

Constantine came down for her herself. She did not explain much on the way. She
said there was a thing that had to be done in the canal and that only Ila could
do it, and that she could say no, and that Constantine would not think less of
her, and that this was not Constantine's asking.

Ila was in the boat before anybody offered her a hand.

"I can go deeper than nine feet," she said immediately. "I can go under the
*Lullwater*. Constantine, tell her—"

"I told her."

There was the wild woman with the wolf, soaked and grey and shivering in the
bilge with a line still round her waist, and Ila felt a small unworthy stab of
pity for a grown adult who evidently could not swim, and was too well brought up
to say so.

And there was the old grandmother from the afternoon, sitting in the stern with
a staff across her knees.

"What am I looking for?"

---

The old woman did not soften it. That is the thing Ila has thought about most
since.

"Look here love. The man who hurt Em is down there, dead and drowned with a
caved in skull. You've got to go down and hook his body so I can drag him out
and put him somewhere so we don't get in trouble."

Ila went completely still.

Not the fear. That came later. First there was just the arithmetic of it. *The
man who hurt Em. Down there. Dead.*

"—caved in," she said.

"Aye."

"Who—" She stopped. She looked at the boathook lying in the bilge. She looked
at the old woman. And she worked out, in about two seconds, that nobody in that
boat was surprised, and that the woman who had come in that morning with a wolf
was the one giving the instructions.

"*You* did it."

"Aye."

And then the old woman said, "I know it's hard but you're all we've got to make
it right," and then, to the shivering wolf-woman, with something close to
desperation in it: "Gardwen — fuck, help me explain. I'm no good at this."

Which is when Gardwen got up out of the bilge water.

She was still shaking and her lips were blue and she did it anyway. She came
forward on her knees so that she was down at Ila's level and not looming over
her, and she took the shawl the girl had already half shed and put it back round
her shoulders, and her voice went quiet and completely steady, which it had not
been all night.

"Ila. Look at me. When I was your age, men came to my house at night and killed
my family. Every one of them. And I hid, because I was small and there was
nothing else I could do, and I have spent ten years being the person who hid."

Ila stared at her.

"Nobody's asking you to hide. There's a job, and you're the only one in this
boat who can do it, and it's a *filthy* job. You'll touch him. It'll be cold
and it'll be dark and he'll be heavier than you think and you will not like any
part of it, and it will be over in a minute, and then Emmeralda's house stays
standing and everyone in it goes on living here."

She put the end of the line into Ila's hands.

"That's the whole of it. You can say no. I'd not think less of you, and neither
would she."

Somewhere off toward the Lullwater a dog was barking. The canal moved under the
boat.

Ila's fingers closed on the line.

"Two tugs if I've got him," she said. Her voice cracked in the middle of it and
she carried on anyway. "Three if I need to come up."

---

She went in without a sound. No splash worth the name — she tipped backwards
off the thwart and the water took her and closed over, and the line paid out
smooth and steady and not in jerks, which is the difference between a swimmer
and everybody else.

It went out a long way. It stopped.

Nothing for a count of ten. Nothing for a count of twenty.

Then two tugs. Firm. Deliberate. Then two more, and the line went heavy —
properly heavy, dead-weight heavy, coming taut across the gunwale — and two
pairs of hands got onto it and hauled.

She came up beside the boat with her arms wrapped round the line, gasping, hair
plastered flat, not looking at anything at all except the sky.

"His face is *up*," she said.

She said the right thing first. That is what the old woman told her afterwards
and it turned out to be the thing she needed said.

---

They took the wet shawl off her and dropped it in the bilge and put the old
woman's own cloak on her instead, which swamped her entirely and smelled of
three weeks of road and pig and was dry. Then the old woman rubbed her arms
hard, the way you bring a lamb round, brisk enough to hurt a bit.

"You went into black water on the word of an old woman you met this morning,
and you found what you were sent for, and you came back up and you said the
right thing first. That's what you gave us. That's the whole job. I've stood
beside grown men in worse than this who did none of it."

"I touched his—"

"Aye. You did. That'll come back on you tonight and probably tomorrow and then
less after that." She pulled the cloak closed under Ila's chin and held it
there a moment. "It doesn't mean anything's wrong with you. It means you did a
hard thing. There's a difference, and nobody tells you, so I'm telling you."

Ila made a sound and folded forward against her, and was allowed to, and cried
properly for about a minute with her fists in the old woman's shirt.

Then she stopped, because she was thirteen and mortified, and pulled back and
scrubbed her face with the heel of her hand.

"Emmeralda's still up there," she said.

"She is."

"Is she going to be all right?"

Behind them, Gardwen did not say anything at all.
