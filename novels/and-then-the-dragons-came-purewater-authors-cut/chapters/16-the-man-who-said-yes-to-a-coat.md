# Chapter 16 — The Man Who Said Yes to a Coat

IT WAS NOT A SLAB. NERISSA GOT THE LAMP FULL OPEN AND HELD IT OUT OVER THE
cleared patch and what they had spent a day digging out of the weed turned out
to be the top of a stair.

Four steps showing, cut into the same worked stone, going down at an angle and
disappearing into black water that did not reflect the lamp the way the rest of
the channel did. Weed hanging off the edges where it had been hacked away.

And the steps were *swept*. There was no silt on them, and there should have
been a foot of it.

Something had been using them.

Nerissa went down on her knees in the water beside it with the lamp held low and
put both palms on the carving and closed her eyes, and she could tell them
exactly what it *did* and not one thing about what it *meant*, and you could
watch that bother her more than the corpse in the water.

"It's a door. Not for people. For the drowned."

She traced the carving with two fingers, following it down, and her hand went
under the surface and kept going.

"There are old stories about the Northern Isles going under. Not one flood — a
slow one, over generations, and everything in them that died went down with the
streets. And whoever cut this cut it so that what stayed in the water had
somewhere to *go*. A way out. You lay them to rest through a thing like this."

She looked up, and there was something urgent in it.

"It isn't a Lady-stone. It's older than her presence in this channel and it
isn't in any doctrine I've read in forty years, and I could not tell you who cut
it or to what."

Behind them, roped and sitting in the shallows, the prisoner said pleasantly:

"That's what he went to fetch the boat for. You'd best hurry — he's been gone
since before dawn, and he'll not be coming back alone."

---

His name was Ellick Sarn, and he had to think about it, which was the
unsettling part.

"Ellick," he said at last. "Ellick Sarn. That's — yes. That's right, I'm fairly
sure. I poled the Forge Quarter run. Timber mostly, sometimes charcoal, and a
woman called Tam who I think was—"

He stopped. Frowned very slightly at the sky.

"There was something about Tam. It's gone."

The boat creaked. Ash rowed. Nobody had told him to start again and nobody told
him to stop.

"They came to me in the winter. Not the young lord — the *quiet* one, him that's
gone for the boat. He asked did I know the Northern Isles and I said better than
any man breathing, which was true, and he paid me eleven marks to take him out
to the drowned streets four times and say nothing. I did say nothing. I want
that noted. I kept my end."

"And then?"

"And then on the fourth time he said, would I like to not be cold any more."

He said it perfectly evenly.

"I've been cold on that water for thirty-one years, missus. Every winter. Hands
gone the colour of the sky. And he said it like he was offering me a coat."

His head tipped a little.

"And I said yes. That's the part everyone wants to skip past. Nobody did
anything to me that I didn't stand still for."

Sana had both hands over her mouth.

"What went in you?"

"Something out of the stairs." A shrug that moved the whole trussed body oddly.
"It's not unkind. It doesn't want anything much. It likes the digging." He
considered it. "It's got a lot more of me than it had at Candlemas. That's the
trade — it warms you and it *wears* you, and you don't notice the wearing
because the part that would notice is the part it's wearing."

Then, conversationally:

"You'll be wanting to know about the one on the ship. That's a different thing
entirely. That one's *furnished*."

---

He told them the rest because he was asked and because he had no particular
reason not to, with the flat helpfulness of a man giving directions.

The quiet one wants the door open. That is the whole of the digging. Four days,
and it wants to be open before the Tourney and not after — he was very hard
about that. There is a tide thing in it. He said something about the water
being *thin* on the day and it having to be while it is thin.

Nerissa's head came round at that. "He said *thin*?"

"His word."

And the door is for taking something *out*.

"That's the bit I did understand, because I asked him — I said is this for
putting things in, like you did with me, and he laughed. Only time I saw him
laugh. He said no, Ellick, that's the easy way and any fool with a knife can do
that. He said the hard thing is getting one *out* whole and putting it somewhere
better.

"Because you can't keep a thing in a boy for ever. He said that too. They wear
through. He said the one they've got has nearly used up what it was put in."

He turned his head and looked at Gardwen for the first time.

"That's why they came here. It's not a rescue they're worried about, love. It's
a *removal*. He's outgrown the boy and they've somewhere better to put him."

And Sana, very quietly, from the far thwart:

"...The girls with the red hair aren't for the demon. They're spares."

---

And then, at last, the name.

"Mine's *content*, see. It got a boatman who was cold and it's warm now and it
likes lifting stone. There's no fight in it. That's why I'm still able to sit
here and talk to you about Tam and nearly remember her."

He nodded north-west, toward where the black hull sat steaming at its pier.

"That one *fights*."

Ash stopped rowing. Nobody told him to start.

"I was aboard twice, carrying. You can hear it through the deck. Not screaming —
worse than that, it's *arguing*. Goes on for hours. Two voices out of one throat
having a row, and one of them's a boy and one of them isn't, and the one that
isn't sounds like a man who's been told to do something he thinks is
dishonourable."

He shook his head slowly.

"That's what the morning cut's for. It's not just the wear. It's to keep it
*tired*. You bleed a man to keep him from arguing.

"They've a name for it. Old-sounding. The quiet one says it like a title, not a
name." He worked his jaw, hunting for it. "Thoo— *Thuban*. Thuban something.
Eta, maybe. He's a knight out of the dragon wars and he's been in one thing or
another for a very long while, and he doesn't want to be, and he never has."

He looked around the boat at all of them.

"You keep saying *rescue the boy*. I'd think about that. There's two in there,
and only one of them's been asking to get out."

---

Nobody rowed. The boat drifted.

Nerissa said it first, slowly, the way you read something off a wall.

"Eleven nights. A knight in dragon-scale who is already dead and does not know
it." She turned to look at Magda. "And *lay the dragon warrior to rest*. Not the
boy. She never once showed you the boy.

"She showed you the **passenger**."

Her hand had gone to her breast.

"Magda — your goddess has been telling you the answer since before you left home
and none of us could hear it because we were all looking at the child."

Gardwen had gone absolutely still in the bow.

"Say the rest of it," she said. "Say it out loud so I've heard it."

Nerissa did.

"If Thuban Eta is laid to rest, he leaves. And whatever is left in that body is
your brother, and only your brother." A pause. "It is not a rescue. It's a
*funeral*. And the boy walks out of it because the funeral wasn't for him."

Ellick, helpfully, into the silence:

"That's what the door's for, mind. Same door. Only the quiet one wants him
lifted *out and moved*, and you want him lifted out and let *go*. You'll be
wanting the same night and the same stair as him, then. And the same thin
water."

And Magda, who had understood perhaps a third of it, said so, and Nerissa
stopped using her temple voice and explained it on three fingers like a woman
laying out a cart's load; and at the end of it Gardwen said, without turning
round:

"And either way my brother comes out of it empty. Alive, and empty, and his
own."

"Told you it was a funeral," said Ellick.

The sky over the Forge Quarter had gone properly grey.

One day. The Tourney was tomorrow.
