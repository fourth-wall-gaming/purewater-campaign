# Chapter 14 — The Drowned Street

GARDWEN HAD BEEN RUN AS A PAIR OF HANDS FOR TWO DAYS AND SHE WAS
starting to notice it. She had closed a wound with her palms and healed a leg
and hauled on a line and held a girl's shoulders in a boat, and at no point had
anybody — including, if she is honest, herself — remembered that she is a
priest with nine spells and a goddess who answers.

It came back to her at Corbie's, standing on a water-stair at one in the
morning looking at four hundred yards of black channel with a dull red light
moving on the far side of it.

The mistress of the school had been crying. She had opened the door and seen
Nerissa and her face had done something terrible.

"We sent to you at *dusk*," she said. "We sent a boat at dusk, Mother — didn't
it come?"

Nerissa went very still on the step. "No. Nothing came."

"Sana's gone. Since yesterday morning. We've had every girl out looking and
we've dragged the channel and I sent Bettan to the temple *myself*—"

And out across the water, on an island where there is nothing at all, a light
that was too low and the wrong colour was moving in a straight line, and
stopping, and after a while moving again.

"Magda," Gardwen said. "I can find out what's on that island from here, if the
Lady's willing to lend me the eyes for it. But it'll cost me, and whatever's
over there might feel it done."

"We need to know where the girl is. Do it."

---

She went down on one knee at the water's edge and put both hands flat in the
channel and said something under her breath that had the shape of a name in it.

And she felt the room open — the way the air goes when a door opens somewhere
else in a house.

Then it closed. Nothing came.

She sat back on her heels, breathing like she had run there.

"Too far. It's not the distance, it's — the water's all *hers* here, and I'm not
hers, and I was asking a favour in someone else's house. I got about halfway
across and it went out like a candle in a draught."

One magic point gone and nothing to show for it.

Nerissa had been watching her the whole time with an expression Gardwen could
not read at all.

"You reached," she said. It was not a question. "From the bank. Across running
water that isn't yours, at a rank you shouldn't manage."

"I didn't reach far enough."

"No." Nerissa was already unpinning her other sleeve. "You reached *at all*.
Girl — you were asking a favour in someone else's house because you were
standing on the doorstep shouting."

She stepped down into the channel until she was shin-deep and held out her hand.

"So come inside and ask properly. Take my hand and do it again, and this time
the Lady will hear where it came from."

Across the water, the dull red light stopped moving.

Then it went out.

---

There was one more thing, and it was the old woman who said it, and Gardwen has
thought about it since.

*Could she use a passion to augment it?*

A woman who has spent ten years looking for a taken child, being asked to find
a taken girl. It is not a trick. It is simply that the thing you love hardest
is a lever and nobody had thought to put it under this.

She took Nerissa's hand and went into the channel up to her knees, and the
second time was not like the first.

Her fingers locked on Nerissa's hard enough to hurt and her breath went out of
her all at once, and the priestess stood in the water and took it without
moving, the way you hold a rope.

Then Gardwen started talking, fast and flat, describing as she went.

"There's — the whole island's *humming*. Underneath. Not evil, just old, there's
a great deal of something old down under the weed and it doesn't care about us
at all. Above that. On the stone. There's a *working*."

"Say what you see," Nerissa said quietly.

"Same flavour as the chalk on her floor. Same hand. It's not finished — it's
*laid out*, like a table before dinner. It's cold. It's been cold a while.
Whoever set it hasn't come back to it in a day or more."

"Living things."

"Two moving." Her head turned, slowly, tracking. "Both of them wrong. Not
demons — *men*, but wrong, the way milk's wrong. They're low down and they're
together and they've stopped, they've gone still since the light went out—"

She stopped. Her whole face changed.

"There's a third. Not moving. Not wrong." Her voice cracked. "She's *small* in
it, Magda, she's gone right down small the way you do when you're hiding — and
she's above the other two, up high, in the stone."

Nerissa's grip tightened. "Is she alive?"

"She's *frightened*," Gardwen said. "You can't be frightened if you're not
alive."

Then she opened her eyes and looked straight across four hundred yards of black
water at an island where nothing showed at all.

"She's been there since yesterday morning and they haven't found her yet."

---

They gave Gardwen the plan and she took it, because it was the one thing that
night she was actually trained for. She crouched and drew it in the mud of the
bank with her finger.

Their boat first. They came out here somehow and it will be on the north side
with them, and if they are spooked they run for it, and then we have nothing and
they have a day's start. Take it or hole it before anything else happens. That
is the whole game — do that and they are on a two-hundred-yard rock with us.

Then Sana. She has been in that stone since yesterday morning with no water and
she is hiding from anything that moves, and if a Sword in armour comes round a
corner at her she will bolt into the weed in the dark and be lost for good. So
it is me and Ruhi who go up, and it is me who calls to her, because I am the
only one here who is not wearing anything.

Then the two men. Not before. Once the boat is gone and the girl is out they
have nowhere to be and nothing to bargain with, and we take them at our leisure,
from three sides, with a priestess and two Swords.

"Alive if they'll have it. Because whoever laid that working knows what the
anchor is."

She wiped the mud off her finger.

"That's my call. You said kill or capture — I'd say capture *hard*. But Magda,
they're wrong. I don't know what that means yet and neither does she. So don't
let anybody close with them alone."

---

Lost Isle up close is a street.

That is the thing that gets you. You can see the line of it — doorways and
window-holes at chest height, a gutter, the stub of a wellhead — and it is all
standing in four feet of black water with weed growing out of the windows. You
walk up somebody's road with the water at your thighs.

Nerissa went past her at the first junction and touched a doorframe as she
passed, briefly, the way you touch a coffin.

The tower-stub came up out of the weed eighty feet ahead, three storeys of it
still standing with one whole corner gone so you could see the floors inside
like shelves.

Gardwen put her hand flat on the stone and called up it, not loud, pitched to
carry that far and no further.

"**Sana.** Your aunt sent me. Nerissa sent me. She's stood in the water at the
corner and she's got her mace and she is *extremely* angry."

Nothing.

Then, from three floors up, very small and very hoarse:

"*Say what she calls my mother.*"

And neither of them knew.

It was the old woman who answered, and what she said was: *I don't know, but I
know you sent her an eleven page letter about a heron.*

A long silence. Long enough that Gardwen looked round.

"I don't— " The voice broke and came back. "I don't remember writing eleven
pages."

"You did. She's told it to me twice tonight and she's not a woman who repeats
herself."

Stone grit came down and landed in the water.

"Is she *really* here?"

"Aye. Stood in the channel at the corner with a mace in her belt and no robe
on."

A sound that might have been a laugh and was mostly not.

---

It took her a long time to come down, because she had been folded into a gap
between two floor-joists for a day and a half and her legs had stopped being
reliable. Twice she stopped and just breathed.

She came out into the weed-light barefoot in a school shift with her hair
chopped ragged on the left side of her head, nineteen years old and looking
about twelve.

Dark red hair. What was left of it.

She got hold of Gardwen's forearm and would not let go and talked in a rush,
too fast, hoarse.

"There were three of them yesterday. Three. There's only been two since this
morning — the other one went off in the boat before dawn and hasn't come back—"

"Slow down, lass."

"You don't understand." She was shaking. "They didn't come for me. They came for
the *place*. I only ran because I *saw* — I came out to look at the ruin like
everyone does and they were *already here*, and they had a girl from the school
with them, and she was walking with them like she knew them.

"Bettan. Her name's Bettan. She's fourteen. She's the one they sent to the
temple last night with the message."

---

The old woman asked the next question, and it was a better one than anybody
else in that drowned street would have thought of, and it cracked the whole
thing open.

*How well do you know this girl? Did she change recently?*

Sana made herself stop shaking to answer it properly, because she is clever.

"About a month ago. She was — Bettan's a *baby*. Fourteen and homesick and
cries at everything, and she'd got herself half-drowned in the misery of it, and
none of us were kind enough about it. Then she went quiet for about a week.
Properly quiet, wouldn't come down to meals, and Mistress Corliss had her in and
we all thought she'd be sent home.

"And then she came back *cheerful*.

"That's the bit. Not better — *cheerful*. And she started being lovely to
everyone. Girls she'd never spoken to. She sat with Ana Vell for a whole week
and Ana Vell is *horrible*. We all said it was nice. Mistress Corliss said it
was nice. Everyone was so pleased."

Then her voice dropped.

"She stopped wearing her shawl. In *February*. She'd sit in the window bay with
the shutter open at night doing her letters and the rest of us are three to a
bed with chilblains, and she'd say she was warm.

"And she started asking about people's families. All the time. Who's your
mother, where's your house, have you got sisters. And we all told her, because
she was being *nice*."

She looked at the ragged side of her own head.

"She did my hair. Three weeks ago. She said she could do the plait my aunt does
and she stood behind me with the scissors and she did it *beautifully*, and I
said thank you."
