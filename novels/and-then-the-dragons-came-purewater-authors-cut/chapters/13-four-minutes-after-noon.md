# Chapter 13 — Four Minutes After Noon

NERISSA HAS SERVED IN THAT TEMPLE SINCE SHE WAS NINETEEN AND IN
forty-two years the channel has never once done what it did at four minutes
after noon on the day Hanzo di Teufel entered the city.

Not fouled. She has had fouled. Fouled is a dead ox in the intake and a week of
prayers and a great deal of tedious correspondence with the Harbor Masters.

This went *thin*.

The Lady's presence in her own water went thin, the way a voice goes thin at a
distance, in the space of about ten heartbeats, and Nerissa was standing in the
tank with her sleeves rolled when it happened and she felt it go out from
underneath her hands.

So she rang the tower.

And by the third peal there was a boy from the Governor's household at her gate
with a written instruction to cease, on the grounds that the city was welcoming
a distinguished guest and the bells were causing alarm during a formal entry.

Written and sealed by Prince Emeric. Who is twenty-four years old, and
well-meaning, and who has been told by three separate men on his council this
fortnight that his uncle would like the Tourney to go smoothly.

And she stopped.

She has been furious about that for a day and a half, and the fury has nowhere
to go, because she was also right to stop. She is the High Priestess of a
temple paid for by that city's Guilds and she had *nothing* — a thin feeling in
the water and no cause she could name in front of a magistrate. If she had rung
on she would have been removed by Thursday and replaced by somebody who would
not ring at all.

She had not slept since.

---

She was sitting on the step of the great tank at midnight with her feet in the
water and her sleeves pushed up, doing nothing at all, when Lilura came down
the colonnade at a pace that was not quite running with a note in her hand and a
wolf at the gate.

Marisette does not send people to her. Ever. That was the first thing.

The second thing was that the wolf sat down in the middle of the gateway and
looked at one of the Swords until he moved his feet, and that half the watch had
made a sign at it, which is the oldest shape their faith has and Marisette knew
exactly what she was doing when she sent it.

They came in through the colonnade past the tank where the channel runs through
the temple itself, and the sound in that room is enormous, water on stone,
everywhere, no other noise at all.

Nerissa did not get up.

"Leave the wolf. It can drink. Nothing in this water will mind."

And then, with no ceremony left in her whatsoever:

"Marisette does not send people to me. Ever. So say the worst of it first and
we'll work backwards."

---

The old woman said the worst of it first.

That she had killed Hanzo's son after he bound a demon into one of the girls at
the Sylph's Embrace, and then fed his body to pigs. That working backwards from
there, they thought he had been planning to do the same thing to Nerissa's
niece. And that they thought the Dragon Knights had taken the young one's
brother ten years ago and were hosting a shade in him so that he could fight in
the Tourney in a few days.

*That's about all of it.*

She said it with blue eyes gleaming and a perfectly sweet smile.

Nerissa has spent thirty years listening to people lie in that room. She read
her all the way through and did not find a single untruth anywhere in her, which
was in some ways more alarming.

She did not move for a while. Her feet stayed in the water.

"You smiled," she said at last, "when you said *pigs*."

"Aye."

"Mm." She took her feet out of the tank and dried them on the hem of her robe
like a farmer's wife, and got up. "Good. I'd not trust a person who could say
that flatly."

Then she told them hers, and they were even.

---

They put it on the low table by the lamp and took it apart for her the way you
show somebody a bad joint of meat. The cloak. The tailor's tower. The ring. The
letter. The eleven coins.

And then the oiled silk. Five of them. Four still full.

Nerissa picked up the first twist. She did not open it. She held it, and her
thumb moved once across the silk.

"So he came into this city carrying five women in his coat," she said. "And he
spent one of them on a girl at Constantine's, and he had four left."

She opened it then, and let the hair out into the lamplight, and looked at it
for a long moment.

Then she asked the question that nobody else in four days had thought to ask,
and she asked it in a voice that had gone completely level.

"Beneath the wire. Is there a knot in it, or is it cut clean at both ends?"

The old woman turned it. The wire was at one end. The other end of the hank was
blunt and square, all the strands the same length.

"Cut clean."

"Then it was taken with shears, off a head, by somebody standing behind her."

She laid it down very carefully.

"It was not gathered off a comb or a pillow. Somebody got close enough to my
sister's daughter to put shears in her hair, and she did not scream, and she has
not told me."

She looked up.

"Her name is Sana. She's nineteen. She has been at the temple school for six
weeks and I have not seen her in three."

---

There is one more thing about that night that Nerissa has not told anybody and
does not intend to.

Her sister has had two letters from Sana in six weeks. The first came at the end
of the fourth week and it was ordinary. The second came eight days ago and it
was three lines, and it was *fine*, and her sister brought it to her and said
"she's settling," and Nerissa said "good."

Three lines.

In a hand she would know anywhere. From a girl who once wrote her eleven pages
about a heron.

She had read it and she had handed it back and she had said *good*, and then
she had gone and done something else, and the day after that the water went
thin and she rang a bell and a boy brought her a letter telling her to stop.

She stood up and started unpinning her sleeves.

"Bring the wolf," she said.

And she went down the colonnade toward the water-stair at a pace that a woman of
sixty ought not to have been able to manage, and nobody who was there said
anything about it, because everybody there had worked out what was driving her
and none of them were fools.
