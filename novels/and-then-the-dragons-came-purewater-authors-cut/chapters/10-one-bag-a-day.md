# Chapter 10 — One Bag a Day

YOU CAN SEE THE SHIP FROM THE SECOND BRIDGE, WHICH IS THE FIRST THING
wrong with it, because a ship you can see from two districts away is not a ship
anybody is hiding.

Above the roofs of the Forge Quarter there was the usual column of dark furnace
smoke, and standing separate from it, lower down at the waterline, a long flat
streak of white.

Steam. A hundred feet of it, coming off the water in a continuous sheet along a
private pier, not stopping, with no fire under it.

The thing at that pier was a deep-water vessel, dragon-prowed, black-hulled,
high out of the water and entirely out of scale with the canal traffic around
it. Moored bow-in with her stern to the open channel and the Mouth beyond,
which means she can leave at any hour without turning.

Where the hull met the water, the water was boiling. Gulls would not go near
it. There was a dead-water slick round the stern with things floating in it
belly-up.

Magda and Gardwen came at it along the working yards, which were working —
coopers, a rope-walk, a chandler, men moving timber — and nobody stopped them,
because two women walking through a working yard in the afternoon are
furniture. Which is a fact about the world that Magda has spent seventy years
being grateful for.

---

She stood in the lee of the last cooperage for the better part of an hour and
watched what a ship actually needs.

Not much went *on*. That was the first thing. No provisioning carts, no
chandler's boy, no water butts — which is strange for a vessel that size at a
pier, until you notice the covered gangway aft with the canvas laced down both
sides, and understand that they are bringing everything aboard where nobody can
see it, in their own hands, from their own boat.

Things came off, though.

Slops. Twice, a knight carried a covered pail down the ramp and emptied it into
the dead water at the stern. Nobody collecting it. Nobody being paid to.

Laundry. A woman came at the second bell with a barrow and took a bagged load
from the *pier head* — not from the deck; handed down to her by a knight who
did not let her past the bollards — and went away again toward the rope-walk.

Ash and clinker, in barrows, from somewhere below decks, tipped on a spoil heap
at the end of the yard. Far more of it than a galley fire makes.

And a cooper's boy delivered four new barrels to the pier head, left them, and
was waved off. The knights carried them aboard themselves.

Nobody who was not a Dragon Knight had been past those bollards all afternoon.

---

There was also, at the far end of the yard, a hog-man's cart with an old nag
and two barrels and a lad who was not paying attention to anything, and who was
making an enormous mess of backing the horse round.

Magda went over to help, because she knows all about hogs.

The horse was not impressed by her either. It laid its ears flat and swung its
quarters and the offside wheel went into the spoil heap and the cart tipped far
enough to put one of the barrels over on its side. The lid stayed on. Mostly.

The lad said a word she would have clipped him for.

Then they spent a couple of minutes getting the wheel out, and she got her
shoulder under the barrel while he righted it, and by the end of it they were
both filthy and he had stopped being embarrassed.

"She does that," he said, glaring at the horse. "Every time. Every single time,
right at the end."

"She's testing whether you've noticed her."

"I've *noticed* her—"

"No, lad. She wants you to go to her head *before* she starts, not after."

He considered this with the deep suspicion of a fifteen-year-old being told
something obviously true.

His name was Pell. He did the Forge Quarter yards on alternate days for Renko
out on the Shoals, which Magda kept her face entirely still about.

She had failed at the only thing she had set out to do, which was impress a boy
with a horse, and had acquired a friend instead. This happens more often than
anybody admits.

---

He complained about the rope-walk, who had put nails in their waste last month.
Then he jerked his chin down the pier at the steaming black hull.

"Not that lot, though. *They* don't give us nothing."

"Tight bunch, are they?"

"Tight? They're *unnatural*, is what they are."

And he counted it off, aggrieved, the way you complain about a bad customer,
and handed her the entire shape of the thing without the least idea he was
doing it.

Every yard on that side gave their waste to Renko. Cooperage, rope-walk,
chandler; even the furnace lads gave up their bones. That is how it works. That
lot tipped it in the water. In the *water*, right off the stern, good scraps,
meat and all. Renko went down there himself on the first day and offered to take
it for nothing — for *nothing* — and they walked him back up the pier by his
elbows and told him not to come again.

And the ash. Barrows of it, off that ship, since the day she tied up. Show me
the galley fire that makes that much clinker in a week.

"Only one that gets anything off them is the laundry woman. Yannick's wife. Bag
down at the bollards, coin in her hand, off you go. She's not allowed past
neither. She won't talk about it, though. Went right off, she has. Which is a
shame, 'cause she'll talk about anything."

Magda thought about that for a moment.

"How big is the bag? Is there a lot of laundry?"

Pell stopped, and actually thought about it, which he clearly never had.

"Not big. One bag. She carries it on the barrow but she could carry it on her
hip if she wanted — I've seen her lift it one-handed off the bollard."

He frowned.

"Every day, though. That's the thing, now you say it. Rope-walk's forty men and
they send out twice a week. That ship sends out *every day* and it's one bag."

He clicked at the horse.

"And it's all the same, she said once before she went quiet on it. Not shirts
and hose and what-have-you like a normal house. All the one thing. Sheets, I
suppose. Bedding."

He got the nag moving, and said the rest over his shoulder, cheerfully, with no
idea at all what he was handing her:

"She had a right go at Renko about it in the tavern, first week. Said she'd take
their coin but she'd not take it long, and if they wanted that much *boiling*
every day they could get a woman with a stronger stomach."

He raised a hand. "Alternate days, missus! Tell Renko I said you know your
horses!"

And the cart lurched away up the yard, and the nag did the thing with her
quarters again exactly as predicted.

---

Magda went back to counting.

Eight. That was all of them. Two at the pier head, two at the top of the ramp,
three working on deck — one of whom did nothing at all but walk the same twenty
feet over and over and look at the shore — and one who came up out of the aft
hatch with a barrow of clinker, went down the ramp, tipped it, and went back.

Eight men. In scale, all of them, on a hot afternoon, aboard a moored ship.
Nobody in shirtsleeves. Nobody idle. Nobody talking.

No banner at the masthead. For a nobleman in residence there would be colours
flying; every other hull in that harbour with anybody aboard had something up.
That mast was bare.

No Baron. Nothing in anything but scale and livery. No servants, no clerk, no
valet, nothing that looked like a household. The good clothes and the gold were
somewhere else — north-west round the point, where the boat went.

And the shutters amidships had not opened once all afternoon. It was a warm
bright day and every port on every hull on that water was open to air the ship
out, and those were closed, and — now that she looked at them properly — they
were closed from the *outside*. There was a bar across.

Gardwen had gone very quiet beside her.

"Magda," she said. "Sheets. Every day. Boiled."
