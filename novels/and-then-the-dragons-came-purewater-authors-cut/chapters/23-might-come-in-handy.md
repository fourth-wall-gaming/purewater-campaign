# Chapter 23 — Might Come In Handy

HE CAME ROUND THE EASTERN POINT WITH ONE SET OF OARS, UNHURRIED, NO
lantern, and along the drowned street with the confidence of a man who has done
it four times before, and he landed exactly where Ellick had landed his, and
shipped the oars, and stepped out into the water without a sound.

He was a long time getting the thing out of the boat. It took him three tries
and a great deal of care.

Oiled cloth, roped, about five feet long. He carried it up the drowned street on
his shoulder with his back bent under it and set it down on the stone at the
foot of the stair — *gently*, the way you set down somebody sleeping — and stood
there breathing.

Then he said, to nobody, in a voice that carried beautifully across still water:

"**Ellick. Get up here and help me with him.**"

Nerissa did not even signal. She said, quietly, "*Take him*," and the wall came
alive, and four Swords came over it at once and two more out of the water behind
him, and he was face-down on the stone with a knee in his back before the echo
of *Ellick* had finished coming off the far wall.

He did not struggle. He did not shout.

He turned his head sideways against the stone, and what he did was *look*. At
the shuttered lamps coming open one by one. At twelve Swords of the Lake. At
Nerissa. At Hesper.

And then at the oiled bundle lying eighteen inches from his face, which he had
set down so carefully, and which he was now not going to be allowed to touch
again.

He was younger than anyone expected. Late thirties. Unremarkable in the way
Crowbill is unremarkable, except that Crowbill chose it and this man was simply
born with it. Ink on two fingers.

Hesper crouched over the bundle and did not touch it either. He looked at the
ropework for a long moment — the way it was tied, what it was tied with — and
his mouth thinned.

"That's a *reliquary* knot," he said. "That's not how you tie cargo. That's how
you tie something you're afraid of."

---

They asked him.

He answered exactly three questions and they were all the wrong ones.

*Is that Thuban Eta in the cloth?* — "Yes." *Will the removal work at this
stair?* — "Yes." *Will you tell us how?* — "No."

And then he settled his shoulders against the wall and stopped.

Hesper tried for a long while, and he is good at it, and he went at it from
underneath, professional to professional — *you've done this and I haven't and
I'd very much like to know how the seat is kept clean* — and you could see the
man enjoying it, and enjoying not answering more.

At the end he said one thing unprompted, almost kindly:

"You'll open him at the wrong point and put a thousand-year-old knight into a
priestess. I'd rather like to see that."

Nerissa stepped back from him and looked like she wanted to wash.

And then Hesper stopped mid-word with his hands coming away from his face and
looked over Magda's shoulder at a tower-stub where a druid was sitting with her
arm round a seventeen-year-old boy.

"Magda. We have been extraordinarily stupid.

"There is a man not thirty feet from us who has been *inside* one of these for a
thousand years. Who was put in by experts and has had every day since to feel
exactly how the seat is fastened.

"Why are we asking the gaoler? **Ask the prisoner.**"

---

Magda's answer to that was to start undoing buckles.

Nobody said anything for the first few, because everyone assumed it was the leg,
or the fatigue, or the fact that she had been in mail since dawn.

The kettle helm went on the stone. Then the coif, and her hair came out flat and
grey and wet. Then she went at the hauberk, which is a two-person job at the
best of times, and Nerissa — who had been watching with a deepening frown — came
and took the weight of it without being asked, and the two of them got it off
over her head, and it went down on the stone with the noise a hauberk makes,
which is like a great deal of money being dropped.

She was in a soaked wool shirt, in the cold, seventy years old, with a
spear-hole in her thigh, standing on a drowned island in the dark.

Hesper had gone very quiet. Nerissa said, "Magda." And then, when she did not
answer, she looked at the black water at the foot of the swept stair — water
they had put a man into, in his mail, with his hands tied — and you could watch
her arrive somewhere she did not like.

"*Magda.*"

She was going to beat him out of the boy. Unarmed and unarmoured and in the
rage, the only way she knows how to do anything, and she went to the tower-stub
and said so to his face.

---

And Thuban Eta said no.

The boy's face was wet and frightened and seventeen. Then it changed — you could
watch it happen by then, the way you watch someone step forward in a doorway —
and the other one was looking out. He took his time. He looked at her bare arms,
and the spear-hole, and the axe she had left thirty feet away on the stone.

"**No.**"

A pause.

"But I understand why you thought of it, and I want you to know I would have
taken that offer from you gladly, if it worked.

"Understand what you are proposing. You would beat me out of a child. **I do not
feel your hands, woman. *He* does.** You would break his ribs and I would watch,
and at the end of it I would still be here, because I am not held in his body by
his body.

"They have been cutting him open every morning for ten years to do exactly what
you just described. If pain could shift me I would have been shifted four
hundred times."

He let that sit.

"Now hear the rest, because you were closer than your priest is.

"There *is* a fight. You were right about that. But it is not yours, and it is
not with the boy.

"They did not fasten me in with iron. Your storyteller told you and he was
correct — **they fastened me in with the night I died.** Every hour I have spent
in here I have spent back on that boat with my hands tied, going over the side,
looking up at Corran and Vey and the rest of them not doing anything.

"That is the room I have been kept in. Not this child. *That.*"

The old voice went thin.

"And I cannot leave it, because to leave it I have to accept that it happened
and that nobody came, and I have not been *able* to accept that, because it is
unbearable, and I have had a thousand years of finding it unbearable.

"So. You do not need to fight me out. **You need to come and get me out of that
water. Say it was wrong. Say whose I am. Say somebody came.**

"And you must mean it, and I will know if you don't, because I have had a very
long time to learn what people mean.

"Open my bones and open your door. And then stand where they stood, and do not
look away when I go under, because they did.

"That is all it ever needed. It has only ever needed one person to turn up."

---

So she went and got her grandmother's country back out of wherever it had been
sitting for fifty years, and it did not come as instructions. It came as smells.
Woodsmoke and wet wool and somebody's aunt weeping professionally.

You put his weapons back in his hands. A man goes out armed, and whatever was
taken off him when he died is given back, and if you have not got it you give
him yours, and that is better anyway.

You say the name. Out loud. First. Before anything, so that everyone standing
there has to hear it.

Then you tell the tale. Not a prayer — a *telling*. Who he was and what he did
and how he died, said in front of witnesses, in the open, at full volume.

You settle the debt. If a man was murdered somebody stands up and says so, and
names it, and the wrong goes into the air where it can be heard. Nobody goes
into the ground with an unpaid killing on him.

Then fire. Or water, for a drowned man. You give him back to whatever took him,
on purpose this time, with people watching.

And then you feast, and you brawl, and you get disgracefully drunk, because a
death that nobody celebrates is a death that did not count.

And then she hit the thing at the bottom of it, and it stopped her where she
stood.

**Valhalla is not his.**

That is for the battle-dead. For the ones who fell with a sword in the hand and
the enemy in front. Thuban Eta went over a gunwale in the dark with his hands
tied by his own people. He was *cheated* of the death that buys that hall, and
he has been paying for their crime with his afterlife for a thousand years.

Which is exactly, precisely, why he is Hel's.

And the wound is not the water and it is not the rope.

It is that in a thousand years, nobody has ever told his tale.

---

Hesper cut the reliquary knots, naming each one as it came off.

The oiled cloth came back.

Bones. Long ones, brown with age, laid the way you lay a man. And around the
wrists, corroded almost to nothing but still there, still *on him* after a
thousand years, two turns of iron wire.

They had put him in the water tied and nobody had ever cut it off.

Gardwen made a sound.

Nerissa said, "*Give me the shears*," and cut them, and dropped the wire in the
water, and that alone changed the temperature of the air on that stair.

---

And then Magda did everything right and it was not enough.

She said the name. She put Vell's axe in the bones' hands. She stood at the head
of the stair where his company had stood over him and said the wrong out loud —
*these men murdered you, and it was cowardice, and it was never answered for* —
and she told his tale in a market-day voice to twelve Swords of the Lake and a
priestess and a mage and a druid and a wolf and a whole Court listening from
underneath.

It was the first telling he had ever had.

And she felt it not land. It is like putting your shoulder to a door that turns
out to be a wall. Everything she had went into it and the world did not move an
inch.

The water at the foot of the stair stayed cold. The lamps did not lean.

The boy on the stone started to shake, and the old voice came out of him ragged
for the first time all night:

"— *I can't* — woman, I can't *reach* it, there's something across—"

Hesper was already up and moving. "The seat's still fastened. Something's still
holding him and it is not the bones and it is not this stair—"

And the roped man against the wall, who had said nothing for twenty minutes,
started to laugh.

It was a quiet, delighted, entirely genuine laugh.

"Oh, that's *good*. That's very good. You've done all of it properly and you've
done it beautifully and you have forgotten one thing.

"He isn't only held by the bones."

He tipped his head back against the stone, thoroughly enjoying himself.

"**The Baron wears the rest of him around his neck.** And the Baron is at the
Governor's table three miles from here, being toasted."

---

She picked the axe up off the stone on the way past without breaking stride.

*Say that again. Damn you. Explain. It. To. Me. Slowly. And fucking clearly.*

He watched her come and he was *still* smiling, and then at about six feet he
stopped smiling.

And then he did not flinch and he did not talk. What he did was look up at her
with an axe six feet from his face and say, quite gently, like a man explaining
something to a child at a funeral:

"You're not going to. If you were, you'd have done it before you asked."

And he closed his eyes and leaned his head back against the stone and was simply
*done* with the conversation, and there was nothing behind that face she could
reach with a weapon.

Hesper gave her the explanation anyway, because he did not need the prisoner for
that part. The bones are the primary anchor. The locket is the working one, the
piece they carry, the one the daily ritual runs through. It is not a spare. It
is the *latch*. They cut a piece off him a century ago and every morning they use
it to hold the seat shut.

"So the door is open. The bones are open. He is *willing* — you did that, and it
worked, and I want you to hear that it worked.

"But there is a lock on this and it is around Hanzo di Teufel's neck."

Nerissa, into the silence: "The Tourney is at noon."

And Gardwen, with her brother shaking against her: "Then that's where we go.
Because that's where *he'll* be. In the Governor's box, in front of the whole
city, wearing my brother round his neck."

---

Magda did not join the strategising.

She stood at the edge of it with the axe hanging from one hand and watched the
roped man, and he knew she was watching, and after a while he opened his eyes
and looked back, and the two of them held that for a long time while everybody
else worked out how to get into a Governor's box at noon.

Nerissa was saying "—then Emeric has to hear it from Sana herself, and not in an
anteroom—"

And she walked over and did it.

It took one.

The sound afterwards was the sound of everyone on that island stopping at once.

She found a grain sack by the stair, tipped out what was in it, put the head in,
knotted the top, and said it to nobody in particular:

"Might come in handy."

---

Corvin — twenty-two, who had taken a rope out to that island for her the night
before — turned round and walked twenty feet away and was sick against a wall.

Hesper closed his eyes. Just for a moment. Then he opened them and went back to
the bones, because he is a practical man and there was work, and he did not look
at her again for some time.

Ravella did not react at all. She looked at the body, and then at the water, and
said quietly to one of hers: "Not that one. He doesn't go down there."

Gardwen looked at her for a long moment. She did not say *why did you do that.*

She said: "Was he still worth anything?"

And Nerissa came and stood in front of her. Her face was perfectly still and her
voice did not rise, and every Sword on that island could hear her.

"He was bound. On my island, under my cordon, taken by my people who had orders
to hold him."

She let that sit.

"And he was the only living soul who knew where **Bettan** is. Fourteen years
old. Cheerful. Cutting girls' hair at a school on the Northern Isles for people
who fill them up."

Her hands were shaking slightly and she was doing nothing to hide it.

"I gave you a reason to stop, once, and you took it. So I will not pretend I did
not know what you were.

"But you have just taken that child away from me and I do not know how to get
her back."

She turned and walked away toward the boats.

Twelve Swords of the Lake watched her go, and then looked at Magda, and none of
them said anything at all.

---

It was Gardwen who found the way round it, and she did it by putting the sack
out of her mind, because there was a job.

She could not track a girl through a city with no ground. But she had found two
men on Lost Isle from four hundred yards across open water without ever seeing
them, because she had not been looking for men. She had been feeling for what
was *in* them.

"Bettan's been filled. If she's carrying one of those things then she doesn't
read like a girl any more, and I don't have to find *her*. I have to stand
somewhere near that school and ask what's *wrong* in it."

She met Magda's eye and did not soften it.

"I'd need Nerissa. Who has just walked off to the boats and will not want to
look at you.

"Go and tell her anyway. Not sorry — she'd smell that. Tell her you've got a way
to find the girl, and let her decide what she does with you afterwards."

---

So Magda shouted "Oi. Priestess!" across a drowned street at the High Priestess
of the Lake Lady, and walked over, and said it, and did not apologise, because
she was not.

*Don't look for the girl. Look for the demon. Gardwen can do it for you if you
put her in the right place. Spare me your delicacies and so-called purities. The
rot's in your city and it's not going to leave because you ask it nicely.*

Nerissa listened to the whole thing without interrupting.

Then:

"You've mistaken me."

Very level.

"I did not object because it was *unclean*. I have buried men off the tide-flats
with their throats opened by people I then had to bless at Solstice. I'm
sixty-one and I run a temple in a port."

She took a step closer.

"I objected because you cut up the only map to a fourteen-year-old girl and then
said *might come in handy*.

"That isn't purity, Magda. **That's arithmetic.**"

She held her eye until she was sure it had landed, and only then did the rest of
her arrive, because she had already been thinking about the thing Magda had
actually come to say.

"—and yes. Yes, that would work. I can put my hands in the water at Corbie's and
hold the channel for her the way I did last night, and if that child has
something in her then it will show like a lamp through a shutter." Her jaw
tightened. "It's better than anything I had, which was *ask the school again and
hope*.

"We go at first light. Not before — the girls will be at breakfast and I want
them in one room and counted while it's done."

Then, as Magda turned to walk away:

"Magda." Not raised.

"I will work with you until this is finished, and I will do it wholeheartedly,
and I will not pretend at you afterwards. When the boy is out and the Baron is
down, you and I are done, and you will not be welcome on Temple Isle."

A beat.

"Sword-Captain Vell's axe stays with you. She'd have wanted that and I'm not
petty."

She got in the boat.

Magda shrugged at her back.

"No. I killed him because he deserved it. Fucker wasn't going to tell you
anything anyway. I don't even know what arithmetic is."

"No," Nerissa agreed. "You don't."

---

At first light, at Corbie's, with forty girls in a refectory being counted and
treating it as an outrage, Nerissa went into the channel to her knees and
Gardwen took her hand and put the other flat on the wall of the school.

It took about four seconds.

"There," she said.

Then, immediately: "*No.* Wait—"

And her grip tightened hard enough to make the priestess wince.

"There's **three**."

Bettan, fourteen, cheerful, in the refectory with the others. And two more in
that room. Girls who went quiet for a week sometime in the last month, and came
back lovely, and started asking everyone about their families.

Nobody had noticed. Why would they? A homesick child getting happier is the
thing you *want* to see.

"Magda — Santo had five twists of hair. He'd used one. Four left, and Sana's was
one of them." Gardwen was grey. "They weren't collecting *vessels*. They've been
**filling** them. Here. For a month. In a school full of forty girls, four
hundred yards from that stair."

Nerissa came up out of the water very slowly.

"The four twists were the ones they hadn't done yet," she said.

She looked at the lit windows of the refectory, where forty girls were being
counted and complaining about it.

"Then how many *have* they done?"
