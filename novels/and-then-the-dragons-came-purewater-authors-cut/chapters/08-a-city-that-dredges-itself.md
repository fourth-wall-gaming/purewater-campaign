# Chapter 8 — A City That Dredges Itself

MAGDA'S FIRST PLAN WAS THE OBVIOUS ONE AND SHE PUT IT ON THE TABLE
straight away, because it had the advantage of being what actually happens to
rich fools in this city about twice a month. Put him somewhere on the approach.
Leave the gold on him. Purewater thieves will kill a wealthy idiot for his rings
quite happily, and everybody knows it, and the Watch will write it down without
looking up.

Constantine did not hesitate. "Yes. God help me, yes. It's the only story that
holds. A rich border boy comes to the Pearl with his father's gold on him and
doesn't know the water, and he's found in the morning face-down off the Shoals
with his purse cut and his rings gone." A glance at the ring on the table.
"You've already done the hard part of that."

Then she stopped, and did the thing she is actually good at, which is finding
the hole.

"But the body's in *my* canal. Behind *my* water door. And it went in heavy and
it didn't come up. The outflow runs west past the Lullwater on the ebb — that's
two hours off yet, and until then he's on the bottom in about nine feet of
water more or less where you put him. After the ebb he could be anywhere, and
'anywhere' includes the grating at the Shoals, which they clear every morning
with a *crowd* watching."

Which is how they came to be out on a canal in the dark with a boathook and a
line and a thirteen-year-old, and how they came, after the girl had been taken
back up the slipway wrapped in Magda's cloak, to be sitting in a skiff with a
sack in the bottom of it and a decision still to make.

---

There were two ways and Constantine laid them both out like a woman reading a
ledger.

Leave the ring on him. Then whoever finds him strips him, and inside a day
there is a Pearl thief trying to sell a di Teufel signet to a fence, and
Crowbill's people hand that boy to the Dragon Knights themselves rather than
have the trouble, and it is finished, and it was never here.

"But the moment that ring turns up, the Baron knows his son is dead. And a
father who knows tears the city apart looking for the *why*."

Keep it. Then he is a drowned foreigner with his purse cut, and he stays
nameless for two days, maybe three, while the Baron is still expecting him at
supper.

"That's time. But there's no thief in the story, and 'nameless drowned man' is
a story the Watch stops believing the moment somebody comes asking after a
missing lord."

As for where — the Lullwater. Past the bridge on the north side there is a
stretch of bad water and worse people, and men wash up there twice a month with
their boots gone and nobody asks a single question. Two hours' poling. And two
hours out on the canals with him in the bottom of the boat the whole way.

Magda shrugged.

"Keep the ring. Keep the clothes and the cloak. How about we do this — he's
never found?"

Constantine looked at the sack for a while.

"Never found is harder than it sounds in a city that dredges itself," she said.
"You weight a man and drop him in a canal, and in six or seven days the gas
brings him up with the weights still on him, and then it isn't a drowning any
more — it's a *murder*, and the Watch works murders."

She counted it off on her fingers, in the dark, in a boat.

"So it's one of two. **Burn him** — and the only fires hot enough are the Forge
Quarter's, which means a guild furnace, which means a guildsman who saw you and
wants paying every year for the rest of his life." She shook her head slowly.
"I've watched a man be bled that way. It doesn't end.

"Or **the deep channel**, past the Shoals, where the harbour drops off into the
sea. Opened so he doesn't float. Weighted. On the ebb, so what's left goes out
and not back in. The ebb starts in an hour and a half and runs about four.
That's the window, and there isn't another one before it's light.

"That's an hour's poling to the harbour mouth in a boat with no light on it,
past the Harbor Masters' pontoon, and out into open water in a skiff that is
not built for it. If a patrol stops us we are three people and a corpse and
there's no story that survives it."

She stood up and took the pole.

A beat.

And Magda, sitting in the stern of a boat she did not own, listening to a
madame with thirty years of running a house on the Pearl explain the tidal
constraints on the disposal of a nobleman, smiled to herself.

*Amateurs.*

"Do you know anyone who keeps pigs?"

Constantine stared at her.

"Give me twenty minutes with a saw and I'll have little old Santo here as a
nice pile of butchered meat we just feed to the pigs. Then he's shit and no
one's the wiser."

It took her a moment to be sure Magda was serious, and then a moment longer to
get past being sure.

"You're—" She stopped. Started again. "In my *house*."

"You've a boot room with a stone floor and a drain that goes straight into that
canal. I saw it on the way down."

She opened her mouth and shut it, because that is exactly what she has.

She turned and looked at the water for a long time. When she spoke again her
voice had gone somewhere flat and far away.

"There's a hog-yard on the edge of the Shoals. Man called Renko — he takes the
kitchen waste off four houses on the Pearl, mine included. Two carts a week,
before dawn, and one of them's tomorrow. He doesn't ask what's in the barrels.
He never has. He's not a good man and he's not a talker, and he'd take coin to
go on not being one."

She rubbed her face with both hands.

"It's *better*," she admitted, and hated it. "No boat. No Harbor Masters. No
four-hour ebb window and no Lullwater. It's all *inside*, and it's done before
the house wakes.

"But it's hours, Magda. It's hours of it, in my scullery, with twenty-two
people asleep over your head and a demon in my green room asking for her shawl.
And in the morning I have to stand in that room and take delivery of my own
bread."

From the stern, quietly: "I'll help."

Constantine laughed. One, with no humour in it at all.

"Of course you will."

She put the pole in and brought the skiff round to the slipway.

"There's a saw and a cleaver in the scullery," she said. "And a bone-tub. Don't
ask me why I know where they are."

---

It is not fast and it is not hard. It is the same job it always is. You find
the joint, you go through the joint, you do not fight the bone. The trick with
a big carcass is patience and a sharp edge and Magda has had fifty years of
both.

Gardwen held what needed holding and turned what needed turning and did not
flinch once, which surprised Magda not at all.

The drain did its work. The canal took the rest.

They talked, because you do. Magda said something about having killed one of
the enemy tonight, which was meant as encouragement, and Gardwen stopped with
her hands on the table and looked down at the work.

"He's the right age to be one of their sons. Not one of the men." She turned a
piece over without being asked. "Ten years ago he'd have been twelve. Sat at
somebody's table while it happened. Getting told the story after, probably, and
being proud of it." Her jaw set. "So no. Not one of the ones who came. But he
grew up into exactly what they wanted him to grow into, and he did *that* to
Emmeralda with four more women's hair in his cloak, and I'd have put the stone
through his head myself."

She wiped her wrist across her forehead and left a mark there she did not know
about.

"It's a start. It's not the one I came for."

The saw went on. Somewhere over their heads a floorboard settled and both of
them stopped, and it did not come again.

Then Gardwen said, not looking up: "Magda. That thing upstairs said *his father
would have to see*. See what?"

"I've no idea. Nothing good."

It took until the small hours. They scalded the slate and the floor and the
drain with three kettles, and Gardwen put the saw and the cleaver back on their
hooks exactly where they had been, which was somehow the worst part of the
entire night.

Four barrels, with Constantine's own house-mark burned into the ends.

Renko's cart came at the fifth bell and Constantine walked it down herself,
because it is not the sort of thing you send a boy with.

Above them, faintly, through three floors, a woman was laughing. Warm and easy
and delighted.

Neither of them said anything about it.
