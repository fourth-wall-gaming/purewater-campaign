# Chapter 9 — Twenty Minutes

MARISETTE RULES THE PEARL AND SHE DOES IT WITH LEDGERS, WHICH IS A THING
people find disappointing when they finally get into the room and discover a
woman in a plain grey wrap doing sums at four in the morning with her hair down
and no jewellery on at all.

She had been up since the third bell, and she had not been up because she was
worried. She had been up because her man on the bridge had come and told her
something and then she had sat with it for an hour and a half, deciding whether
to send men, and had decided to give them until dawn.

This is what he had told her. Noise at Constantine's, at about the third bell.
A woman screaming, and then not screaming, which is worse. The water door open
and a light on the slipway. The skiff out on the canal twice — once with three
people in it, and once with four.

And that on the second trip one of the four had been Ila. That she had gone
into the water. And that she had come out of it crying.

He had come and told her that last part because he knows Marisette likes Ila.

So when the black door opened at four in the morning and Constantine came in
with a wet bundle and two strangers, Marisette had already done most of the
work.

She poured coffee into three cups without asking whether anyone wanted it, and
pushed them across, and sat back.

"Right. Before you start."

She laid it out — the noise, the light, the two trips, the girl — flatly, in
order, without any pleasure in it.

"You've got twenty minutes before I have to be someone else. Tell me what's in
my island."

---

The old woman was not, by her own admission, one for talking, and then talked
anyway, and did it in about six sentences with no shape to them at all, which
is a delivery Marisette has learned to trust more than eloquence.

One of Emmeralda's clients had cut her open and put something in her. He had
been swimming away. She had killed him with a sling. They had got him out of
the water afterwards and turned him into pigment for disposal.

And then, in exactly the tone of a woman getting to the point at last:

*It was Hanzo di Teufel's son. He was planning on doing this to four more girls
and he wanted to show his dad.*

Marisette did not move at all while she talked.

When she stopped, Marisette reached out and turned one of the coffee cups a
quarter-turn on its saucer, and that was the only thing that happened for about
five seconds.

"Say the last part again."

"Hanzo di Teufel's son."

"Santo." Flatly, like checking a figure. "Twenty-two, black hair, very
handsome, laughs before the end of the joke."

"That's him."

"He came to my house three days ago asking after Asphodel and I turned him away
because I didn't like his hands."

She is completely still when she says this and her voice does not change at
all, which anybody who knows her would find alarming.

"I sent him to *Constantine's*."

Constantine said, "Marisette—"

"Don't." Not sharp. Just closed.

---

She went through the evidence the way she goes through stock. The ring she
barely glanced at — turned it once, set it down. The letter she read twice. The
coins she pushed aside.

Then she came to the four twists of oiled silk and opened the first one with
her thumbnail, and the copper wire and the long dark red hairs came out into
the lamplight.

And Marisette stopped.

She sat looking at it for a great deal longer than she had looked at anything
else.

"Constantine. Who at your house has red hair?"

"Nobody."

"No." She laid the hairs flat on the desk and separated them with one
fingernail, four or five strands, precisely, the way you lay out evidence. "
Nobody at mine either."

She looked up, and for the first time since they had come in she was not
running the room.

"There are two women on this island with hair that colour, and I know them
both, and neither of them works. One of them is a Watercrafter's daughter."

Her jaw tightened.

"The other is the High Priestess of the Lake Lady's own niece."

---

The girl told her tale then, because the old woman nodded at her, and told it
the way you would give a report — no shape to it, nothing arranged, which is
somehow the worst possible way to hear it.

Nine of them. Eleven years old. She went back in the morning and counted them
all and her brother was not there. He was seven. They had taken him alive and
taken only him and burned the rest so nobody would count.

"That boy in the canal said his father would *have to see*. See what he'd made.
That means his father makes things too. And his father has had my brother for
ten years."

Marisette sat back.

"A litter," she said slowly. "Curtains sewn shut. Guards facing inward."

"Aye."

"He entered a champion for the Single Combat. Nobody has seen the man. It's
been the joke of the week — the Baron's mystery swordsman. Not one soul in this
city has laid eyes on him."

She picked up the ring, finally, and turned it over.

"But a champion nobody has seen is not a champion. It's a *thing being kept*.
Men who are proud of a swordsman show him off. You parade him at the Siren's
Call, you let the odds move, you take money off the Guilds — that is the entire
point of entering one." She set it down. "Hanzo di Teufel has not let a living
soul look at his. He has him behind a wall and a litter with the curtains
stitched, and his guards face inward, and you don't face inward to keep people
out. You face inward to keep something *in*.

"How old would your brother be now?"

"Seventeen," Gardwen said.

It came out flat and immediate, the way a number does when you have counted it
every year for ten years.

"He'd be seventeen. Santo was twenty-two." Her hands had gone white on her
knees. "It's not him."

"No," Marisette agreed. "It isn't."

She let that sit exactly long enough.

"Seventeen is young for the Single Combat. It is not too young. I have seen
younger die in it."

The clock behind them went on being expensive.

"I'm not telling you it's your brother, girl. I've no idea and neither have you
and I'd not have you break yourself on a hope." Said almost gently, which from
her appeared to cost something. "I'm telling you that the thing in that estate
is being hidden, and the boy who came out of it was carving women up to impress
his father, and that his father is at this moment three streets from the
Governor with a demon-worker's blood in him and an entry in the Lady's own
Tourney."

She stood.

"Which makes it my island's business now, and I don't have twenty minutes any
more."

---

She looked at the old woman properly then, the way she had looked at the hair.

"Who are you?"

"Magda. I keep pigs."

"Mm." She did not smile. "You killed the Baron's heir with a sling from a
doorway in the dark, at what — thirty feet? — and then took him apart in a
scullery and had him in barrels before the fifth bell." A beat. "You keep pigs."

"Aye. And other things."

She accepted that. She wrote one line in the ledger and blotted it.

Then she gave them the only job in Purewater that nobody else could do, and she
was honest about why.

"Every face I own is known on this island. My people can't get within a street
of that estate without it being reported to somebody by noon. You two walked
through the water-gate yesterday morning. Nobody in this city knows your names.

"That is worth more than anything else you've brought me tonight."

She sat back down and picked up her pen.

"So: sleep. Eat. And this afternoon I want to know what is behind that wall.
Not a rescue. Not a fight. *Looking.*"

And then, without looking up:

"And when you find out whether that boy in the litter is seventeen, Gardwen —
you come to me before you do anything about it. Not after."
