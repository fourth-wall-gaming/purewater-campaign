# Chapter 3 — A City With No Ground

THE CUSTOMS MAN AT THE WATER-GATE HAD A SYSTEM AND HE WAS PROUD OF IT,
and it had worked all morning, and it was about to stop working.

Carts and cargo paid by the wheel. Foot traffic paid a tourney-week head-toll,
or produced something that made it not worth his morning to argue, and he had
already turned two people back and waved three nuns through without looking up.
He had a tally-board under his arm. He had put a lot of himself into that board.

Magda did not wait to be got to. She went to him — straight up the outside of
the line past the fishmonger and the family with the goat, which is wrong, and
everyone in that queue knew it was wrong, and she could feel forty people
deciding at once not to be involved.

He turned round with his mouth already open. Something practised. *Back in
line, mother.*

She looked at him.

She has put her hand over the eyes of men who were shouting an hour before, and
gone out afterwards and fed the pigs, because the pigs do not care. Whatever
that does to a face, it has had fifty years to do it to hers. She let him have
all of it.

"We're going in."

The mouth closed.

He looked at the staff, which is not a walking stick and has never once
pretended to be. He looked at the wet grey animal watching him with the total,
patient attention of something that has never in its life been told it is a
good girl. He looked back at her face and did not find anything there that was
negotiating.

She could see him reaching for the words — *toll*, *licence*, *by order of the
Harbor Masters* — and she could see each one arrive, look at the situation, and
decide against it.

"There's a fee," he said. It came out with no starch in it at all.

"There is."

She let the pause go long enough for him to understand that she was not going
to fill it.

He looked down at his board. "Two on foot," he told it. "And a *dog*." He wrote
*dog*. Then he underlined *dog*, as though the underlining were a form of
protest that history would one day recognise. "Muzzled inside the Merchant's
Quarter, that's not me, that's the Guild, and if it goes for anyone it's your
neck not mine—"

"Understood."

"—and I never saw it."

"You never did."

He stepped aside. He did it small, so it wouldn't look like stepping aside.

And as she went under the chain-boom he said it, not quite to her, in the
flattened voice of a man reasserting something to himself: "Everyone's mad this
week. It's the Tourney." And then, because he could not help it, because the
thing had been sitting in the top of his head since dawn:

"Third lot today gone through with something they won't show me."

---

The noise arrived first. It comes up off the water and off the stone at the
same time and hits you from underneath. Then the smell: salt, tar, fish, hot
oil, wet rope, and under all of it the sweetish standing note of a city that
sits in its own water and has made its peace with the arrangement.

Gardwen got six feet inside the gate and stopped dead with her back against a
wall and her hand knotted in the wolf's fur, breathing like somebody who has
just been put down somewhere with no trees in it.

"There's no *ground*," she said.

"Aye. It's all boats and barges and slipways here, lass."

She learned it the way woodland people learn things, which is badly for ten
minutes and then all at once. Magda kept her on the inside with a hand flat to
the wall, and after the third bridge she stopped looking at her feet, and after
the fifth she was reading the water — which boat has right of way, which shout
means *move*, which of the poled barges will take a passenger and which one is
somebody's home. Ruhi hated every part of it and did it anyway, low and close,
ears flat, a wet grey misery at heel.

The ferryman across the Lullwater charged them for the wolf and did not charge
them for Gardwen, and would not explain why.

Bunting everywhere. Bad wine on trestles. Somebody's cousin selling favours in
di Teufel colours off a tray, dragon-scale green, three for a copper, and doing
extremely well out of it.

Away over the roofs a bell started up, and then another one, ragged and out of
time with the first. Then they stopped. Nobody they passed seemed to know what
they had been for.

---

The Pearl is an island and it is the loudest quiet place in the city. Narrow,
tall, swept, scrubbed, and lamplit at three in the afternoon because the alleys
there never really get daylight. Music from somewhere above. Two women on a
balcony watching the street the way farmers watch weather.

Halfway along a canal-side row there is a green door under a carved lintel — a
sylph with her wings folded, worn nearly smooth by two hundred years of hands.

Magda knocked the way she had knocked twenty years ago, and the woman who
opened it was a great deal older than that knock remembered.

"*Magda.*"

"Constantine."

"You're not dead." Said with genuine delight, one professional to another.

"Not yet. It keeps being suggested."

Constantine took in the rest of it in a single pass — the young woman standing
much too straight, the staff, the wolf — and to her enormous credit the only
thing that moved was one eyebrow, and only slightly.

"Beds," she said. It was not a question. "It's Tourney week, I've got the city
and half the border in my lap and every room's spoken for—" She stepped back
and held the door wide. "—so you'll have the top back room over the water, and
it's cold, and the shutter sticks, and I'll bring a brazier up. Bring the dog.
If it eats a client I'll take it out of your purse."

The hall smelled of beeswax and orange peel and, underneath, the honest fug of
a house where a great many people sleep. A girl of about thirteen came past the
foot of the stairs with an armful of linen twice her size, saw the wolf, stopped
dead, and dropped the entire load.

"*Ila*," Constantine said, without heat.

The girl was not listening. She was looking at Ruhi with her mouth open and her
whole face lit up like a feast-day.

And on the landing above, leaning on the rail with her hair down and a cup of
something in one hand, was a woman who did not appear to be in a hurry about
anything, ever.

"Constantine," she said down the stairwell, amused. "You've brought home a
*wolf*."

"I've brought home a *friend*, Emmeralda. The wolf is incidental."

Emmeralda came down the stairs the way water comes down stairs, took Magda's
chin in two fingers and turned it, unhurried and professional and examining.

"Not one day. Not *one*. You were a hard old woman when I was a young one and
you're the same hard old woman now, and I've had two decades of people telling
me *my* face is unnatural."

"Mine's a curse."

"Mine's an *asset*."

Then, later, at the bottom of the stairs, with something under the brightness
for just a moment: "You didn't come for the Tourney."

"No."

"No." She accepted it, and filed it, and did not push. Which was, in the event,
the last decision anybody in that house made freely for some time.
