# Chapter 21 — Two Voices, One Throat

THE BOY DOES NOT HAVE A NAME AND HAS NOT HAD ONE FOR TEN YEARS, WHICH IS
not the same as having forgotten it, and is worse.

They call him nothing. That is the honest description of it. The dressing-man
says *hold still* and the razor-man says *other side* and the one who brings the
bowl does not speak at all, and the knights outside the door refer to him, on
the rare occasions when they refer to him, as *it*.

He is seventeen. He is thin the way people are thin when somebody else decides
what they eat. The whole upper left of his chest is a long-healed, re-opened,
re-healed, re-opened mess, a wound that has been made and remade in the same
place four hundred times, and it is the only clock he has.

And there is a man in him.

---

They are not friends and it would be obscene to say so, but they have been in
one room for a decade and there is a kind of arrangement.

The man is a soldier. That is the shape of him. He is unfailingly, exhaustingly
courteous, and he apologises, and he means it, and it does not help. When they
open the boy in the mornings the man goes very quiet and stays quiet for hours
afterwards, and the boy has worked out that this is not weakness but a sort of
withdrawal, the man taking himself as far away inside as he can go so that the
boy can have the room to himself while it hurts.

When they make him fight, the man does it, and the boy is not there for that at
all, which is the nearest thing to a mercy in his life.

And in between they argue.

That is what the crews hear through the deck and cannot stand: not screaming.
*Arguing.* Two voices out of one throat having a row for hours, and one of them
is a boy and one of them is not, and the one that is not sounds like a man being
told to do something he thinks is dishonourable.

The morning cut is not only for the wear. It is to keep them tired. You bleed a
man to keep him from arguing.

---

That night the ship changed its noise.

The boy noticed before anyone. He has nothing to do but notice. The hiss along
the hull that has been there for five days stopped, all at once, and the cabin
got colder by a degree, and the man in him came awake so fast that it hurt.

*Something is happening.*

Then the bell. Then the bell stopping, which was worse. Then a great deal of
running about that was not the ordinary running about.

Then a noise from somewhere forward that neither of them had heard before, and
the man — who has been in this boy for ten years and has, in that time, been
enthusiastic about precisely nothing — said, in the boy's own head, with
something the boy could only describe as *appetite*:

**"Oh. Oh, that's the death-goddess. Listen to that."**

---

There were two of them in the cabin with him and neither was a soldier.

The one with the bowl was already backing into the corner with his hands up. The
one with the razor was standing over the cot, and he had heard the order come
down from the deck — *cut the boy's throat* — and he had not moved yet.

The boy watched him not move. He would like to be able to say he was brave about
it. He was not. He was seventeen and tied into a routine and he lay there and
watched a man decide whether he was the kind of person who does that, and he
could not think of one single thing to say that would help.

Then the beam came out of its second bracket and the door went inward.

---

What came through it was an old woman.

She was in bloodied mail and she filled the doorway and she was screaming, and
she had an axe, and there was something behind her on the deck that had recently
been an extremely dangerous man.

The razor-man dropped the razor. It hit the boards and skittered under the cot.
Then he sat straight down on the deck with his hands on his head — which was the
single most sensible thing anybody did on that ship all night — and started
saying *I only do the dressings, I only do the dressings* over and over.

And the boy said "*Don't*—"

And the man in him finished it, in a voice a thousand years older:

"— **she's not here for you.**"

---

The old woman pointed the axe at the razor-man and did not move.

The boy understood, later, that she was trying with everything she had not to
close those eight feet, and that it was costing her more than the fighting had.
At the time it simply looked like an old woman shaking in a doorway making a
noise through her teeth that was not words in any language.

And the man in him got an elbow under them and sat up, and the ruined chest
moved, and he winced on the boy's behalf.

"**Woman of the death-goddess.**

"I have been listening to you come up this ship for a quarter of an hour. I know
what you are and I know who sent you, and I have been waiting for somebody to
come with that particular smell on them for a *very long time*."

Then the boy's face contorted, because they were both trying to use one throat
at once.

"— hurts, it hurts when he talks, *please* —"

"— **I know. I am sorry. It will not be much longer now.**"

And then, to her:

"Get us off this ship before the other one comes back with my bones."

She got two words out. Ground through her teeth. Not a greeting and not a
promise — the acknowledgement one soldier gives another across a yard.

"**General Thuban.**"

And the man in him said, with something like relief:

"*Ah. Good.*"

Then she turned round and went back out onto the deck, because that was where
the rest of them were, and the boy did not see the next minute and the man did,
and the man did not describe it to him, then or ever.

---

Some time after that a woman came and stood in the doorway and did not come in.

She had a staff in her hand and she had one hand on the frame and she stood
there for about a minute and a half.

The boy did not know who she was. He wants that understood. He looked at a
stranger of about twenty-one in woodland leathers and felt absolutely nothing at
all, and was ashamed of it, and could not help it.

*Do you know her?* the man asked. Not out loud. He can do that.

*No.*

*Look again.*

And it was the man who worked it out, because the man has a thousand years of
faces and the boy has ten years of a cabin, and what the man said next he said
gently and out loud so that she would hear it too, and it was short, and
afterwards the boy's whole face changed.

Then the man did the last generous thing available to him.

He stepped back.

He took himself as far away inside as he could go and gave the boy the room, the
way he does in the mornings, except that this time it was not so that a child
could be hurt privately.

And the boy's mouth moved.

What he said was one word, and it was not her name, because he was seven when
they took him and there are things a seven-year-old calls a big sister that are
not a name.

Her legs went. She caught herself on the edge of the cot and then she just put
her forehead down on his shoulder — carefully, on the right, away from the wound
— and stayed there, and he got one arm up around her head, and neither of them
made any sound that carried as far as the door.

A wolf came over the rail from somewhere, soaked, and poured herself into that
cabin and lay down under the cot without being asked.

And the man in him, from a long way off, said one thing more, and then nothing
at all for the rest of the night:

**"Good. Now hold on to that, because I am going to need you to be very brave
tomorrow, and you have already been braver than anyone will ever tell you."**
