# Chapter 20 — The Mouth

THE MOUTH IS A MILE OF SLOW BLACK WATER WHERE THE HARBOUR LETS OUT TO SEA
with the city's lights behind you and nothing at all ahead, and the ebb had
started, and you could feel the pull of it under the punt, patient and enormous.

Six punts, no lights. Crowbill's twelve were exactly what he had promised: canal
men in dark wool, no armour worth the name, barefoot, each with a short blade
and a coil of line and a grapnel wrapped in sacking so it could not ring. They
talked in a low register that does not carry over water. None of them had asked
what was on the ship.

Crowbill himself was not there. Of course he was not.

Ravella was. She was in the bow of Magda's punt in something that was not a
dress, with four of hers in the water alongside — you could not see them, but
occasionally the surface did something a surface should not.

Gardwen was amidships with her staff across her knees. Ruhi would not get in the
boat and was somewhere on the north bank keeping pace, which nobody had been
able to prevent.

Magda was in mail, with a dead captain's axe across her back and fourteen stones
in the pouch, and she had said something about not being a very good swimmer and
possibly needing help, and had meant it as a joke, and it was in fact the plain
truth. She swims about as well as Gardwen does, which is to say badly and not
for long, and she had thirty pounds of riveted mail on top of it. If she went
into that channel she was going straight to the bottom of it and staying
there. The nearest canal man grinned at her in the dark
and tapped the grapnel line. *You go up from the boat, mother. Feet dry.*

---

Then the lights changed at the pier end of the harbour, a mile back. A lantern
up and down. Another.

She had cast off.

A long time later — twenty minutes, half an hour, an age of sitting in the dark
— she came down the channel under a scrap of canvas and the sweeps, unlit but
for a single shaded lamp at the stern, and she was enormous close to, and even
at two hundred yards you could see the white where her hull met the water and
hear the *hiss* of it carrying flat across the surface.

Ravella turned round in the bow.

"When I go in, that water will kill anyone who isn't mine for about a minute
while it turns. After that it's cold and it's climbable and I can hold it as
long as I'm conscious." She waited. "So say when, and be sure. Because I only
get to do this once tonight."

Two hundred yards. One-fifty. A hundred. Eighty. Men on her deck, forward,
looking where she was going.

"**Now.**"

Ravella did not answer. She went over the side without a splash and the water
took her like it was expecting her.

---

The water went first.

You do not see her do it. What you see is the white line along that hull *stop*
— forty feet of hissing steam just ceasing, mid-length, like a candle pinched
out, and the dead-water slick behind it going dark and ordinary and cold.

Six punts moved at once. Grapnels went up in sacking and landed with a sound
like a fist in a pillow, and canal men were on the lines before the hooks had
set, going up the side of a moving ship barefoot with knives in their teeth, and
it was the most casually competent thing Magda has ever watched anybody do.

But a ship that has hissed for five days and suddenly does not is a ship that
has changed its noise, and there was a man aft who had lived beside that sound
long enough to notice its absence.

A voice on the quarterdeck. Not a shout. A question.

Then a shout.

Then a bell.

---

She did not go up the hull. She is seventy and in mail and she knew exactly what
she was, which was a woman with a sling and a clear line at forty feet of rail
with the sky behind it.

The first man leaned out over the side with a knife, going for the grapnel lines
— which would drop three men into cold water and the ebb. Her first stone went
past his head close enough that he flinched back, which bought the line about
three seconds and no more.

She was already loading the next one. That is the thing about a sling. You are
never really out. You are only ever between.

And the second stone was the best shot she has ever made in her life, and she
would like it noted that she has no idea how.

He had turned inboard, half-lit by somebody's dropped lantern, side-on at forty
feet — and the stone took him under the raised arm where the scale gapes.

He did not fall over the side. He folded sideways onto the rail with his arms
over it, and the knife dropped past her into the water, and he stayed there
hanging half over the edge with his legs still on the deck, which is worse for
everyone aboard than if he had gone in, because now the men behind him had to
get past him and the whole rail was blocked at exactly the spot they were
massing.

Two more canal men went up the far line while that was happening.

---

Then the bell stopped. The shouting stopped. It went very quiet up there, very
suddenly, which was the most frightening thing that had happened all night.

A single lantern shutter opened on the quarterdeck and swung out over the side,
throwing a cone of light down onto the black water, and it began to sweep along
the hull, punt by punt.

Looking for the boats. Looking for her.

The lantern made him a target and he knew it and he did it anyway, which tells
you something about him.

She put a stone through the middle of him before it got there.

She *heard* it hit. Forty yards and dark and she heard it — the flat thud of a
stone going into a body that has nothing on it but wool, because the man does
not wear armour, because he has apparently never in his life expected to need
any.

The lantern dropped. Not thrown. *Dropped*, out of a hand that had stopped
working, and it went over the side and hissed out and the quarterdeck went black
again.

Somewhere up there a man said something short with no breath behind it.

---

The rage came up out of her on the rope.

She had not let it since the woods and it does not arrive like anger. It arrives
like warmth, like getting into a hot bath, everything narrowing to the next
handhold and the noise above. And it is the only reason a seventy-year-old woman
in a mail hauberk with an axe on her back got up forty feet of wet line at all.

She came over the rail into it.

A lantern on its side by the mainmast throwing everything sideways. Two Dragon
Knights down that she could see and one canal man, face-up, not moving. The mass
of it forward where the boarders had got in first — knives against scale, and the
knives winning, because there are nine of them and because a man in scale on a
wet deck at night is a slow, loud, obvious thing.

Aft, on the quarterdeck steps, Blau on one knee with a hand pressed flat into
his own middle and the other holding the rail, and he was not out of the fight.
He was *directing* it, in that voice that does not rise, and three knights were
peeling back from the bow to form on him.

And behind him, under the quarterdeck, the barred shutter — from this side a
door, with a beam across it and a knight standing in front of it who had not
moved from that spot since the fighting started and was not going to.

Vell's axe came off her back.

---

She went up that deck making a noise that stopped the fighting at the bow for a
full second.

It is not a war cry. It is older and uglier than that — the sound you make over
a byre full of dead stock in a bad winter — and there were words somewhere in
the middle of it about violators, and stealers of souls, and rapists of the
dead, and every canal man on that deck decided at once that whatever that was,
it was on their side.

The man in the way had a sword and a shield and it did not matter in the
slightest. The axe came over the top of the shield — a size larger than it had
any right to be, in hands that were not feeling the weight — and went through
the scale over his belly with a sound like somebody standing on a crate. He sat
down against the mast. His legs simply stopped being interested.

Eight feet to Blau.

He had watched all of that happen and his face had not changed.

"*That's* her," he said. "The northern woman."

A pause, and something that might have been a laugh with no air behind it.

"Nobody told me she was **seventy**."

---

It went badly for a while after that, and she will tell you so.

She evaded two spears and both evasions put her flat on her back on a wet deck
in a hauberk, which is what evading costs and what parrying does not, and Blau —
still not raising his voice, still on one knee with a hole in him — said: "Don't
crowd her. She's on her back. Spears."

Which is exactly the right thing to do to a downed woman with an axe.

She swung up from the deck at a knee and there was no power in it and no reach,
and the axe stuck for half a second in wet oak, and a spear went into her right
thigh through the mail skirt. The rage halved it. She felt the pressure of it
and nothing else, the way you feel a hand on your shoulder, which is the most
frightening thing about being like that.

Then Blau got off the steps.

He did it slowly, with one hand still pressed into his own middle, and the two
guards moved aside for him without being told, and at three feet he looked down
at her and drew a knife left-handed. A working knife. Not a duelling piece.

"You're the pig woman.

"He was a foul little animal and he'd have been the ruin of the house inside
five years." Perfectly level. "You've done the Baron a service and he will hang
you for it, and I'll be there."

He is an extremely good man with a knife and he could not make it work, because
there was a hole in his abdomen that she had put there from a boat forty yards
away in the dark, and every time he bent at the waist something inside him
refused.

He went down onto one knee to reach her and stopped halfway with his teeth
locked, and the knife came in short and slow and wide, and he had to put his
other hand on the deck to keep from going over.

Neither of them touched the other.

For a second the two of them were simply face to face on a wet deck, a foot
apart, both bleeding, seventy years apart in age and neither able to finish the
other.

Then he said, quite quietly, with what breath he had:

"Ah."

And he looked over her shoulder, past her, at the barred door under the
quarterdeck.

"*Cut the boy's throat.*"

---

She came up off the deck at him — not standing; lunging, all of it from the hips
and the one good leg, with the axe going out low and flat.

And she did not take the leg.

She took the *hole*. The one she had put in him from a boat in the dark forty
yards out, that he had been holding shut with one hand for five minutes because
he is a man who does not believe in armour.

The axe went in where his hand was and took the hand with it.

He went flat. There was a moment where he was looking at the mast, and then
there was not anybody looking at anything.

The two guards saw it happen from four feet away.

That was the second thing that night that stopped a fight. The first was the
noise she made coming over the rail. Blau was the reason those men were still
standing in formation on a deck they had already lost, and now Blau was a shape
on the boards with an old woman lying next to him.

One of them stepped back.

---

"**MAGDA — DOWN!**"

She was already down. She got flatter.

Whatever Gardwen had been saving came down the length of that deck. You do not
see it. You feel the deck go cold and wet in a rush from bow to stern, and then
every rope on that ship remembers that it used to be something growing.

Cordage, weed off the rigging, wet line coming alive off the pins — going round
ankles and wrists and throats, and *pulling*.

It caught both guards. It caught three knights forward. It caught two canal men
who would be very rude about it later.

And it caught the knight at the door, with the beam half out of the brackets,
two feet from a boy behind it.
