# Chapter 17 — Nobody Ever Came Back

THORNE HAS SAT IN THE SAME CORNER OF THE MOIST OYSTER FOR ELEVEN YEARS
and buys nobody a drink, and there is a widely held view in that house that he
is a harmless old man who tells stories, which he has worked extremely hard to
encourage.

He was Order of the Lake for thirty-one years before he was not, and they do not
throw you out of that for nothing.

He watched her cross the room and he had her before she reached the table.

The mail under the borrowed cloak. The mud on the hem. The particular grey-green
weed dried on her boot that grows in exactly one place in this city.

He moved his cup six inches to the left, which is an invitation, and said:

"Sit down, mother. You've had a night."

She had not said a word yet.

"Two nights, at a guess. One of them wet. One of them worse.

"I'll tell you what *I've* had, since you're wondering. I've had a girl in here
at seven this morning saying Constantine's house has a physician in it and won't
say why. I've had a lad off Renko's cart telling anyone who'll listen that some
old woman knows about horses. And about an hour ago I had two of the Baron's
people asking whether anybody's seen a big northern woman with a face like a
spade."

He looked up.

"I said no. I'd like it noted that I said no *before* I knew what I was saying
no about, which is the sort of thing I'll want paying for eventually."

---

She offered to pay him to tell them yes, and then to tell them a few other
things besides, and Thorne put his hand flat on the table and stopped her.

Because people never understand what they are buying, and he has got tired of
finding out afterwards.

"You've just offered to pay me to tell a man a lie.

"I don't sell lies, mother. I sell *stories*, and the difference is that a story
has to hold up when it's repeated by an idiot in a different tavern three days
later. You can't just hand me a falsehood and a coin. You have to give me
something that has the *shape* of true.

"A lie collapses. A story goes and gets *itself* believed."

Two conditions, and they are not negotiable. He will not put a name on anybody
who would hang for it — if the story wants a body it uses a dead one or a made-up
one, never a living neighbour. And he is told what it is *for*; not everything,
but enough that if it goes wrong he knows whether he ought to be sorry.

"Meet those and my price is small and strange: I want the *true* version.
Afterwards. All of it, in order, told to me once and never written down.

"So. What do you want Blau to hear?"

---

And then she did the thing that nobody ever does.

She gave him the true one first.

A randy young nobleman carves up a courtesan to put a lust-demon in her, gets
the charm right and botches the escape, falls in the canal and catches a
slingshot to the temple.

Thorne went very still while she talked. When she had finished he did not say
anything for a while. He turned the chalk over three times.

"You've just done the thing nobody ever does," he said at last. "You've handed
me the *true* one first."

He was, if he is honest, faintly offended by how well it worked.

"And it's better than anything I'd have built, because every part of it is
checkable. There *is* a physician at Constantine's. There *are* four houses on
the Pearl that won't take di Teufel coin by tonight once the Pearl hears why.
There *was* a young lord in that house on the night, and there are eleven people
who saw him come in and none who saw him leave.

"That's not a story I have to sell. That's a story that sells itself house to
house and comes back to me by Thursday with improvements."

Then he held up a finger.

"But you've a problem in it and you've walked straight past it. The truest
version has an old northern woman with a sling in it. And there is a man on a
ship asking after an old northern woman with a face like a spade.

"So here's the craft of it. Everything you told me stays *exactly as it is*. And
the stone comes out of nowhere.

"He fell in the canal and *the water took him*, and the Lady's channel does not
care for demon-work, and a man who profanes a Pearl house with a lust-demon and
then goes into the sacred water — well." He spread his hands. "That's not even a
lie, is it. That's a *sermon*. Half this city will believe it before I've
finished saying it, and the other half will believe it harder because the temple
hasn't denied it."

He finally drank.

"As for how I feel about the good Baron." Something moved behind his face. "I
was Order of the Lake for thirty-one years, mother. I know what a stair under
Lost Isle is for. You didn't mention one. You didn't have to — you've got weed
on your boot that only grows in the drowned streets, and you came in here asking
about the dead.

"Tell me I'm wrong."

---

She laughed and said he was not wrong, and gave him the rest of it, and nudged
the girl into telling hers.

Gardwen told it the way she told Marisette, with no shape to it. Nine of them.
The counting in the morning. The brother who was not there.

And then the name.

Thorne put the chalk down.

"Say that again."

"Thuban Eta. The knight in the boy."

---

Here is a thing about being Order of the Lake for thirty-one years. There is a
great deal of it you can put down afterwards, and a small amount that is simply
in you, and one of the things that is simply in Thorne is the second-year lesson
about the Eta company.

"Thuban Eta was real. That's not a story name. That's a *muster roll* name.

"The Dragon Kings had knight-companies, and the Eta were one of them — the
letter's a company mark, not a family. And Thuban is the one they teach you
about in your second year, because of how he died.

"He wouldn't burn a town. That's the whole of it. The company was ordered to
fire a valley and he refused, and argued it in front of the others, and about a
third of them came round to him. So the ones who hadn't took him out on the
water at night to talk it over, and they put him over the side in his mail with
his hands tied.

"Drowned. By his own company. For refusing an order that even *they* thought was
filthy — that's the part that damns them. They knew he was right and they killed
him for making them know it."

He looked up.

"And that is *exactly* the kind of dead man you can bind. You don't hold a shade
with iron, mother. You hold it with the thing it can't put down." He tapped his
own chest. "You keep him in the moment of it. Tied hands and cold water and his
own men's faces looking down at him — you keep him *there*, and he'll pull
against it for a hundred years and never once get out, and every time he pulls
you can use the pulling."

Gardwen said, very low: "That's what's in my brother."

"That's what's in your brother. And it has been for ten years, and it has hated
every hour of it, because a man who wouldn't fire a valley is not going to enjoy
being made into a champion."

He pushed the chalk away.

"Your goddess said *lay him to rest*, and everyone at this table has been
thinking that means a door and a ritual. It might not. You don't lay that man
down with a stair.

"You lay him down by *undoing the betrayal*. By someone standing in front of him
and taking it back on behalf of the ones who did it.

"The door gets him out of the boy. Something else has to make him *willing to
go*."

---

The old woman told him he was mad.

Which is fair, and is the correct response, and he was pleased with her for it.

*That was a thousand years ago. Who the fuck could do that now? The kings
themselves and all their armies were laid to rest. Every child knows that.*

"You're right," Thorne agreed. "There's no company left to answer for it and no
descendants worth the name. If it wants the *actual men*, it's impossible and we
may as well go home."

He held up a finger.

"But that's not how a debt like that works. It never wanted the men. It's been a
thousand years — Thuban knows they're dust. What holds him isn't a grudge
against nine specific bastards.

"It's that **nobody ever came back for him.**

"He went over the side and the water closed and that was the end, and no
company, no order, no god, no sister — *nobody* has stood over that water since
and said 'this was wrong, and he was ours, and we're taking him home.'"

And then the druid girl said the thing that finished it, slowly, in a voice she
had not used all night.

"There's a thing we do in the wood, for a beast that's died badly. In a snare, or
under a fall, where it couldn't get free. You don't pray it away. You go and you
*sit* with it, and you say out loud that it wasn't right, and then you carry it.
That's the whole rite. Somebody has to actually pick the thing up.

"It doesn't need the ones who did it. It needs somebody willing to *stand where
they stood* and answer for it. Not because it's theirs — because they're the one
who turned up."

Thorne let that sit exactly as long as it deserved.

Then he laid his card down.

"And who exactly do we know that serves the god of the ones nobody sings about?

"Not the battle-dead, you said. The other kind. In the bed, in the byre, in the
bad winter. A knight drowned in the dark by his own friends for refusing to burn
a village, and left out of every song since, in every land, for a thousand
years.

"Mother — whose *is* he, if he isn't hers?"

---

Nobody said anything for a moment.

Then the old woman said "Fuck me," and then "Hel," and then, with the particular
exhausted affection of a woman who has been outmanoeuvred by her own deity:

"The old lady has her ways. OK. I'll do it."

Thorne picked up his cup and finally drank from it properly, like a man closing
a ledger.

"Well," he said. "There it is."

---

And then, before she left, she took her kettle helm off and set it on the table
and leaned in across the cup and put her forehead against his.

Gently. For her.

There was still an audible knock.

Thorne sat back with his eyes watering slightly and a hand going to his brow,
and he was not displeased at all.

"*Ohh*," he said. "That's a northern thing."

"That's a northern thing."

"I've read about that." He worked his jaw. "It reads considerably better than it
feels."

The girl mopping had stopped mopping. The dog under the table had reconsidered
its position entirely. Gardwen had put her face in her hand.

Thorne straightened his collar with enormous dignity.

"Thirty-one years in the Order, two of them at the Governor's table, and no one
has ever done that to me. I've been given a chain of office and a commendation
from a Prince and neither of them landed like that."

He picked the chalk back up.

"Go on then. Get out of my corner."

And as she was standing, without looking up:

"Mother. When they ask me this morning — and they will — I'm going to say I've
never seen you.

"That's on the house."
