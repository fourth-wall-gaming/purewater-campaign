# Chapter 6 — Four Still Full

THE QUARTER OF AN HOUR THAT CONSTANTINE HAS DECIDED NOT TO ASK ABOUT WENT
like this.

Magda had gone at the cloak once already, on the slipway, in the dark, with feet
coming down the boot-room stair — and had not finished. Outer pockets first,
because that is where a hurrying man puts things: a few coins, a folded scrap
gone to pulp in the canal, a lump of resin or wax.

Then her hands had found weight. Something small and hard sewn in behind the
lining at the breast, with no seam to it on the outside, needing a knife and a
minute and a light.

She had none of the three, and there were people on the stair, so she had put it
over her arm and gone up.

She got as far as the door of that room and stood in it for about four seconds,
which was long enough.

Then she went back down the passage, because there is nothing an old woman can
do for a thing like that and there was something an old woman could do about a
wet coat.

Two doors along there is a linen closet with a lamp on a bracket.

She took the lamp, put the cloak on the floor, and opened it out flat.

Gardwen's knife would have been quicker and she was not going back into that
room for it, so she used the thumb-edge of her own belt knife, and the stitching
gave after a bit of arguing.

Two things came out of the lining.

The first was a **ring**. Heavy gold, a man's, big enough to want a knuckle,
with a device cut into the face: a beast coiled round on itself, long, with its
tail through its own jaws. Good work. Old work. Sewn into a cloak by somebody
who did not want it on his hand that night.

The second was a twist of **oiled silk**, still dry inside. In it, a few long
hairs bound at one end with fine copper wire, and a flake of something brown and
hard that was not wax.

The hairs were not dark. In the lamplight they were dark red.

Emmeralda's are black to the root.

---

Then she took the whole thing apart properly, unhurried now, with a lamp and the
lining already open, seam by seam, the way you go through a carcass when you are
not in a hurry and want to know what the animal died of.

**The coins.** Eleven of them, and wrong for Purewater — heavier, older, off a
different mint. Border coin. He had not changed money since he arrived, which
means he arrived recently and had not needed to.

**A tailor's mark**, stitched inside the collar where the wearer never looks.
Not a Purewater house. A little tower with three windows, embroidered rather
than stamped, which is a mark for somebody whose custom is worth the extra work.

**The pulped paper**, teased apart with the point of the knife and dried against
the hot lamp glass. Most of it was grey mush. Two things survived: part of a
line in a fine clerk's hand — *—ee days before the Lady's Tourney, at the hou—*
— and the bottom half of a wax seal, broken, with the same beast on it as the
ring.

**And in the left cuff**, folded over twice and stitched flat so that it would
not rattle, a second twist of oiled silk.

Empty. Whatever had been in it had been used.

The first twist had three more like it wrapped in the same bundle.

All full. All hair.

None of it black.

---

She found Gardwen in the passage afterwards, out of the room now, with her back
against the wall and her hands hanging. There was blood to both elbows and it
was not hers.

"Where did you go?"

Magda leaned in and told her quietly. *I killed him in the water. Slingshot to
his skull. Found his cloak too. He's got all sorts of weird shit, look…*

Gardwen went very still while she told it and did not interrupt once.

"Good," she said about the killing. Flat, immediate, no hesitation in it at all.

Then, a moment later, quieter, as the rest of it arrived:

"—and bad. Because someone paid for that cloak."

She took the twist of silk and turned it under the lamp and held it a long time,
and her jaw worked.

"Hair and wire." She shook her head, and there was something close to anger in
it, and it was aimed at herself. "I know what a fever looks like in a badger. I
know nine things you can do with willow. This is—"

She handed it back.

"I don't know what this is, Magda. I've never seen anything like it and I don't
know anyone who has."

Which is, in its way, the most useful thing anybody said in that house all
night. She is not a woman who fills a silence with a guess. When Gardwen of the
Ash Vale tells you that she has no idea, you can build on it.

She looked at the bundles in Magda's palm and counted them, silently, her lips
moving, and got to four, and stopped.

Because they had both heard that number about ten minutes earlier, out of the
mouth of the thing in the green room, said warmly, as a piece of household
gossip.

*He said he'd come back for the others. Four more, he said.*

It is one thing to be told a number by a demon that is enjoying itself. It is
another to count it out in your hand.

"Four more," Gardwen said. "Four more *what?*"

---

Neither of them had an answer and neither of them said the obvious thing, which
is that somewhere out in that city there were four women going about their
evening — going to bed, or not going to bed, or standing at a window
complaining about the noise off the canal — who did not know that they had
already been chosen, and picked out, and *shopped for*, and that a piece of each
of them had come into Purewater sewn into a dead man's coat.

Above them, quite clearly, in a warm and perfectly ordinary voice, Emmeralda was
asking Constantine whether the young gentleman had enjoyed himself, and when he
might call again.
