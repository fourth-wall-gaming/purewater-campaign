# Chapter 18 — Terms

CROWBILL IS WIRY AND FORGETTABLE AND WOULD BE INVISIBLE IN ANY CROWD IN
the world except for a small crow inked on the back of his right hand, and he
was eating bread and cheese off a paper at his desk when they brought the pig
woman in, and he did not stop.

"The pig woman," he said. "Sit down. You've had a busy two days and I've heard
four versions and none of them agree about the wolf."

He took another bite.

"Go on, then."

She asked him whether he had ever wondered what was on the Baron's ship.

"Every day since it tied up," he said. "That's not a boast, that's my *trade*. A
foreign lord brings a deep-water hull into a canal city and moors it at a
private pier and lets nobody aboard — of course I've thought about what's on it.
So has every fence between here and the Shoals."

He wiped his fingers on the paper.

"And here's why it's still sat there with everything on it.

"**One**: ten men in scale and one way up. **Two**: it's a *ship*, so if it goes
badly they don't have to win, they just have to cast off, and then I've lost
people for nothing. **Three** —" he held up the third finger and looked at her
over it "— a Baron. Not a merchant, not a guild. A man with a border tower and
three hundred men and a long memory, who would spend two years and a lot of
money finding out who did it, because that's what men like him are *for*."

He sat back.

"Which is three good reasons, and you didn't come here without knowing them. So
you've got something that answers all three, or you wouldn't have got past my
door.

"So let's not do the part where you sell it to me. Just tell me the two things I
actually need. When is she sailing? And what do you want off her that isn't
cargo?"

---

She told him. Tonight, and everything on that ship is out of this city for ever
after that. And what she wanted off it was the champion.

Crowbill put the bread down.

He has been reading people across that desk for twenty years and he could not
read her, and he said so, because it was true and because it was the sort of
thing that tells the other party something useful about your position.

"See, now, that's a strange thing to want, and I can usually tell when someone's
shading me and I can't tell with you, which I don't enjoy." He tapped the desk
twice with one knuckle. The crow moved. "The cargo I understand. Cargo is cargo.
But the fighting man is the one item on that ship the Baron will actually
*chase*. Gold he replaces. A champion the day before the Lady's Tourney? That's
him publicly beaten in front of every Guild in this city.

"So you're not asking me to rob a lord. You're asking me to *humiliate* one, and
put my name on it, in exchange for goods I'd have to fence quietly for a year."

He leaned back.

"So here's my question, and I want the true answer, because I'm not putting
forty men on a pier for a story.

"**What's wrong with him?**

"Nobody hides a swordsman. You hide a thing that would *lose you everything* if
it were seen. And whatever it is, it's coming into my city on my boats and I'll
be the one holding it."

---

"It's not a humiliation," she said. "It's a rescue."

And then she told him, flatly, with no arrangement to it: a general dead a
thousand years, put into the body of that young woman's brother, bled every
morning to keep him quiet.

"Seventeen," Gardwen said quietly, from the side.

Crowbill looked at her, and then back at the old woman, and did not say anything
for a while.

Then: "A boy with a dead general in him."

"Aye."

"Being kept on a ship for it."

"Bled every morning to keep him quiet. There's a laundress on that pier who's
been boiling the sheets for five days and can tell you the shape of the cut."

He stood. He went to the window, which looks at a wall, and stood there with his
hands behind his back.

"I've a rule," he said to the wall. "I've a few. Most of them are about money and
one of them isn't, and the one that isn't is why I've still got a city to work
in.

"Children aren't stock. You don't sell them, you don't use them, and you don't
let anyone do it on my water and call it business."

He turned round.

"Your Marisette knows that rule. It's why we get on."

He came back to the desk and did not sit.

"So. I'd have done it for the cargo anyway. Now I'll do it *properly*, which is
different, and here are my terms — and they're not money.

"**One.** Everything on that ship that isn't the boy is mine, and nobody counts
it but me.

"**Two.** My name is nowhere. Not in the temple's version, not in your
storyteller's, not in a Governor's warrant if you ever get one. Tonight was
*thieves*, and this city has plenty.

"**Three** — and this is the one I care about."

He put one finger on the desk.

"When it's done and the boy's out and whatever you're doing with the ghost is
done, the High Priestess of the Lake Lady stands up and says publicly that the
canal folk kept her water clean. Not me. Not names. *The canal folk.*

"Because I have four hundred people who get moved on and blamed and dredged out
of the channel, and one sentence from that woman is worth more to them than a
Baron's whole hold."

He raised an eyebrow.

"Can you get me that?"

---

Then he asked the practical question, which is how forty poled men are supposed
to get past ten men in scale up one ramp, having already explained that this is
how people die.

And the old woman said: "You're the river men, I'm just a pig farmer."

And then she said the thing that made his face become interested for the first
time in the conversation.

"She has to leave to sail. Once she's off that pier she's not got four men at
the bollards and four at a ramp — she's just a hull in the dark with a channel
round her, and the channel's yours."

"It is." He was already pulling a scrap of paper over. "And she cannot get out
to sea without going through the **Mouth**, and the Mouth on the ebb is a mile
of slow water with the harbour lights behind it, and I have men who have been
going up the sides of anchored hulls in that stretch since they were nine years
old."

He drew.

"You don't need forty. You need *twelve*, in six punts with no lights, and you
take her from three sides at once while she's making way and everyone aboard is
looking forward.

"And the best of it — a ship under way has half her people at the ropes and the
rest looking where she's *going*. Nobody watches a wake."

Then he stopped drawing.

"One problem, and it's yours to solve, not mine."

He tapped the waterline on his own sketch.

"That hull *steams*. Whatever your Baron's got in her, it cooks the water where
it touches, and there's a dead slick round her the whole way out. I've had a man
put his hand in that water off the stern two days ago on a dare and he's still
got no feeling in three fingers.

"My people go up the outside of hulls. They cannot climb *that* one — not at the
waterline, and that's where you have to start.

"So. Between the lot of you, can anybody make forty feet of that hull cold
enough to touch for the four minutes it takes to get twelve men over the rail?"

---

Gardwen ruled herself out immediately, which she was right to do — she does bark
and bones and bleeding and could not cool a kettle — and then named the two who
could, and what each would cost.

Nerissa could try. It is her channel and that hull is wounding it, and she felt
it go thin from a mile off. But she had already gone past what she was sure of
once that day. She would be doing it from a boat, in the dark, in a moving
channel, against something that had been fouling her water for five days.

"Meaning she'd probably do it and it'd probably cost her more than she'd tell
us."

And then the other lot, that nobody had said out loud yet.

"The Mermaid's Court. Their chambers are *under Lost Isle*. We've been standing
on the roof of them all night and the only reason nothing came up those stairs
at us is that nothing chose to."

Crowbill set his pen down.

"**Ravella.** Siren's Call. Half of one and half of us, and she brokers for them
when they can be bothered to want anything. I've done business with her twice
and both times I came off worse and knew it at the time."

He looked up.

"She'd be able to. Water that's been made wrong is exactly their complaint, and
that hull's been bleeding into their front hall for five days."

A shrug.

"But you'd be asking the Court for a favour, mother. They don't take coin and
they don't take gratitude. They take *arrangements*."

---

Ravella came into the little room off the landing stage at the Siren's Call ten
minutes after Crowbill's name got them into it. Handsome, forty-ish, unhurried,
faint scales at the hairline that could be a trick of the light, and fingers
very slightly webbed which she made no attempt to hide, which is itself a
statement about who she thinks you are.

"You're the pig woman. You've had a remarkable two days and I've heard *nine*
versions." She pushed a cup across. "Crowbill sent you, which means you want
something from under the water. So let's save an hour: what do you want, and
what have you got that the Court might conceivably care about?"

The old woman said: "The demonologist Hanzo di Teufel and his festering shitpile
of a barge."

Ravella's cup stopped halfway to her mouth. She set it down without drinking.

"Say the rest."

And when she had heard it, the broker's smile went off her face entirely and
what was underneath it was much older and much less friendly.

"Let me tell you what 'thin' means from underneath, since nobody up here ever
asks.

"There are people living in the deep channels of this city who were here before
the causeway. Not many. They keep out of the way and they mind their own
business and their whole lives are *water that behaves*. And for five days there
has been a hot wound in the eastern channel that pushes ahead of the tide, and
every one of them has had to move — old, young, sick — and two haven't managed
it."

She said the last part very flatly. Outside the window the canal had gone glassy
in a strip about four feet wide.

"So yes. We mind."

Then she picked the cup up again and the broker came back, which was somehow
worse.

"But you'd have got that from a fishwife. Here's the part you'd have paid for.

"**They came down our stairs.**

"Under Lost Isle. Four days ago, three men with a lantern, digging out a door
that has been left shut and unmarked since before this city had a name. And they
*took* things off it — the small confused ones that sit near the threshold and
don't go through, which are ours to look after and nobody else's.

"The Court could not stop them. Won't say why and I've stopped asking. So we
have had our water burned and our threshold robbed in the same week, and we
have been able to do precisely nothing about either.

"And now an old woman walks into my house smelling of Lost Isle weed with a
dead captain's axe.

"How many of the taken have you seen?"

"Two."

"Where."

"One's dead. I put an axe through his head on Lost Isle last night and he went
face-down in the drowned street and didn't float. The other's roped in a cellar
under the Lake Lady's temple with two Swords on the door, and he talks.
Boatman. Ellick Sarn."

Something passed over her face at the name and she did not explain it.

"Ellick. He poled the Forge Quarter run. He carried for us twice."

She was quiet for a moment.

"Then you already have both of them and you didn't know what you had."

She sat back.

"Here is the arrangement, and I'll speak for the Court because they'll agree and
because there isn't time to go down and ask.

"**Give them back.**

"That's the whole price. Not the ship, not the Baron, not your boy — those are
your business and the Court doesn't care about a dead general or a barony. The
two you've got are *ours*, and they need to go back through that threshold and
be let go properly, and until they are they're neither ours nor dead nor alive,
and that is an obscenity."

She looked at her very directly.

"You should understand what you're agreeing to. The thing in Ellick Sarn came
off our threshold and it goes back to it. What's left of Ellick is — " a pause,
and she chose the word carefully " — *thin*. There isn't a version of this where
a boatman walks home to his supper. He said yes to a warm coat in a cold winter
and he has been paying for it ever since, and the kindest end available to him
is the right one.

"You'd be taking him to that stair and standing there while it's done."

She turned her cup again.

"And I understand that's your trade."

Then, briskly, the broker again:

"Do that, and I will come out to the Mouth myself tonight and put my hands on
that hull and make it cold enough to climb. For as long as you need, not four
minutes. And I will bring people who can be in the water beside it while it
happens, which your Crowbill's boys cannot.

"Two dead men, laid down properly, for one cold ship. Do we have an
arrangement?"

"Don't know where the second one is," said the old woman. "But sure. I'll do
what I can."

Ravella took her hand anyway. Her grip was cool and slightly damp and a great
deal stronger than it looked.

"You don't need to know where he is. He went into a drowned street off Lost Isle
with an axe wound in his head and did not float. He's in our water. We'll have
him on that threshold by dark and you won't have to look for him — you'll have
to *stand* there, which is the part that matters."

She stood and smoothed her dress.

"I'll be at the Mouth from the eighth bell with four of mine. And Magda — when
you're standing over those two tonight, say it out loud. Whatever your goddess's
words are.

"The Court will be listening from underneath and they will want to hear that
somebody up here said it."

---

Nerissa's answer came back across the water in her own square hand, on a scrap
of paper the size of a palm, carried by a gondolier who would have done it for
nothing.

> *Tell the crow he shall have his sentence — from the temple steps, at the
> Tourney, in front of the Governor and every Guild in this city. **The canal
> folk kept the Lady's water clean.** Those words, that place, and I will not
> soften them.*
>
> *I would have said it years ago if anyone had thought to ask me. That he had
> to buy it is a shame on my order, not on him.*
>
> *— V.*
