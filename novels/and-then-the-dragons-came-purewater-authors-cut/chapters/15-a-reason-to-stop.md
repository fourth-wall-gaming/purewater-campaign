# Chapter 15 — A Reason to Stop

THE TWO MEN WERE SITTING SIDE BY SIDE ON THE EDGE OF A SLAB IN THE DARK
and they had been sitting there without speaking for twenty minutes, and it was
Nerissa who put a name to what that meant.

"Two men who have sat in the dark without speaking for twenty minutes are
waiting for something."

Magda went up the drowned street alone with the staff across her body and the
water pushing at her thighs. Past the punt in its doorway with a young Sword
folded down beside it who did not look up as she passed. Sixty yards.

Then the big room. Half a roof, no wall on the water side, so she could see
straight in from thirty feet: a stone floor above the waterline, a raised slab,
and a heap of dragged-up weed and muck stacked against the standing wall. Where
they had cleared it there was cut stone showing, deep-carved, and the carving
went on down under the water at the edge, and it was a great deal older than
anything else on that island.

The red-slide lantern sat on the slab, shut.

And the two of them were sitting on the edge of it, facing the water, facing
her, in the dark.

She was twenty feet away before either moved.

Then both of them turned their heads at once. Not together, like men who heard
the same thing. *At once* — the same speed, the same distance, like two doors
on one hinge.

Neither stood.

One of them — sandy, thirties, a boatman's forearms — said in an entirely
ordinary voice:

"You're not her."

And the other one, without any pause at all, in the same voice, finished it:

"—she was meant to bring the girl."

---

"No girl," Magda said. "Just little old me. All alone surrounded by big scary
warriors."

And the stone went out on the last word.

It took the sandy one high on the right arm, on the outside, and she *heard* the
bone go from twenty feet.

The arm dropped. It did not swing. It simply stopped being part of him and hung
there.

He did not make a sound. He did not look at it. He did not look at her.

Both of them stood up at the same moment, at the same speed, as if the slab had
tipped them.

And the sandy one said, conversationally, with his arm hanging off him:

"That's going to make the poling difficult."

And the other one finished: "—we'll manage."

---

What followed took about a minute and a half and Magda has been in enough
fights to know that it was not really a fight, it was a discovery.

She parried the billhook and rolled her wrists and took it out of his hands and
put it in the water, and he watched it go with mild interest. She pivoted and
put everything she had into the sandy one's ruined arm and took it apart again
below the elbow, so that what was hanging off his shoulder had bone through the
sleeve, six inches of it.

He went back one step from the force of it. Then he straightened up.

He looked at the ruin of his own arm — and this time he *did* look at it,
curiously, turning slightly to see it better, the way you would inspect a torn
coat.

And the other one, standing empty-handed in the water beside him, said: "That's
the arm gone, then."

"Mm," said the sandy one.

Neither of them had bled a drop.

Then Gardwen came out of the dark forty yards up the drowned street at a dead
run with the wolf ahead of her, and Ruhi took the billhook-man off his feet
into the black, and the two of them went under together in a boil of white — and
he did not thrash and he did not come up for air, because he did not need any.
He simply got a hand in the wolf's ruff and began, methodically, to work it
deeper.

Magda brought six feet of seasoned ash down into the boil with her whole weight
behind it and the water took nearly all of it, which is what water does, and it
landed on the back of his skull like dropping a log. It should not have been
enough for anything. But it *did* something, because for the first time all
night one of them made a movement that was not smooth, and the hand loosened,
and Ruhi got her hindquarters under her and came backwards out of his grip with
a great sheet of water and half his sleeve.

He sat up with the back of his head split and no expression at all and said:

"Oh — that was the wolf, was it."

And Gardwen put her hand out flat toward the sandy one and said three words in
the old tongue, and the water between them went still in a ring, and he stopped
mid-step with his weight forward and the ruined arm still swinging gently from
the momentum he had a second before.

His eyes went on moving. Something in him was furious about it and none of it
reached his face.

"Ohhh," he said. Just that. Perfectly pleasant.

Gardwen had gone white.

"It shouldn't hold," she said. "Magda — that's *Hold Person*. There shouldn't be
enough person in him to grip."

---

The other one got hold of Magda in the end and would not let go, and Ruhi took
his leg out from under him, and because he would not let go she went down into
the black with him. He lay on the bottom of a drowned street holding her
forearm, not struggling, not pushing her down, simply waiting, because he did
not need to breathe and he had all night.

Her face was about eight inches under.

Gardwen put everything she had into one word and the hand opened — all four
fingers at once, like a catch releasing — and Magda came up off the bottom into
air and lamplight coughing canal.

He sat up beside her with his hands open in his lap, palms turned upward, and
looked at them.

"That wasn't me," he said, with what might almost have been interest.

---

And here is the part that is not a fight story.

He got up. He did not come at her again. He waded, unhurried, toward the slab
and the uncovered stone behind it, with his hands still open at his sides — and
Nerissa was between him and it, and she did not move, and neither did he.

They looked at each other across four feet of black water.

Then he said: "You're the Lady's, aren't you."

"I am."

"Good," he said. "She'll want to see this."

And he stepped sideways, out of her path, and gestured with an open hand at the
cut stone they had spent a day digging out of the weed.

Magda came out of the water and planted her back foot in the mud and swung the
way you swing at a fence post that has offended you.

Six feet of ash across the temple. Nothing between it and the bone.

His head went sideways past where a head goes. He dropped straight down where he
stood, with no stagger, and the water took him and closed over.

The body did not float.

Nerissa did not move. She was standing exactly where she had been, mace up,
four feet away, with her mouth slightly open.

She lowered the mace slowly.

"He had stopped," she said. Not accusing. Just stating it, the way a woman reads
a figure back to check she has it right. "He had stopped, and he was showing me
the stone."

---

Magda went for the other one next. The held one. The one who could not move.

She broke his good arm with the staff while he was frozen and could not flinch,
and he watched the staff come down and go back up and there was nothing behind
his eyes at all, and he said, "You've done that twice now."

Nerissa came through the water and stopped beside her. She was careful not to
take hold of her arm.

"Magda." Very level. "The other one had put his hands down. I don't say you were
wrong — I don't know what these things are and neither do you. But I will not
stand here and watch you take a helpless man apart piece by piece, so tell me
plainly what you want him for, and then we'll do that thing and stop."

And Magda, from somewhere a long way down inside the noise, said the only true
thing available to her:

*I'm a berserker. That means I fight until someone convinces me to stop.*

---

Nerissa has spent thirty years in a room where people lie to her, and she has
been watching this woman since midnight, and she read her completely.

Whatever she had been going to say next, she did not say it.

She changed her footing. She stopped standing like a woman about to remonstrate
and started standing like a woman at the side of a horse that is still running.

"Ah," she said. Very quietly. "*Ah.* You're not deciding anything, are you."

She lowered the mace all the way and let it hang.

"I've seen this twice. Once in a man at the Shoals after a fire, and once in my
own father." Her voice stayed completely even — not soothing, not raised, the
pace of somebody reading a list. "You're not cruel. You've simply not been given
a reason to stop yet, and nobody in this water has thought to give you one.
They've only told you to."

She moved round until she was in front of her rather than beside her, slowly, so
that Magda could see her do it.

"So. Here's the reason.

"Sana is in a boat forty yards behind us, nineteen years old, with her hair cut
off, watching. That girl has spent a day and a half in a hole in the dark being
brave, and what she can see from there is an old woman breaking a man who can't
move.

"That's it. That's the whole reason. Not the man — he's nothing to me and I'd
hang him tomorrow. **Her.**"

She held out one open hand. Not to take the staff. Just open.

Magda did not answer, and she did not lower the staff either.

She just stopped.

Water running off her, breathing like a bellows, everything in her face still
exactly where it had been — and standing entirely still in the middle of it,
which is not the same thing as being calm, and everybody there knew the
difference.

Nobody asked her for the staff. Nerissa did not move her hand away and did not
reach any closer.

Corvin and Ash roped the prisoner in silence, working around the ruined arm
rather than through it, which was more care than Magda would have taken.

And when the ring of stillness let go and the man came back to life mid-step and
found himself trussed and sitting, he looked up, and found her, and smiled for
the first time all night.

"You're the one who did the boy in the canal," he said. "The face like a spade.
He described you before he went."
