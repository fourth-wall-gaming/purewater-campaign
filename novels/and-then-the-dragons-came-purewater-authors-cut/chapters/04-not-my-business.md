# Chapter 4 — Not My Business

THE KNOCK CAME AT THE HOUR WHEN A BROTHEL STOPS BEING A HOUSE AND STARTS
being a business, and it was the kind of knock a young man gives a door when he
is entirely certain of his welcome. Three, brisk.

Magda was three floors up with a brazier going and a shutter that stuck, and
Gardwen was sitting with her back against the bed-frame cleaning mud off her
boots with a knife, and Ruhi was asleep across the door, and none of them had
any business with whoever that was.

Then a second voice in the hall. Young. Male. Well-bred. Border vowels.

And a name, clear enough through the floor to be certain of it: *Emmeralda.*

Magda stepped over the wolf and got the door shut without letting the latch
speak, and went down as far as the turn above the second landing, where the
overhang puts you in shadow and the hall lamp does not reach.

She has spent most of seventy years moving quietly around animals. She got
where she was going and nobody marked it.

From up there she could see most of the hall. Constantine with her back turned.
The young man facing her, hood back, dark clothes, road-dust still on the hem —
he had not changed since he rode in. Nothing in his hands. Talking quickly.
Constantine nodding in the way she nods.

Then Emmeralda came in from the parlour door, changed since the afternoon —
hair up, something green. She said something. The young man laughed.

Then she went up the near stair, the one that comes past the second landing,
twelve feet from where Magda was standing.

He followed her.

They would pass below her in a few seconds. The shadow was good. It was not a
wall. Staying put was about to become a contested thing, and there was no
particular reason to be found on a stair in a house that was not hers.

She withdrew.

*Not my business.*

And it *wasn't*. That is the thing to hold on to, afterwards, and she did hold
on to it, and it did not help at all. Constantine had run that house for thirty
years. Emmeralda was not a girl. Hel had not walked her three hundred miles to
mind a brothel door, and a woman who came in through the water-gate that
morning without paying does not go and tell a nobleman that she does not care
for his face.

She went back up. Gardwen did not ask, which was a thing Magda filed away about
her. Ruhi resettled across the door and the house carried on being a house —
the stringed instrument, someone laughing on the second landing, water working
at the stone under the window.

She lay on the bed with her boots off and her staff under her hand where it
always is, and looked at the ceiling, and after a while she went under.

---

Somewhere below, an hour later, a young man who was not nearly as good at this
as he believed attempted something he had never been trusted with, and — against
every expectation of his father's, and of his father's men, and very probably
of his own — he got it right.

Then he tried to leave, and made a comprehensive disaster of it.

---

There was no scream. That was the first thing, and it was worse than a scream.

There was a crash — something big going over, a table or a chest, and glass
after it — and then a man's voice, high and completely undone, saying *no no no
no* very fast. Then a door hitting a wall hard enough to shake the frame two
floors up.

Running feet on the canal-side stair. A slip. A worse noise: a body hitting a
landing full length, and something metal skittering away across boards.

More running. A shutter thrown.

And a splash in the canal below that was far too big to be a man getting neatly
into a boat.

Magda came up out of sleep on animal reflex because Ruhi was already standing.

Then the house began shouting at itself all at once — Constantine bellowing
with no words in it, a woman crying on the second floor, somebody hammering on
a door.

Gardwen was on her feet with the boot-knife held low and reversed, looking at
Magda, waiting to follow her lead.

The metal thing that skittered across the boards had not gone into the water.

And from the room the two of them had gone into: nothing at all.

---

Going down three flights in the dark at speed at seventy is not a heroic act and
Magda has never once pretended otherwise. Nothing dramatic happened. She did not
fall. She simply took the stairs at the pace a body takes them at the end of a
long life, one hand hauling on the rail, with the lamp on the second landing gone
out and the turns tight and every second of it audible to her as *not fast
enough*.

She met Ila coming up. The girl hit her rather than stopped — two fistfuls of
skirt, barefoot, white to the lips.

"She won't *open* it—" hauling, trying to turn her round, back up the way she
had come. "The door's shut and she won't open it and Constantine's got the bar
out and Emmeralda *won't answer*—"

Gardwen went past both of them toward that door without breaking stride.

And Magda stood there for exactly as long as it took to notice that the
canal-side stair kept going *down* — past the boot room, to the water door and
the slipway — and that not one person in that house was going that way. Every
soul under that roof was going up.

Down there in the dark it was quiet, and the water was still moving from
whatever had gone into it.

She took the girl's hands off her skirt.

She loaded as she went. Stone in the pouch, cord round the knuckles, staff
cocked back along the forearm.

---

The water door was unbarred and standing half open and swinging. Whoever came
through it had left it that way.

Four stone steps going down into black water, green and slick below the third.
A skiff moored to the ring at the bottom, riding oddly — the painter still tied
but the boat shoved out and drifted back, rocking harder than the canal
warranted.

The water directly off the slipway was still working. Not ripples. The deep,
slow turnover of something that went in heavy and recently.

One lamp on the far bank, forty feet off, its light lying flat on the surface.
Under that surface, nothing at all.

There was a shape on the second step down, right at the waterline, that was not
stone. She got a hand on it and hauled it up: a cloak, heavy with canal, dark,
good cloth, expensive, the hem still carrying road-dust under the wet. It had
caught on the mooring ring. It had not come off a man who was being careful.

She dropped it on the step behind her, because it could wait.

Then she looked out across the water and found something to shoot at.

---

Thirty feet out and moving left, upstream: the fast ugly chop of somebody
swimming badly and in a hurry. A head. The pale smear of a face turning back
toward the house. Then just the shoulders working.

He was making for the far ledge where the lamp was, where there was a set of
iron rungs up the wall. Forty feet of open water between him and them, and he
had covered maybe a third of it.

He had not seen her. She was a shape in a dark doorway and he was looking at
the upper windows, where all the noise was.

Here is the whole of the reasoning, and it took about a second and a half.

She did not know his name. She did not know he was a baron's heir, or that the
house would be turned upside down for him inside a day, or that a man with no
colour in his face at all would spend three days walking that city with her
description in his mouth. She did not know that killing him would put her
between a border lord and everything he had spent a century building, or that
it would cost a boatman his rest, or that in three days' time she would be
standing in black water on a drowned island paying for it.

She knew that somebody had gone into a room in a house where she was a guest
and done something to a woman in it that made a wolf refuse a doorway.

She knew he was thirty feet away and getting further.

And she knew she had a sling.

That is all the deliberation there was, and she has never once revised it.

---

Backlit against the far lamp. Dark water. A moving target. She has been putting
stones into rats and foxes and the occasional dog since before anybody now
living in that house was born, and the range was nothing.

She set her feet on wet stone. The cord came round and the staff came over, and
the stone went out flat across the water with that low buzzing note they make.

She did not hear it hit.

She *saw* it — the head snapped sideways, hard, wrong, the way a head does when
it has stopped being steered from inside.

The working shoulders stopped working.

He went under without a sound. No thrashing, no cry, no arm coming up to grab
at the world on the way past. The chop where he had been smoothed out into
ordinary canal, and the wake he had been dragging caught up with the place he
was not any more, and there was one hand above the surface for a moment, open,
not grasping at anything.

And then that was gone too.

Forty feet away the lamp on the far bank went on being a lamp. The iron rungs he
had been making for went on being empty.

The water closed and kept moving.

---

She stood on the slipway a while longer with an empty sling in one hand,
listening to it not stop moving, the way you stand at the side of a byre after
you have done the thing you came to do.

Behind her, up in the house, a woman started screaming and this one did not
stop. It was not Emmeralda's voice. It was older, and there were words in it,
and Magda could not make out one of them.

Then Constantine, cutting across it, shouting for somebody to get a light. Get
a light down here—

And feet on the stairs above her, coming down, toward the water door.

She picked the cloak up off the step and put it over her arm.

*Not my business.*

She had said that about two hours ago and it had been true when she said it and
it was going to be a very long time before anything was true in that way again.
