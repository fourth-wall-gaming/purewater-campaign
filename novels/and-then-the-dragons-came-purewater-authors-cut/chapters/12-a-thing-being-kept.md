# Chapter 12 — A Thing Being Kept

HESPER HAD SPENT THIRTY HOURS IN A GREEN ROOM ON THE PEARL WITH A DEMON
that would not stop talking to him, and he had come away with a notebook full
of very small handwriting and the settled conviction that boredom is the most
underrated interrogation technique in the profession.

He is an unremarkable man. That is deliberate and it has taken work. Plain dark
coat, physician's bag, a voice that does nothing at all, and thirty years of
being the person nobody in a room afterwards can describe.

He was in the corner of Marisette's counting room when the two of them came
back from the Forge Quarter at dusk, and he had been sitting there with a cup
of something going cold since before they arrived, and neither of them had
noticed him on the way in, which he permitted himself a small private pleasure
about.

Marisette listened to the whole of the recon without interrupting once. Eight
men. No banner. No Baron. The boat from the north-west. The barred shutter. The
barrows of clinker. One bag a day. The same place on the sheet. The cut
arming-points. And the laundress's name and the laundress's message.

At the end she said, "Eight." And then, differently, "*Eight*."

And then: "Ann Yannick will not lose that work, and she will not be alone in
that wash-house again after tomorrow. I'll see to it."

Hesper put his cup down.

"Your friend asked after the courtesan. She's alive. She'll stay alive. It's got
no interest in killing her — quite the reverse, it likes the house and it likes
being fussed over and it has been extremely helpful all afternoon, which is the
part I want to talk to you about.

"But I'd rather start with what you just said. Because a wound in the same
place, opened every morning, with a grey scorch round it that numbs the hands—"

He stopped.

"How long has that ship been at that pier?"

---

This is the part that Hesper has taught for years and never once had to use.

The Baron rode in through Caravan Square yesterday at midday. Marisette had it
from three people within the hour.

But the ship came before him. She came by sea, round the Mouth, and tied up at
that pier five days ago — Marisette knows because the Harbor Masters made a
noise about the berth and were made to stop making a noise about it.

Five days. And the laundress has been washing that bed for four.

"Then it started the day *after* the ship tied up, and the Baron was still
three days up the road when it did."

He sat forward.

"Do you see what that means? Nobody started anything here. That is a *routine*.
That is a thing that has been done to somebody every morning for however long,
in whatever house it was done in before, and they carried on doing it at sea,
and they carried on doing it here."

He picked the cup up, found it cold, put it down again.

"And they brought him by *water*. Twenty-odd knights ride three days up the
road in the open, and the thing in the litter goes round by sea in a hull that
steams. You don't do that for a champion. You do that because a road has
villages on it, and villages have people in them, and whatever is in that cabin
cannot be taken past people for three days."

Marisette said quietly: "Hesper."

"I know."

"Tell them what it said."

He was silent for a moment.

"The thing in Emmeralda spent the afternoon complaining. It's bored, it's fond
of the girl's memories, and it will talk about anything to anyone who'll sit
with it. And what it complained about, at some length, was that it had been
promised something better than a courtesan.

"It said its master's father keeps a *proper* one. And that his is 'the old
kind, the kind that remembers being a person.'"

---

The old woman grunted at that. *Like a ghost? Like a dead knight?*

And then she told him about her goddess.

Eleven nights. A woman with a wolf. A burning house. A knight in dragon-scale
who is dead and has not been told.

And the instruction.

*Lay the dragon warrior to rest.*

Hesper did not say anything for a long time. When he did, the unremarkableness
had gone out of his voice, which is a thing that happens to him perhaps twice a
decade.

"Say the last part again."

"Lay the dragon warrior to rest."

"*Rest*," he repeated. "Not destroy. Not banish. **Rest.**" He put both hands
flat on his knees. "Madam, you don't lay a demon to rest. There's nothing in a
demon to rest. You bind it or you break it and either way you may as well be
arguing with a fire.

"You lay a *dead man* to rest."

He stood up and took two steps and stopped, because it is a small room.

"'The old kind. The kind that remembers being a person.'" He turned round.
"That is a **shade**. A person who died and did not go where the dead go — kept,
and bound, and put somewhere. And you cannot keep a shade in a jar or a stone
for long; it wears through. You keep it in a *living body*, because a living
body renews itself, and the wearing-through goes into the flesh instead of the
vessel.

"Which is why somebody opens the same place on that boy's chest every single
morning. It is not torture and it is not a ritual scar. It's *maintenance*. You
bleed the host to keep the seat clean. The grey your laundress can't boil out
is what comes off the seam — that's the shade's wear coming out of him with the
blood, and it's numbing her hands through a copper of lye."

The druid girl had not moved at all.

"How long," she said, "can they keep a person like that?"

"Years," said Hesper. "If they start young enough that the body grows around
it."

She sat down.

---

He watched the old woman go over to her afterwards and put a hand on the back
of her neck, and it was not a gentle gesture, and it was exactly the right one.

"Seventeen," the girl said. "*If they start young enough.*"

"Aye."

"He was seven, Magda. I've spent ten years hoping he was alive." Her voice was
very steady and very small. "I never once thought about what for."

"I'm all right."

"You're not."

"No." A pause. "But I'll do."

---

And then the old woman asked about the bells.

Hesper had not thought about the bells. He is prepared to admit that. He had
been in a room with a demon for thirty hours and had not once considered that
anything was happening outside it.

Marisette set her cup down.

"Yes. Yesterday, just after midday. Everyone assumed it was for the Baron's
entry, because it started while the column was in Caravan Square and this city
will assume anything."

"It wasn't?"

"It was the Lady's temple on Temple Isle. And that wasn't a welcome peal, it was
ragged and out of order and it came from the water-tower, not the campanile."
Her mouth thinned. "That's the *fouling* alarm. They ring it when something goes
wrong with the sacred channel — a dead beast in the intake, a spill, a body.
It's been rung four times in my life."

Hesper said quietly: "And they stopped it inside a quarter of an hour."

"They did. Which they don't do unless somebody senior tells them to."

She looked at the old woman.

"The High Priestess Nerissa rang the alarm on the day Hanzo di Teufel entered
this city, and then somebody had it silenced before the third peal, and nobody
has said a word about it since."

She picked the pen up and put it down again.

"And her niece has red hair."

---

The old woman's face went hard at that, and she squared off at Marisette and
said the obvious thing, which is that there are only eight of them on that boat
and with enough men you could simply go and take him. She was honest that it
was not the smartest way. She said it was her way.

Marisette did not flinch and she did not laugh.

"Eight is eight. I can put twenty men at that pier before the sixth bell
tomorrow. Crowbill can put forty, and he'd do it for what's in your purse.

"And then I have twenty men against eight Dragon Knights in scale, on a deck,
at night, and I lose eleven of them and it's still even money. And by dawn every
one of them has a name and a mother on this island, and Hanzo di Teufel — who is
at this moment eating supper with the Governor's brother — knows to the hour who
came for his ship."

She spread her hands.

"It's not that it's stupid, Magda. It's that it's *last*. You only get to do it
once, and after you've done it there is nothing else you can do."

Which was correct, and Hesper let it stand, and then said the part that
actually frightened him.

"There's a worse problem. You'd be stealing a lit lamp."

He leaned forward.

"Whatever is seated in that boy is held there by something, and it is not the
ship and it is not those eight men. Take the body out and you have not freed
anyone — you have carried a bound shade into the middle of the Pearl with its
keepers coming after it, and the first thing that happens when the maintenance
stops is that the wearing-through goes somewhere else."

He looked at Marisette, then at the old woman.

"You cannot rescue him until you know what the anchor is. Otherwise you're not
rescuing a boy. You're moving a problem into a house full of women."
