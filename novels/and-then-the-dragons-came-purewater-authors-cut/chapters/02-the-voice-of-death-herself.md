# Chapter 2 — The Voice of Death Herself

MAGDA HAD BEEN PERFECTLY HAPPY BEFORE THE DREAMS STARTED, THANK YOU
very much. She was old. She had crops and pigs and a roof that mostly kept the
weather where it belonged, and a life that did not, on the whole, involve
killing anyone, which after a certain age is the sort of arrangement a woman
comes to appreciate.

The dreams were nightmares. They always are. One does not enter into an
understanding with the Goddess of Death and then get pleasant dreams about
orchards; there is a price for the contact and the price is that some part of
the dark is always looking back at you, however brief the visit.

There were three things in it and there were three things every night.

A young woman with a wolf.

A house on fire.

And a knight in dragon-scale who was dead, and walking, and had not been told.

Eleven nights of that, and always the same instruction at the end of it, in a
voice like a rusted hinge, under a blasted sky, with dire wolves howling
somewhere off in the dark where she couldn't see them:

*Lay the dragon warrior to rest.*

Not kill. Hel is specific by being unspecific. Hel points, and does not explain,
and expects you to do the arithmetic yourself, and if you get it wrong that is
apparently also part of the lesson.

Magda had turned it over for three hundred miles and it still would not come
apart in her hands.

And there was the other thing, which she would not have admitted to anyone: she
was seventy years old and she did not look it, and had not looked it for a very
long time, and that was the goddess's doing and not hers. It is a strange kind
of blessing that mostly means you outlive everybody you were fond of. She had
buried enough people to be extremely clear-eyed about the terms.

So. Three hundred miles, a quarterstaff, a staff sling, fourteen stones and a
purse with very little in it, and here she was at a ford outside a city she had
last seen twenty years ago, standing in a queue, with no further instructions
whatsoever.

And then a young woman came out of the treeline with a wolf.

---

Up close, the girl was worse than the dream had suggested. Road-grime worked
into the seams of the knuckles and none at all under the nails, which means she
washes what she can and has given up on the rest. Thin in the way of someone
who eats when there is food rather than when there is hunger. And young — much
too young for the flat, patient watchfulness in her face, which is not a thing
anybody is born with. Somebody put that there.

"Gardwen," she said, when she was asked. And then, in the voice of a woman who
has had this conversation before and expects nothing at all to come of it:
"You'll want to be somewhere else, mother. People who stand near me have a poor
time of it."

Magda grunted at her. It is not a word. It is the noise you make at a gate
that has swollen shut, and it carries about the same amount of argument.

"I've been dreaming of you, girl."

Then she walked past her — past, not at, on the wolf's side — and put her hand
into the fur at the base of the animal's neck, where the ruff is thickest and a
hand goes in to the wrist.

The wolf locked. The whole animal, shoulder to haunch, one decision made at
once, and its head came round and its jaw was four inches from Magda's forearm
and its breath was hot on the inside of her wrist.

She did not move her hand. She scratched, down, once, the way you work a burr
out.

The rigidity came out of the shoulders first and then the back, and the head
turned away, and the animal made a low sound in its chest that no townsman
would recognise as anything at all. Up north they would have known it. Up north
they would have said the dog had decided you were furniture, and furniture is
the highest rank a stranger gets.

"—*don't*," Gardwen said, three seconds too late, her hand half out and
stopping. "Ruhi doesn't let people do that."

"My goddess tells me you're someone I need to help," Magda said. "Don't know
why. That's what she told me."

"Your goddess." Slowly. "*Which* one."

And Magda could hear exactly what the question was. The girl was asking whether
this was one of the singing ones. Whether she was about to be told that it had
all been for the best.

"Hel," she said. "Death." The wolf's weight settled against her shin. "Not
death in battle." She said it the way you would correct someone about which
field the boundary stone is in. "The other kind. Everywhere else. In the bed,
in the byre, in the bad winter. The ones nobody sings about."

Gardwen went very still.

Magda has seen that look before, on people who came expecting to be sold
something. It is the look of a bucket that has had the bottom go out of it.

"No," Gardwen said quietly. "No, that's — that's an honest god."

Then, fast, like a woman pulling a splinter: "Does she show you a boy?"

Her hand had gone into the wolf's ruff beside Magda's. She did not appear to
have noticed doing it. Her knuckles were white.

There was a beat.

"No," said Magda. "Not a boy."

And she did not dress it, because she had delivered enough bad news across
enough gates to know that softening it only makes people hear it twice. A
knight. Dragon-scale. Dead and standing up and walking and nobody had told him.

She watched the girl's face do the arithmetic and arrive somewhere else
entirely from where it had been sent.

"Dragon Knights," Gardwen said. Flat. "It's Dragon Knights you dream of."

And then Magda knelt down in the mud.

It made a sound like a boot coming out of a bog. It was cold, and it went
straight through the wool, and she was seventy years old on the outside and
there was nothing dignified about any part of it. Behind her one of the
tumblers said something and another one laughed and was hit.

She held the staff out crosswise in both hands, the way it is done, the way her
grandmother had done it once to a man who died the following spring.

"I'm to fight for you, girl. Protect you and yours, however you'd ask it. Die
for you, if it comes to that."

Gardwen did not take the staff.

She stood there with her hand in her wolf's fur next to a stranger's hand and
her mouth doing something unsteady, and she looked hard at the middle distance,
the way people do when they will not do the other thing in public.

"Get up," she said. It came out wrong. "Get *up*. You'll — the ground's
freezing, you'll ruin your knees, get up—"

And she was reaching down and hauling, one hand under Magda's arm, and her grip
was a great deal stronger than it looked and not steady at all.

"Ten years," she said, very low, for nobody else. "Ten years and not one
person. Not one. And a pig-farmer kneels in the *mud* at a *ford*—"

She stopped. Swallowed it. Put the flat watchfulness back on like a hat, and it
did not sit right any more, and she knew it did not.

"I don't know what to do with you," she said.

Then she offered her forearm, wrist to wrist, the woodland version of the way
soldiers do it.

"Gardwen. My brother was taken by men in that armour. Ten years ago. He's the
only thing I have left to look for, and I'm three days behind that column, and
if your god has business with a dead man in dragon-scale then Hel and I want
the same road for a while."

Neither of them, standing in the mud at the ford with their hands in a wolf,
had the least idea that they had just described the same person.
