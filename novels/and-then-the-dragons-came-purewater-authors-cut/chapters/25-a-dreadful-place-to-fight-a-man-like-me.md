# Chapter 25 — A Dreadful Place to Fight a Man Like Me

THE BOX HAD BEEN BOUGHT OFF A CHANDLER FOR MORE THAN IT WAS WORTH: A
fitted wooden case with a sliding lid, the sort a merchant sends samples in.

The note took a while, because Magda writes like a woman who learned late.

> ***YOUR SHIP IS ON THE BOTTOM. YOUR MAN IS DEAD.***
> ***I HAVE YOUR CHAMPION AND HIS BONES.***
> ***— THE PIG WOMAN***

Gardwen read it over her shoulder and said nothing for a while, and then:

"You've spelled *champion* wrong."

"He'll get the sense of it."

---

Before that, there had been an hour of being an old woman at a fair, and it was
excellent.

She bought fried fish and found a place on the public rail and watched the
Archery, and for about an hour nobody in Purewater wanted anything from her at
all. It was the first hour like that in four days. She would like it recorded
that she enjoyed every minute of it and that the fish was very good.

And Nerissa had put the Lady's cloth on her to get her through that rope, which
had cost the priestess something and which she had said so, plainly, while she
did it with her own hands.

"I am going to put the Lady's cloth on a woman who beheaded a bound prisoner on
my island eight hours ago, and I am going to do it with my own hands, and I
would very much like you to appreciate what that costs me."

Magda had kept her face absolutely straight. It cost her something too.

"Don't," said Nerissa, when it was done.

"Wasn't going to say a word."

"You were *radiating*."

---

Marisette had got the servants. Three of her girls had worked Tourney service
for years and one of them — Corliss's youngest, eighteen, who has poured at four
Tourneys and is very good at being furniture — was in the Governor's enclosure
that afternoon because she always is.

Nobody had to be told a story. Marisette said *look at what's round the Baron's
neck and tell me* and they said *right you are*.

She went in with the wine four times over the hour and came out with this.

It is on him. Gold, a flattened oval about the size of a thumbnail, on a fine
chain, worn under the shirt — but the chain shows at the collar and the shape of
it shows through the green when he leans forward.

And he touches it. Not fidgeting. Every so often his hand goes flat to his chest
over it, briefly, the way a man checks a purse. And once, when a Guildmaster
leaned in to toast him, he moved his shoulder to put it further away.

"He does it about every quarter hour. And after he does it, he looks round."

Then she told them the rest, and her hands were not steady.

"He caught me."

Fourth pour. She was closer than she needed to be and she looked a half-second
too long, and when she came back up he was already looking at her.

And he smiled. And said — pleasantly, in front of two Guildmasters and the
Prince — "*You've very good hands, girl. Who do you serve?*"

She said the Guild steward's name, because it is true.

He said: "*Of course.*" And went back to his cup.

"He wasn't angry," she said. "That's what's wrong with it. He was **pleased**."

Marisette had her off the island and onto a boat before she told anybody, which
is the correct order to do those two things in.

---

From a hundred and forty feet, Magda saw almost nothing, and that was the point.

He slid the lid. He looked down into the box for about two seconds. He slid the
lid back.

He said something to the Guildmaster beside him and the Guildmaster laughed.

Then he set the case down beside his chair, stood, and made a small apologetic
gesture to Prince Emeric — *forgive me, a moment* — and Emeric waved him away
good-naturedly, and he stepped out of the back of the canopy with his cup still
in his hand.

He did not hurry. He did not look round the ground once.

He is very, very good.

"Did he—" said Gardwen.

"He read it. He's going for his boat."

---

Down the public rail, through the fried-fish crowd, past the ale trestles, round
the back of the colonnade where the ground gives way to worked stone and the
noise drops off like a door closing.

The water-stair behind the Governor's enclosure. Twelve feet wide, a wall on the
left, the channel on the right, thirty-odd steps down to a landing where three
boats were tied and two bored oarsmen were playing dice.

Out of sight of six thousand people.

She got there first, because she was closer and because he was being unhurried
and correct.

Gardwen came down behind her and put herself flat against the wall inside the
arch. Ruhi was somewhere above on the colonnade roof, which Magda had decided
not to think about.

Footsteps in the passage. Unhurried. One set.

Then a second set, quicker, catching up. "*My lord — my lord, the herald's
calling the Single Combat—*"

And Hanzo di Teufel said, perfectly pleasantly, from fifteen feet the other side
of the arch:

"Yes. Tell them my champion withdraws. Injury in training."

A pause.

"And find out who delivered that box."

---

She loaded flat against the wall inside the arch with the vestment sleeve pushed
up out of the way. Stone in the pouch, cord round the knuckles, staff cocked
back along the forearm.

The servant's footsteps went away up the passage.

His did not.

He came through the arch. Close to, Baron Hanzo di Teufel is a well-made man of
about fifty in dragon-green with grey at the temples and very good boots, and
the cup was still in his left hand, and he had not spilled a drop of it.

He was looking at the boats.

He got three steps down before he registered that there was an old woman in a
Lake Lady novice's vestment standing against the wall with a loaded staff sling.

He stopped.

He looked at her for a moment — at the vestment, at the sling, at her face — and
she watched him do arithmetic a great deal faster than the serving girl had.

"Ah," he said.

And then, with what sounded like genuine warmth:

"**The pig woman.**"

He had not put the cup down.

"You've cost me a great deal in three days and I would very much like to know
how you did the ship. I'll pay for that answer, and I pay well." He tilted his
head slightly. "Or we can do the other thing, and you should know before you
choose that you are standing on wet stone at the bottom of a walled passage,
which is a *dreadful* place to fight a man like me."

Behind him, in the arch, unhurried, blocking it, Temerach Nebulo had an arrow
already on the string.

Magda spat on the step between them.

"Hel's waiting for you, Baron. Time you went down."

---

He threw himself sideways and he is quick for fifty and the stone found him
anyway.

It took him high in the chest and turned him completely round, and the cup went
out of his hand and shattered on the step, and he landed badly on wet stone and
came up on one elbow with his court silk torn open and a bruise the shape of a
fist coming up underneath it.

And now he was not warm and he was not amused.

He got a hand up — palm out, fingers spread, and she had seen exactly that once
before, at a ford, when a druid asked a favour of the water — and the air in
that passage went *dry*.

"**MAGDA—**" from the arch.

And behind him, from the top of the stair, absolutely calm:

"**My lord Baron. Put the hand down.**

"You are under arrest by warrant of the Order of the Lake Lady for the
profanation of the Tourney and the sacred channel. There are two Guildmasters
and a Prince forty feet above us.

"I have been trying to say those words to you for four years.

"**Put the hand down.**"

He was on the steps between an old woman with a loaded sling and the finest
archer in the realm.

His hand did not go down.

---

He was prone and unarmoured and he had chosen to finish the spell rather than
defend himself, because he is a man who has never once been hit by anything.

She did not throw the staff. She went down the three steps with it held short in
both hands like a woman going at a rat in a grain store, and brought the end of
it down on the wrist.

There was a crack that was mostly the staff and partly not.

The hand shut.

And whatever had been gathering in that passage — the dry, tight, wrong feeling
of air being *asked* for something — came apart all at once with a sound like a
held breath being let go, and the temperature came back, and somewhere above
them a gull started complaining again.

Hanzo di Teufel made a noise through his teeth. It was the first entirely honest
sound he had made in three days.

He cradled the arm against his chest and looked up at her from the wet stone
with his hair come loose and his silk torn and a stone-bruise spreading under
it.

Fifty years of never once being touched, undone on a service stair by an old
woman with a stick.

"You *hag*—"

---

And then his left hand — the good one — moved very slightly toward his own
collar.

She slapped it down out of the air like a woman swatting a wasp off a pie.

Then she took a fistful of green silk and collar and chain, all together, and
put her face about four inches from his.

And she did not rip it. Not straight away.

She held him there, eye to eye, on the wet stone, and let him get a proper look
at what had been coming up that stair for him — which is not a knight and not a
priestess and not a magistrate. It is an old woman who keeps pigs, sent by a
goddess he has never once thought about, wearing a novice's vestment she has no
right to, with fried fish on her breath.

He stared back and he was, for the first time, entirely out of arithmetic.

Then she pulled.

The chain did not break at the clasp. It broke across the back of his neck, and
it took skin, and he cried out.

And it came away in her fist: gold, a flattened oval about the size of a
thumbnail, warm — *properly* warm, the warmth of something that has been alive
against a chest for a hundred years — and a great deal heavier than a thing that
size has any right to be.

Inside it, something moved.

Not rattled. *Moved.*

---

He went very still underneath her.

And then, in a voice that had lost every bit of its charm:

"You have no idea what you are holding. You cannot *open* that, you stupid
woman, if you open that here he will go into the nearest—"

"**I know.**"

That was Hesper, standing in the arch at the top of the stair with his bag,
entirely out of breath, having run the length of the colonnade.

"She's not going to open it here."

Temerach came down and put a boot between the Baron's shoulders and got his good
arm behind him, and it was over, and it had taken about ninety seconds.

Above them, six thousand people were being told that the di Teufel champion
withdrew through injury, and there was a certain amount of booing.

---

Hesper took the locket in a folded cloth and did not touch it with his skin, and
put it in a box within a box, and his hands were not quite steady.

"Bones, door, and the latch," he said. "That's all three. For the first time in
a hundred years, all three are in one place."

And Magda, who had been waiting an hour to say it, told Temerach about the
second pass. Six shafts, six hits, two into the same target at the furthest and
fastest, and the apology to the judges.

*One of the most beautiful things I've seen in my life. And I'm a lot older than
I look.*

And the finest archer in the realm, who had just arrested the man she had hunted
for four years and who had a knee on him at the time, went completely to pieces
for about a second and a half. She went pink to the ears and looked at the step
and said:

"The sixth was high."

"It was in the target."

"It was *high*." Firmly.

Then, quieter, and clearly having to make herself do it:

"...Thank you. That's a kind thing to say and I never know what to do with
them."

And she hauled the Baron up onto his knees.
