Every child on the Pearl can tell this story, and no two tell it the same way. What
follows is the version most of them agree on. Nobody alive knew her, and the
council rolls of her time were lost to the damp long ago.

## The girl who was bought

Two hundred years ago, or near it, the Pearl was a fishing islet with a garden lobe
at one end. A merchant prince of the North Quarter, whose name the story never
gives, kept a walled summer house on the small islet beside it. He bought a girl of
fifteen from her family on the Shoals. He didn't hire her and he didn't marry her,
at least not at first. He bought her outright, with a bill of sale witnessed by two
councillors, the way a man buys a boat. Later he married her, because a wife was
cheaper to keep than a purchase was to explain. She lived behind the wall on the
islet, and he built the bridge with a gate at her end of it so that she couldn't
leave.

## The night the basin took him

The legend says she spent eleven years at the window over the basin. She lit a lamp
in it every dusk, because the lamp was the one thing in the house that was hers,
and she talked to the water. The basin answered her.

One night in the autumn storms her husband was crossing back from the Quay by boat.
The basin was flat calm, and it took him all the same: the boat came back empty and
dry, and his body was never found. The Pearl's women say she asked the water for
him, and the price was that she would never set foot off the Pearl again. The
North Quarter says he was drunk. Both sides agree she was at her window with the
lamp lit, and that she didn't go into mourning.

## The house and the rule

She was the **Widow** from that night on, and she was rich. She went across the
bridge for the first and last time, to the Pearl itself, and bought the biggest
house on the south shore. That house is **[[The Mother of Pearl]]**. She opened its
doors to women who had been sold the way she had been: sold to husbands, to
creditors, to the houses on the mainland. Her terms were the ones every house on
the Quay still keeps: **a night is for sale; a person is not.** No bill of sale for a
woman would be honoured on the Pearl, and no man who brought one would be let in
anywhere on the island again. That is the rule [[Marisette]] enforces, and the
reason the Mother of Pearl is the oldest house on the Quay and has never needed a
sign. Other houses opened along the shore to sit under the Widow's protection. By
the time she died the fishing islet had become the Pearl.

She kept her bargain. She died in the walled house on the islet without once
crossing to High Isle, and she was buried in its garden. The house has been
shuttered ever since. It still goes by **the Widow's House**, and the bridge to it
by **the Widow's Bridge**.

## The lamp

Her lamp is the magic in the story, and the part the Pearl still acts out every
night.

At dusk the doorman of the Mother of Pearl lights a taper from a lamp that, by
tradition, has not gone out since the Widow lit it. Each house on the Quay lights
its own lamps from that taper, then the posts of the **Lantern Bridge**. That is why
the bridge lamps are never let go out before dawn. The Pearl's women believe three
things about that flame:

- **No one under it can be owned.** A bill of sale signed under the Pearl's lamps is
  void, and so is a contract of indenture or a binding of any kind. Ink fades off
  the page by morning, they say, and a chain unlocks on its own.
- **The water listens.** A woman in real need who speaks into the basin under the
  lamps will be heard. The story doesn't say by whom.
- **The Widow's gate opens for the sold.** Anyone running from an owner who reaches
  the Widow's Bridge will find the gate unlocked, however it was left.

Hedge-wizards on the Quay say it is old folk magic: a Shimmer worked into a flame
and kept alight two hundred years by people who believe in it. The Order of the
Lake Lady says nothing on the subject, which on the Pearl counts as a yes.

## How it plays

*GM: none of this is confirmed, and nothing in the scenario depends on it.* Let it
stay a legend unless the table needs it not to be. Some uses:

- **After the carving.** [[Santo di Teufel]] tried to *bind* something into a woman,
  under the Pearl's lamps, in a house that keeps the Widow's rule, and it went
  wrong. Every woman on the Quay will draw the same conclusion by breakfast. It
  costs nothing to let them, and it hardens Marisette's response.
- **The Lantern Bridge going dark.** If the di Teufels or the Knights ever put out
  the bridge lamps, the whole Pearl takes it as an act of war. It is a cheap
  provocation for the Baron's side, and a costly one in the end.
- **The shuttered house.** The madame of the Mother of Pearl holds the key to the
  Widow's House. What is kept there, and what the garden grave is like, is for the
  GM. A party that needs to hide someone on the Pearl, outside Marisette's
  authority, could be pointed at its gate.
