Agreed with the player on 2026-11-18, after an arc in which the city's worst
criminal was taken apart by one guardsman with a shout and a sling. The setting
already said Purewater was dangerous. The operations built from it did not. This
guide is the scale, written down so that it holds.

## What is normal here

**Somebody goes into the water most nights.** The Watch fishes two or three bodies a
week out of the Lullwater and the Shoals in an ordinary month, more in a hard one.
Most are logged *drowned* or *drink*, and nobody asks twice. A throat cut in a
Lullwater alley is a bad night, not news. Visitors are told to stay off the low
water after the night bell, and the locals don't need telling.

**Under the holiness, almost everyone is working an angle.** Respectable districts
lie in ledgers instead of alleys. Honest people exist, and they have learned to keep
their heads down. Kindness is real and rare enough to matter.

**Nobody trusts a uniform.** The Watch is ninety people for a city this size, and at
the posts that matter a sergeant's loyalty can be bought for two gold a week. The
low water remembers that the Prince's own signature ordered the Watch to *record and
refer* while the Baron's Knights took their boats. A guardsman who walks into the
Lullwater asking questions gets silence, lies, or a knife, until he earns better.
Make the player earn every witness.

**The low water is lethal.** The Lullwater and the Shoals at night: unlit stairs,
black boats with no lamps, dens with water doors, and people who have watched the
Watch walk past their dead for years. Danger here gives warning (a lamp-man who stops
walking, a boat moored dark, a chalk mark on a door), and then it is real. Describe
violence plainly and without relish; let it kill.

## Crowbill, at scale

He runs the low water. Not a dozen porters: **around a hundred and twenty people on
his payroll in some way**, among them two dozen blades, a night crew with its own
boats, forty watchers from lamp-men to children, a bought sergeant at the Lullwater
post, a Tide Lord who waves his cargo through, and one or two people whose only job
is to end other people. Income runs to about **two hundred gold a week** before
anything special, with strongboxes in more than one safe house. A guardsman's purse
is a joke to him. Size every operation against these numbers.

**The code is a reputation, not a restraint.** *No murder of locals, no children, no
sacred sites* is what the city believes about him, and that belief is one of his
most valuable assets. He protects it the way he protects a strongbox: by making sure
anyone who could prove otherwise is never heard. He is courteous, patient and
genuinely loyal to some people, and none of that stops him ordering a death as a
business decision.

**He is gettable through exposure.** If the low water ever believes he has broken
his own code, it turns on him, and the Dredgers alone can put three hundred people on
a stair. That, not a fair fight, is his real weak point.

## Preparing antagonists here

- **Fix the scale before play** from what the setting says the person commands, and
  hold to it. Finite means is a rule against inventing assets mid-scene, not a reason
  to make them small.
- **An early win takes a piece, not the whole.** Breaking one crew costs Crowbill a
  crew. If a single session ends him, the scale was wrong.
- **Lethal, with warning.** Every operation that can kill a PC or someone they love
  has a visible warning and a way to meet it. Then it is allowed to happen.
- **Bodies are evidence.** A death in Purewater is the start of an investigation,
  and the city's habit of calling everything *drowned* is the first lie to break.

## What a good night here looks like

Confirmed in play on 2026-11-21 by the player, about a session that ran this
guide as written: "high stakes, interesting politics, great action, tension
everywhere and a suitably evil villain." These are the things that session did.

- **Something is already wrong in the first line.** A chalk crow on the outer
  sill of a window you sat beside all night. A senior sister holding her hand
  out for a junior's notebook, with the stove door open. A boat whistling for
  a watch that won't answer. A man coming down his own water stair with a
  satchel, leaving. Never open on a wait.
- **The politics are levers in the player's hands, not exposition.** Here they
  are the forms in *The King's justice*: testimony sworn over still water before
  the Prince, an outlawry and a sealed warrant, a storyteller with a voice the
  low water trusts crying it from his own chair, the Order's claim on profaned
  water, and the Swords, whom the Order cannot command. Each costs somebody
  something to use. Give the player the person with standing and let them make
  the case.
- **Every NPC wants something and has an edge.** The clerk is afraid of being
  the one who's blamed. The Warden signed the stand-down. The bellows-boy wants
  out. The blade captain drinks his clerk's health at midwinter and will still
  have him shot. Pressure on the want moves them; nothing else does.
- **Fights are lethal, shown first, and short.** Before any roll that can kill,
  name the hazard: a fireball forming, a bound man in a boat under a closing
  arch, a dwarf in mail beside deep water. Let the player choose. Then roll
  honestly and keep what the dice give, even two 96s in a row on the lift that
  matters. Crowbill's people break when the fight turns, and a broken crew is
  capacity he has really lost.
- **The villain is polite, curious and without pity.** The fire-binder greets
  the dwarf with "Oh, good." He calls them vessels, and the trade sells
  ferry-boys by the head. Never explain him. Let the ledger speak for him.

Conduct slips that were caught at the table, and are worth not repeating:
quote hit points only after checking the sheet; never pick the skill for the
player (ask, or use the one they've been using); and remember that ranged
attacks can't take melee-only special effects (Trip, Bash, Disarm, Grip).
