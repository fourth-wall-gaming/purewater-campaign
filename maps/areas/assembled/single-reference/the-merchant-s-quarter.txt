THE FIRST REFERENCE IMAGE IS THE AUTHORITY. It is the master map of the city of
Purewater, drawn in black pen-and-ink on aged parchment. The sheet you are
drawing now is an ENLARGEMENT OF ONE PART OF THAT MAP — the same city, the same
survey, the same hand, drawn closer so that what was a few strokes on the master
becomes a street of individual houses here. It must look as though it were cut
from the same atlas by the same cartographer in the same week.

Take from the reference, and reproduce exactly:

- The coastlines, canal courses, island shapes and bridge positions of this part
  of the city. Do not redesign the geography. Where the reference shows a bank,
  a channel or a quay, it goes in the same place and the same shape here, only
  larger and with more of its detail resolved.
- The palette: aged off-white parchment with mottled staining, and COOL BLACK
  AND GREY ink. Grey wash in the water. No colour, and in particular NO sepia,
  NO brown ink, NO warm tan — the master is black on bone, not brown on tan.
- The hand: fine, confident, technical pen-work. Buildings as outlines, water
  with stippled and contour-hatched shading, marsh as reed tufts.
- The lettering: hand-set small-capital serif place names, letter-spaced, black.
- The furniture: an ornate compass rose with north at the top, a scale bar, a
  decorative title cartouche, a thin ruled decorative border, and — only where
  this sheet actually contains such things — a small legend box using the
  master's symbols: bridge, one-storey, two-or-more storeys, temple, canal.

THE CARTOUCHE CARRIES THIS SHEET'S OWN TITLE. Copy the master's cartouche
shape, border and lettering style, but NOT its wording. The master is titled
"THE CITY OF PUREWATER"; this sheet is not the city, it is one part of it, and
its cartouche must read the title given in the brief below. Do not write "THE
CITY OF PUREWATER" anywhere on this sheet.

THREE RULES, each of which a generator will break unless told not to. Rule one
is the one most often broken.

1. ORGANIC, IRREGULAR, ASYMMETRICAL SHAPES. This is a silted lagoon and river
   delta. Every coastline, island and canal bank is ragged, lumpy and
   unpredictable, with inlets, spits, notches and shallows. IF AN ISLAND'S
   OUTLINE COULD BE DRAWN WITH ONE SMOOTH CURVE, IT IS WRONG. No ovals, no
   lozenges, no round blobs, no concentric rings, no symmetry, no smooth
   lens-shaped islands.

   The same applies on land. Street blocks are irregular quadrilaterals of
   differing size, not a grid. Streets bend, narrow, fork and dead-end. This is
   NOT a planned Roman town and NOT a modern grid.

2. STRICT TOP-DOWN ORTHOGRAPHIC PLAN VIEW. The camera is straight overhead at
   ninety degrees, like an architect's floor plan or a cadastral survey. Drawn
   FLAT. NOT a bird's-eye view, NOT oblique, NOT isometric, NOT axonometric,
   NOT perspective. NO little three-dimensional house icons, NO pitched roofs
   seen from the side, NO building elevations, NO skyline or horizon anywhere,
   NO cast shadows, NO drop-shadows, NO bevelled or embossed walls, NO ambient
   occlusion, NO hill-shading, NO vignette, NO glow, NO atmospheric haze, and
   none of the "epic fantasy atlas" styling. Distance is shown by drawing LESS
   detail, never by drawing a horizon. Flat, even, shadowless, archival.

3. RESOLVED HOUSE BY HOUSE. Every building is a separate small rectangular
   footprint seen from directly overhead, packed tight along the streets so the
   district reads building by building. One-storey is a plain outline;
   two-or-more is a double-lined box. NOT impressionistic massing, NOT blurry
   blocks, NOT low detail.

WATER GOES THROUGH, NOT JUST AROUND. In Purewater the canals are the streets.
Thread narrow working canals INTO the interior of every district — branching,
crooked, of varying width, ending in private water-stairs and blind basins —
rather than drawing a dry block of streets with one big canal at its edge.
Buildings meet the water directly, with steps, mooring posts and loading doors
onto it. Show clearly which ways a person can walk and which only a boat can
pass.

Because this sheet is an enlargement it carries detail the master could not
hold: individual water-stairs and landing steps, mooring posts, jetties,
footbridges, courtyards and light-wells, wells and cisterns, boats as simple
flat ovals at their moorings, and narrow alleys between blocks.

Monochrome: black, grey, parchment. Crisp, technical, archival pen-and-ink
cartography.

AREA BRIEF -- The Merchant's Quarter

THE TITLE CARTOUCHE ON THIS SHEET READS: The Merchant's Quarter

The south-west corner of High Isle, where the landward channel and the road
from Caravan Square bring the city's trade ashore.

The great MARKET SQUARE as an open paved plaza with regular rows of stall
footprints marked on it -- the one deliberately regular thing on the sheet,
because it is laid out by the city. Around it the guildhalls as large formal
double-lined blocks with courtyards: Merchants, Shipwrights, Dyers, and the
Watercrafters' Guild.

Counting-houses and warehouses crowd the water, their loading doors opening
directly onto the canals, hoist-beams and cranes at the upper floors, stone
wharves with bollards and goods-yards behind. The palazzi of the merchant
princes as courtyard houses with private water-stairs.

Dyers' drying-racks and tenter frames in the open yards near their hall.

Barge-canals wide enough for a loaded hull thread in from the Grand Canal and
branch into narrower channels between the warehouse blocks, with a dense
tangle of crooked alleys on the land between them.
